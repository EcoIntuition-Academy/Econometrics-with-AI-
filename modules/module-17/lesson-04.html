LESSON 17.4 GMM Foundations & Moments

GMM Foundations: Moments, Plims & OLS

Establish the foundations of Generalized Method of Moments (GMM): define sample and population moments, apply probability limits from probability limit theorems, and derive the Ordinary Least Squares estimator as a special case of GMM.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define moments as sample functions with known probability limits (plim).
  • State and interpret probability limit theorems regarding convergence of sample averages to population expectations.
  • Derive consistent estimators of population mean and variance from first and second sample moments.
  • Formulate the orthogonality assumption \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_i \varepsilon_i] = \mathbf{0}\) as a population moment condition.
  • Prove mathematically that OLS is a special case of GMM where regressors serve as their own instruments.

1. Moments and Probability Limits

How can unknown parameters be estimated when economic theory tells us that certain population averages must equal known values? This is the core question answered by the Generalized Method of Moments (GMM).

According to the source, a moment is a function of the data with a known probability limit (plim). The central idea of GMM is to solve for parameters by setting the sample moment equal to its theoretical probability limit:

SAMPLE MOMENT CONVERGENCE

Sample Moments and Probability Limits

\[ m_k = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n x_{it}^k, \qquad \text{plim} \left[ \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n g(x_i) \right] = \mathbb{E}[g(x)] = \mu_{g(x)} \]
WHERE:
\(m_k\) The \(k\)-th sample moment of the data series.
\(\text{plim}\) Probability limit as sample size \(n \to \infty\).
\(\mathbb{E}[g(x)]\) Population expectation (theoretical moment) of the function \(g(x)\).

💡 Core Pedagogical Intuition

These theorems imply that if the sample moment converges to the population moment, then the solution to the sample moment condition provides a consistent estimator for the parameter. GMM replaces unobservable population expectations with observable sample averages.

2. GMM Examples: Mean, Variance, and Classical Regression

Example 1: Estimating Mean and Variance of an IID Random Variable

Consider an independent and identically distributed (IID) sample \(y_1, \dots, y_n\). We formulate two sample moment conditions based on powers of \(y_i\):

First Moment: Population Mean

\[ \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n y_i \xrightarrow{p} \mu_y \]

Sample average converges to the population mean \(\mu_y\).

Second Moment: Variance + Squared Mean

\[ \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n y_i^2 \xrightarrow{p} \text{Var}(y_i) + \mu_y^2 = \sigma_y^2 + \mu_y^2 \]

Sample second moment allows recovery of population variance: \(\hat{\sigma}_y^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum y_i^2 - \bar{y}^2\).

Example 2: The Classical Linear Regression Model as GMM

Consider the classical regression model: \(y_i = \mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta} + \varepsilon_i\). Under Classical Assumption 3, the regressors are orthogonal to the error term:

DERIVATION · OLS AS GMM

Deriving OLS from the GMM Orthogonality Moment Condition

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_i \varepsilon_i] = \mathbf{0} \implies \mathbb{E}\left[ \mathbf{x}_i (y_i - \mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}) \right] = \mathbf{0} \] \[ \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{x}_i (y_i - \mathbf{x}_i'\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) = \hat{\mathbf{m}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) = \mathbf{0} \] \[ \frac{1}{n} \mathbf{X}' (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) = \mathbf{0} \implies \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{GMM} = (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y} = \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{OLS} \]
WHERE:
\(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_i \varepsilon_i] = \mathbf{0}\) Population moment condition asserting exogeneity between regressors and disturbances.
\(\hat{\mathbf{m}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) = \mathbf{0}\) Sample analog moment conditions set exactly to zero.
\(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{GMM} = \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{OLS}\) The resulting GMM estimator, which is identical to the classical Ordinary Least Squares formula.

💡 Key Unifying Insight: OLS is a Special Case of GMM

Even though derived via moment conditions, the GMM estimator under classical assumptions collapses to the OLS estimator.
This demonstrates how GMM provides a universal foundation that generalizes classical methods. The moment condition essentially imposes orthogonality between instruments and residuals, and in classical regression, the instruments are simply the regressors \(\mathbf{x}_i\) themselves!

The Universal GMM Workflow

1. Economic / Statistical Assumption (e.g., Exogeneity, Rational Expectations)
↓
2. Population Moment Condition: \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{g}_i(\boldsymbol{\beta})] = \mathbf{0}\)
↓
3. Sample Analog Function: \(\hat{\mathbf{g}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \frac{1}{n}\sum \mathbf{g}_i(\boldsymbol{\beta})\)
↓
4. Choose \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) to set sample moments as close to zero as possible → Estimator!

👁 WHAT YOU ARE SEEING

The blue trajectory traces the cumulative sample mean \(\bar{y}_k = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^k y_i\) as sample size grows from 1 to \(n\). The horizontal green line marks the true theoretical expectation \(\mu = 2.0\).

🔎 WHAT TO NOTICE

For small \(k < 25\), the trajectory wanders with high volatility. As \(k\) exceeds 100, the law of large numbers dominates, compressing deviations into a tight band around the true population mean.

◆ WHY IT MATTERS

This convergence is the cornerstone of GMM. Because sample averages consistently estimate population expectations, setting sample moments to zero guarantees that parameter estimates converge to their true population values.

📊 ECONOMETRIC INTERPRETATION

By probability limit theorems, \(\text{plim} \frac{1}{n}\sum g(x_i) = \mathbb{E}[g(x)]\). In classical regression, setting \(\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{X}'(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) = \mathbf{0}\) reproduces OLS precisely.

4. Quick Knowledge Check

CONCEPT CHECK GMM Foundations

Why can the classical Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) estimator be interpreted as a special case of GMM?

Hint: What is the core exogeneity assumption \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_i \varepsilon_i] = \mathbf{0}\)? What sample equation does OLS solve?

Solution: The classical exogeneity assumption \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_i \varepsilon_i] = \mathbf{0}\) specifies that regressors are uncorrelated with errors. Replacing this population expectation with its sample analog yields \(\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{X}'(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) = \mathbf{0}\). Solving this sample moment condition yields \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y}\), which is identical to the OLS estimator.

Lesson 17.4 Summary

The Generalized Method of Moments provides a unified foundation for econometric estimation:

  • Moment Definition: Functions of data with known probability limits. The Law of Large Numbers guarantees \(\text{plim} \frac{1}{n}\sum g(x_i) = \mathbb{E}[g(x)]\).
  • Moment Matching: Setting sample analogs to theoretical expectations allows consistent recovery of population parameters.
  • OLS Synthesis: Setting the sample regressor-error orthogonality condition \(\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{X}'(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) = \mathbf{0}\) directly produces OLS.
  • Looking Ahead: In the next lesson, we extend moment conditions beyond regressors to external instrumental variables, exploring just-identified and overidentified systems.
