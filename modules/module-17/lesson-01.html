LESSON 17.1 Likelihood Inference

Likelihood Ratio Tests

Learn how econometric hypothesis testing imposes restrictions directly on the likelihood function, compare unrestricted and restricted maxima, define the likelihood ratio statistic, and establish its asymptotic chi-squared distribution.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Understand how a hypothesis test acts as a parametric restriction on the parameter space of the likelihood function.
  • Distinguish between unrestricted estimates \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_U\) and restricted estimates \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_R\).
  • Formulate the likelihood ratio \(\lambda_{LR} = L_R / L_U\) and its log-transformation \(-2 \ln \lambda_{LR}\).
  • Establish the asymptotic \(\chi^2_q\) distribution under the null hypothesis.
  • Master the foundational intuition: if a restriction is valid, imposing it should not reduce the likelihood substantially.

1. Restrictions on the Likelihood Function

In maximum likelihood estimation, a hypothesis test imposes a restriction on the likelihood function. When no restrictions are imposed, we choose all parameters freely to achieve the highest possible probability density of the observed sample.

Consider the source example where our null hypothesis is that the first parameter equals a specific constant:

HYPOTHESIS SPECIFICATION

Null Hypothesis as a Parameter Restriction

\[ H_0 : \theta_1 = 1 \]
WHERE:
\(\theta_1\) The parameter of interest restricted under the null hypothesis.
\(1\) The hypothesized constant value imposed on \(\theta_1\).
\(q = 1\) Number of parameter restrictions being tested.

When estimating under the restriction, we no longer choose \(\hat{\theta}_1\) to maximize the likelihood freely. Instead, we hold \(\theta_1 = 1\) fixed and maximize the likelihood over the remaining parameters.

Unrestricted Model

Estimate all parameters freely by maximizing the likelihood without constraints:

\(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_U = \arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} L(\boldsymbol{\theta})\)

Attains the global maximum likelihood value: \(L_U = L(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_U)\).

Restricted Model

Hold \(\theta_1 = 1\) fixed and maximize over the remaining parameters:

\(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_R = \arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta} \in \Theta_0} L(\boldsymbol{\theta})\)

Attains the constrained maximum likelihood value: \(L_R = L(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_R)\).

💡 Core Pedagogical Intuition

"If the restriction is valid, imposing it should not reduce the likelihood very much."
Because the unrestricted estimator searches over the entire parameter space, \(L_U \ge L_R\) always holds. If the hypothesized restriction is true in the population, the unconstrained maximum should naturally occur close to the restricted subspace, causing only a minor loss in likelihood. Conversely, a large drop in likelihood suggests that the restriction is inconsistent with the data.

2. The Likelihood Ratio Test Statistic

To formalize this intuition, we construct the likelihood ratio \(\lambda_{LR}\) by comparing the constrained and unconstrained likelihood values:

TEST STATISTIC · LIKELIHOOD RATIO

Likelihood Ratio and Asymptotic Chi-Squared Statistic

\[ \lambda_{LR} = \frac{L_R}{L_U}, \quad \text{or equivalently:} \quad -2 \ln \lambda_{LR} = 2 \left[ \ln L_U - \ln L_R \right] \] \[ -2 \ln \lambda_{LR} \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2_q \quad \text{under } H_0 \]
WHERE:
\(\lambda_{LR}\) The likelihood ratio, strictly bounded in the interval \([0, 1]\).
\(L_R\) Likelihood function evaluated at the restricted parameter estimates \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_R\).
\(L_U\) Likelihood function evaluated at the unrestricted parameter estimates \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_U\).
\(q\) Number of independent restrictions imposed by the null hypothesis \(H_0\).
\(\chi^2_q\) Chi-squared distribution with \(q\) degrees of freedom.
⚠️

Common Student Mistake: Ratio Direction

MISCONCEPTION: "A small likelihood ratio \(\lambda_{LR}\) value supports the restriction because small values mean the restriction changed very little."

CORRECTION: As the authoritative source emphasizes, "If the ratio is too small, the restricted likelihood is too low compared to the unrestricted one, so we reject \(H_0\)." Because \(\lambda_{LR} = L_R / L_U \le 1\), when \(L_R \ll L_U\), the ratio \(\lambda_{LR} \to 0\), causing \(\ln \lambda_{LR} \to -\infty\) and \(-2 \ln \lambda_{LR} \to +\infty\). A small ratio is decisive evidence against the restriction!

SCENARIO INSIGHT

At \(\theta_0 = 1.00\), the restriction perfectly coincides with the unconstrained maximum. There is zero loss in likelihood (\(\lambda_{LR} = 1.000\)), yielding a test statistic of 0.00 and failing to reject \(H_0\).

👁 WHAT YOU ARE SEEING

The blue parabolic curve depicts the log-likelihood function \(\ln L(\theta)\). The green dot marks the unrestricted maximum \(\hat{\theta}_U = 1.00\) with height \(\ln L_U\), while the red marker indicates the restricted candidate \(\theta_0\) with height \(\ln L_R\).

🔎 WHAT TO NOTICE

Because \(\hat{\theta}_U\) maximizes the likelihood globally, \(\ln L_R \le \ln L_U\) in every case. As you drag \(\theta_0\) farther away, the vertical drop \(\ln L_U - \ln L_R\) expands quadratically, causing the test statistic to surge.

◆ WHY IT MATTERS

The Likelihood Ratio test directly gauges empirical fit penalty. Imposing a false restriction forces the model into low-probability regions of the sample space, producing a massive drop in likelihood that cannot be explained by sampling noise alone.

📊 ECONOMETRIC INTERPRETATION

Under standard regularity conditions, \(-2 \ln \lambda_{LR} \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2_1\). Once the statistic crosses 3.84, the p-value falls below 0.05, leading to rejection of the null hypothesis at the 5% significance level.

4. Quick Knowledge Check

CONCEPT CHECK Likelihood Comparison

Why does a very small likelihood ratio \(\lambda_{LR}\) indicate strong evidence against the null hypothesis \(H_0\)?

Hint: Recall the definition \(\lambda_{LR} = L_R / L_U\). What does it mean for this ratio to approach zero? How does the restricted model's ability to explain the data compare to the unrestricted model?

Solution: Because \(\lambda_{LR} = L_R / L_U\), a small ratio implies that \(L_R\) is minuscule relative to \(L_U\). Imposing the restriction dramatically reduces the probability of observing the actual sample data. Under \(H_0\), the reduction should be small sampling variation; a massive drop causes \(-2 \ln \lambda_{LR}\) to become very large, rejecting \(H_0\).

Lesson 17.1 Summary

The Likelihood Ratio test constitutes the first of the classical testing trinity:

  • Restriction Formulation: Hypotheses constrain the parameter space (e.g., \(\theta_1 = 1\)), yielding restricted estimates \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_R\) and unrestricted estimates \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_U\).
  • Ratio Definition: \(\lambda_{LR} = L_R / L_U\), taking values between 0 and 1 because \(L_R \le L_U\) always holds.
  • Asymptotic Distribution: Under regularity conditions, \(-2 \ln \lambda_{LR} = 2[\ln L_U - \ln L_R] \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2_q\), where \(q\) is the number of restrictions.
  • Core Intuition: Valid restrictions do not substantially depress the likelihood; significant likelihood drops lead to rejection.
