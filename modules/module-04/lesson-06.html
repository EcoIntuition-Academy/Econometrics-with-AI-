LESSON 4.6 Asymptotic Methods Nonlinear Transformations

The Delta Method

In empirical research, we frequently care not about an original regression parameter \(\theta\), but about a nonlinear function of it: \(g(\theta)\)—such as an elasticity \(\exp(\theta)\), a quadratic turning point, or a ratio of coefficients. How do we conduct statistical inference on nonlinear functions? Enter the Delta Method.

OBJECTIVES

Learning Objectives

  • Motivate why nonlinear functions of estimators arise in applied econometrics (\(\theta^2\), \(\exp(\theta)\), ratios).
  • Understand tangent-line local linearization using first-order Taylor series approximations.
  • State and interpret the formal Delta Method theorem under standard regularity conditions.
  • Analyze how the first derivative \(g'(\theta)\) scales and amplifies parameter variance by \([g'(\theta)]^2\).
  • Visualize the relationship between curvature, tangent slopes, and uncertainty expansion.

1. Motivation: Why Nonlinear Functions Matter

In linear regression, we obtain an estimate \(\hat{\theta} = \hat{\beta}_2\) with estimated variance \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\beta}_2)\). But theoretical economic hypotheses often involve nonlinear functions \(g(\theta)\):

Example 1: Quadratic Functions
\(g(\beta_2) = \beta_2^2\)

Used in cost function curvature, production returns, or variance parameterizations.

Example 2: Exponentials
\(g(\beta_2) = \exp(\beta_2)\)

Translates semi-elasticities from log-level wage equations into percentage wage impacts.

Example 3: Coefficient Ratios
\(g(\beta) = -\beta_1 / (2\beta_2)\)

Calculates the peak of a quadratic lifecycle wage or age-earnings curve.

The Fundamental Challenge: Even though \(\hat{\theta}\) is approximately normally distributed, a nonlinear transformation \(g(\hat{\theta})\) is generally not normally distributed in finite samples, and its variance cannot be computed directly using simple linear algebra rules!

2. Tangent-Line Local Linearization: The Taylor Approximation

How does the Delta Method solve this? It replaces the nonlinear curve with its tangent line evaluated at the true parameter \(\theta\).

By Taylor's Theorem, any smooth, differentiable function \(g(\cdot)\) can be expanded around the true value \(\theta\) as:

Key Equation: First-Order Taylor Approximation
\[g(\hat{\theta}) \approx g(\theta) + g'(\theta)(\hat{\theta} - \theta)\]
We linearize the nonlinear function \(g(\hat{\theta})\) locally around the true population parameter \(\theta\).

What Each Component Represents

  • \(g(\hat{\theta})\): The transformed sample estimate whose distribution and standard error we wish to compute.
  • \(g(\theta)\): The true, non-random population value of the transformed parameter.
  • \(g'(\theta) = \frac{dg}{d\theta}\Big|_{\theta}\): The first derivative (slope of the tangent line) evaluated at the truth.
  • \((\hat{\theta} - \theta)\): The estimation error in the original parameter, which we already know is asymptotically normal!

3. The Delta Method Theorem

Under standard regularity conditions, the Delta Method is formally stated as follows:

Definition: The Delta Method
\[\sqrt{n}\left(g(\hat{\theta}_n) - g(\theta)\right) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}\left(0, \; [g'(\theta)]^2 \sigma^2\right)\]
Consequently, in terms of the estimated asymptotic distribution: \[g(\hat{\theta}_n) \xrightarrow{a} \mathcal{N}\left(g(\theta), \; \frac{1}{n}[g'(\theta)]^2 \sigma^2\right)\]

How the Derivative Scales Uncertainty

Notice that the asymptotic variance of \(g(\hat{\theta}_n)\) is simply the original variance multiplied by the squared derivative: \([g'(\theta)]^2\).

Steep Slope: |g'(θ)| > 1

Uncertainty is amplified. Small estimation errors in \(\hat{\theta}\) are projected along a steep tangent line, creating huge dispersion in \(g(\hat{\theta})\).

Gentle Slope: |g'(θ)| < 1

Uncertainty is compressed. The flat slope dampens variation, producing a tighter distribution for \(g(\hat{\theta})\).

The nonlinear function \(g(\theta) = \theta^2\) (blue curve) with its red dashed tangent line at operating point \(\theta\). The black dot represents the parameter location \((\theta, g(\theta))\).
WHAT TO NOTICE
At \(\theta = 1.0\), the slope is gentle (\(g' = 2.0\), variance scale \(4\times\)). But as you move the slider to \(\theta = 3.0\), the slope steepens to \(6.0\), causing the variance scale factor \([g'(\theta)]^2\) to surge to \(36\times\)!
WHY IT MATTERS
Nonlinear functions do not scale uncertainty uniformly across the parameter space. Where the curve is steeper, equal estimation uncertainty in \(\hat{\theta}\) creates massively larger uncertainty in \(g(\hat{\theta})\).
ECONOMETRIC INTERPRETATION
In exponential policy models (\(\exp(\beta)\)), large estimated coefficients \(\hat{\beta}\) lead to explosive confidence intervals because the derivative \(\exp(\beta)\) grows exponentially.
EXPERIMENT Drag the slider from \(\theta = 1.0\) to \(\theta = 2.5\). Watch the slope double from \(2.0\) to \(5.0\), causing the variance multiplier to jump by over 6 times from \(4.0\) to \(25.0\)!
QUICK CHECK Derivative Scaling

Suppose an estimator has \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\theta}) = 0.01\). We are interested in the transformation \(g(\theta) = 3\theta + 5\). What is \(g'(\theta)\), and what is the exact asymptotic variance \(\operatorname{Var}(g(\hat{\theta}))\)?

Show Answer & Explanation
ANSWER: \(g'(\theta) = 3\), and \(\operatorname{Var}(g(\hat{\theta})) = 0.09\).

WHY?
  1. The derivative of \(g(\theta) = 3\theta + 5\) with respect to \(\theta\) is simply the constant \(g'(\theta) = 3\).
  2. By the Delta Method formula: \(\operatorname{Var}(g(\hat{\theta})) \approx [g'(\theta)]^2 \operatorname{Var}(\hat{\theta}) = 3^2 \times 0.01 = 9 \times 0.01 = 0.09\). Because this transformation is linear, the Taylor approximation is exact!
SUMMARY

What You Should Now Understand

  • The Delta Method provides the asymptotic distribution for smooth nonlinear transformations \(g(\hat{\theta})\) of CAN estimators.
  • It operates through a first-order Taylor series tangent-line approximation: \(g(\hat{\theta}) \approx g(\theta) + g'(\theta)(\hat{\theta} - \theta)\).
  • The asymptotic distribution is \(g(\hat{\theta}_n) \xrightarrow{a} \mathcal{N}\left(g(\theta), \frac{1}{n}[g'(\theta)]^2\sigma^2\right)\).
  • The first derivative \(g'(\theta)\) acts as a local scaling factor: the variance of the transformation is multiplied by \([g'(\theta)]^2\).
  • Nonlinear transformations expand variance wherever the curve is steep, resulting in substantially wider confidence intervals.
