Convergence in Probability & Consistency

Suppose we repeatedly estimate an unknown economic quantity using larger and larger samples. For every finite \(n\), the estimate is a random variable. But as \(n \to \infty\), does the distribution collapse tightly onto the true constant?

OBJECTIVES Learning Objectives

  • Develop intuition for how sequences of estimators behave as sample size \(n\) approaches infinity.
  • State and unpack the formal mathematical definition of convergence in probability (\(\operatorname{plim}\)).
  • Distinguish the positive tolerance parameter \(\varepsilon\) from the regression error disturbance \(\varepsilon_i\).
  • Define an estimator's consistency (\(\hat{\theta}_n \xrightarrow{p} \theta\)) as an asymptotic probability property.
  • Differentiate consistency from finite-sample unbiasedness and avoid common misconceptions.

1. Intuition: What Happens When Sample Size Grows?

In finite-sample econometrics (Module 3), we analyzed the exact sampling distribution of an estimator for a fixed sample size \(n\). We asked: Is the estimator unbiased on average across all possible samples of size \(n\)? What is its exact variance?

In many real-world economic settings, however, exact finite-sample properties cannot be established. For example:

  • The error terms \(\varepsilon_i\) may follow a skewed, heavy-tailed, or discrete distribution rather than an exact Gaussian bell curve.
  • The estimator might involve nonlinear combinations, ratios, or matrix inverses where expectations \(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}]\) cannot be computed analytically.

This motivates large-sample (asymptotic) theory. Rather than fixing \(n\), we imagine a thought experiment where the econometrician collects progressively larger datasets: \(n = 25, 50, 100, 500, 1000, \dots, \infty\). At each sample size, we compute an estimate \(x_n\). We obtain a sequence of random variables:

\[\{ x_{25}, x_{50}, x_{100}, x_{250}, x_{500}, \dots, x_n \}\]

Each element in this sequence is random. Yet, under well-behaved conditions, the random variation compresses toward zero, and the probability mass concentrates entirely around a single non-random constant \(c\).

2. Formal Definition: Convergence in Probability

The mathematical definition of convergence in probability is formally stated as follows:

Definition: Convergence in Probability (Probability Limit / plim)
\[\lim_{n \to \infty} \Pr(|x_n - c| > \varepsilon) = 0 \quad \text{for every positive } \varepsilon > 0\]
Equivalently, as sample size goes to infinity: \(\lim_{n \to \infty} \Pr(|x_n - c| \le \varepsilon) = 1\).

If this condition holds, we write:

\(x_n \xrightarrow{p} c\)  or  \(\operatorname{plim}_{n \to \infty} x_n = c\)

What Each Symbol Means in the Definition

  • \(x_n\): The random estimate calculated from a sample of size \(n\).
  • \(c\): A fixed, non-random target parameter (the true value).
  • \(|x_n - c|\): The absolute deviation (distance) between the sample estimate and the true parameter.
  • \(\varepsilon\): An arbitrarily small positive tolerance radius (e.g., \(0.01, 0.001\)).
  • \(\Pr(|x_n - c| > \varepsilon)\): The probability that our estimate strays outside the tolerance envelope \([c - \varepsilon, c + \varepsilon]\).
  • \(\lim_{n \to \infty} = 0\): As the sample grows infinitely large, the likelihood of straying outside that microscopic window shrinks to zero.
NOTE Critical Notation: The Dual Meaning of Epsilon (ε)

Notice the Greek letter \(\varepsilon\) in \(\Pr(|x_n - c| > \varepsilon)\). Here, \(\varepsilon\) represents an arbitrarily small positive tolerance distance (just as in classical calculus \(\varepsilon\)-\(\delta\) limit definitions).

It is NOT the unobserved regression error disturbance \(\varepsilon_i\) from the model \(y_i = x_i'\beta + \varepsilon_i\). Econometricians use \(\varepsilon\) for both concepts by convention, but their mathematical roles are completely distinct!

4. Definition: Consistency of an Estimator

With convergence in probability established, we define the foundational large-sample property of an estimator:

Definition: Consistency of an Estimator
\[\hat{\theta}_n \xrightarrow{p} \theta \quad \iff \quad \operatorname{plim}_{n \to \infty} \hat{\theta}_n = \theta\]
An estimator \(\hat{\theta}_n\) is consistent for \(\theta\) if and only if its probability limit equals the true parameter \(\theta\).

In plain language: As sample size grows infinitely large, the estimator becomes arbitrarily close to the true parameter with probability approaching one.

CAUTION Common Mistake

"A consistent estimator is guaranteed to give the exact correct answer in my sample."
Correction: Consistency is an asymptotic property that describes sequence behavior as \(n \to \infty\). In any actual finite sample (\(n = 150\) or even \(n = 5000\)), the estimate \(\hat{\theta}\) is a single random draw that almost certainly differs from \(\theta\). Consistency tells us that larger samples reduce uncertainty, not that a given finite estimate is error-free.

Unbiasedness vs Consistency: A Critical Comparison

Unbiasedness (Finite Sample)

\(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}_n] = \theta\) for any fixed \(n\). The sampling distribution is centered on the true value across repeated samples of size \(n\), but its spread may remain wide.

Consistency (Large Sample)

\(\operatorname{plim}_{n \to \infty} \hat{\theta}_n = \theta\). As \(n \to \infty\), the variance collapses to zero and the entire probability distribution contracts to a single point at \(\theta\).

QUICK CHECK Convergence & Consistency

Suppose an estimator has a finite-sample bias of \(\frac{1}{n}\), so \(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}_n] = \theta + \frac{1}{n}\), and its variance is \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\theta}_n) = \frac{2}{n}\). Is \(\hat{\theta}_n\) unbiased? Is \(\hat{\theta}_n\) consistent?

Show Answer & Explanation
ANSWER: It is biased in finite samples, but consistent as \(n \to \infty\).

WHY?
  1. For any finite \(n\) (say \(n = 50\)), \(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}_{50}] = \theta + 0.02 \neq \theta\), so it is biased.
  2. As \(n \to \infty\), the bias \(\frac{1}{n} \to 0\) and the variance \(\frac{2}{n} \to 0\). Because both the mean squared error and variance vanish, the estimator converges in probability to \(\theta\): \(\operatorname{plim}_{n \to \infty} \hat{\theta}_n = \theta\). Thus, it is consistent!
SUMMARY

What You Should Now Understand

  • A sequence of estimators \(x_n\) converges in probability to a constant \(c\) if for any arbitrarily small positive tolerance \(\varepsilon > 0\), \(\lim_{n \to \infty} \Pr(|x_n - c| > \varepsilon) = 0\).
  • The \(\varepsilon\) in the convergence definition is a positive distance threshold, not the regression disturbance \(\varepsilon_i\).
  • An estimator is consistent (\(\hat{\theta}_n \xrightarrow{p} \theta\)) if its probability limit equals the true population parameter.
  • Consistency is an asymptotic property: it guarantees that estimation error contracts to zero as \(n \to \infty\), but does not mean any specific finite-sample estimate is exact.
  • An estimator can be biased in finite samples yet still be fully consistent if its bias and variance shrink to zero as \(n \to \infty\).
