Asymptotic Normality of OLS

Consistency tells us that the OLS estimator collapses to the true parameter \(\beta\). But when we zoom in on the remaining estimation error, what does its distribution look like? Discover the asymptotic normality of OLS, master the estimated asymptotic variance matrix, and contrast variance with standard error.

OBJECTIVES Learning Objectives

  • Clearly distinguish consistency ("Where does \(\hat{\beta}\) go?") from asymptotic normality ("What shape does its distribution take?").
  • State the asymptotic normality theorem for OLS: \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} \xrightarrow{a} \mathcal{N}\left(\beta, \frac{\sigma^2}{n}Q^{-1}\right)\).
  • Correctly define and interpret the estimated asymptotic variance-covariance matrix \(\widehat{\operatorname{AsyVar}}(\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}}) = s^2(X'X)^{-1}\).
  • Never confuse variance (squared units) with standard error (linear parameter units).
  • Understand why normality of the disturbance \(\varepsilon_i\) is NOT required for large-sample OLS inference.

1. The Fundamental Distinction: Consistency vs. Asymptotic Normality

Learners often confuse consistency with asymptotic normality. They answer two fundamentally different questions about an estimator's large-sample behavior:

Property 1

CONSISTENCY: Where Does β̂ Go?

\(\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} \xrightarrow{p} \beta\)

Consistency asks about the location of the probability mass. It states that the entire sampling distribution collapses onto a single point—the true parameter vector \(\beta\)—as \(n \to \infty\).

Property 2

ASYMPTOTIC NORMALITY: What Shape Does It Have?

\(\sqrt{n}(\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} - \beta) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \sigma^2 Q^{-1})\)

Asymptotic normality asks about the shape of the distribution when we rescale by \(\sqrt{n}\). It proves that the remaining estimation fluctuations follow a multivariate Gaussian bell curve.

2. Asymptotic Normality of the OLS Estimator

Under classical large-sample theory, if the error term is independently distributed with mean zero and finite variance \(\sigma^2\), and the Grenander regularity conditions are satisfied:

Definition: Asymptotic Normality of \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}}\)
\[\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} \xrightarrow{a} \mathcal{N}\left(\beta, \, \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \left[\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{X'X}{n}\right]^{-1}\right) = \mathcal{N}\left(\beta, \, \frac{\sigma^2}{n} Q^{-1}\right)\]
The symbol \(\xrightarrow{a}\) denotes an asymptotic normal distribution approximation in large samples.

In practice, the true variance \(\sigma^2\) and the limiting moment matrix \(Q\) are unknown. We estimate them using sample data:

  • Estimate theoretical error variance \(\sigma^2\) using the sample residual variance: \(s^2 = \frac{e'e}{n-k}\).
  • Estimate the limiting matrix \(Q = \lim \frac{X'X}{n}\) using its sample analogue: \(\frac{X'X}{n}\).

Substituting these sample estimates cancels the sample size factor \(\frac{1}{n}\):

\(\frac{s^2}{n} \left(\frac{X'X}{n}\right)^{-1} = \frac{s^2}{n} \cdot n (X'X)^{-1} = s^2 (X'X)^{-1}\)
Key Equation: Estimated Asymptotic Variance-Covariance Matrix
\[\widehat{\operatorname{AsyVar}}(\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}}) = s^2 (X'X)^{-1}\]
Strictly label this as the estimated asymptotic variance-covariance matrix. Do NOT call this entire matrix a standard error!

3. Precision Check: Variance vs. Standard Error

A frequent point of confusion in applied econometrics is mixing up variances and standard errors. Always maintain this strict distinction:

VARIANCE (Squared Units)
\(\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\beta}_k) = s^2 S_{kk}\)

The diagonal entry of the estimated covariance matrix \(s^2(X'X)^{-1}\). It is measured in squared units of the coefficient (e.g., dollars squared, percent squared). You cannot add or subtract variance directly to a point estimate!

STANDARD ERROR (Linear Parameter Units)
\(\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\beta}_k) = \sqrt{\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat{\beta}_k)} = \sqrt{s^2 S_{kk}}\)

The square root of the estimated variance. It is measured in the exact same units as \(\hat{\beta}_k\). Confidence intervals and standardized \(z\)-tests always use Standard Errors, never variances!

4. Interactive Lab: The Central Limit Theorem at Work

A foundational econometric principle states: Normality of \(\varepsilon_i\) is NOT required for asymptotic normality of \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}}\).

Below, choose a severely non-normal error distribution (Bimodal, Uniform, or Skewed Chi-Square). Then drag the sample size slider to witness how the standardized estimator \(\sqrt{n}(\hat{\beta} - \beta)\) converges to the standard normal bell curve!

Disturbance Shape (ε_i)
Disturbance Distribution \(\varepsilon_i\) (Left) Standardized Estimator \(\sqrt{n}(\hat{\beta} - \beta)\) (Right) Limit Normal \(\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 Q^{-1})\)
WHAT YOU ARE SEEING
Left panel: The non-normal error distribution \(\varepsilon_i\) (amber). Right panel: The standardized estimator distribution \(\sqrt{n}(\hat{\beta} - \beta)\) (blue) compared against the benchmark standard normal limit (dashed gray curve).
WHAT TO NOTICE
Even when \(\varepsilon_i\) has two stark peaks (bimodal), as you increase \(n\) above 40, the standardized estimator's distribution smoothens into a symmetric single bell curve that aligns with \(\mathcal{N}(0, 1)\).
WHY IT MATTERS
In empirical economics, disturbances are virtually never exactly normal. Asymptotic normality guarantees that standard \(z\)-tests, \(t\)-tests, and confidence intervals remain valid in large samples without assuming normal errors!
ECONOMETRIC INTERPRETATION
Because \(\hat{\beta} - \beta = (X'X)^{-1}\sum x_i \varepsilon_i\), the estimator is a weighted average of independent random shocks. The Central Limit Theorem smooths away the idiosyncratic shapes of individual disturbances.
TRY THIS: Select the Skewed (Chi-Square) error shape with \(n = 5\). Notice the strong right-skew in the estimator. Now drag \(n\) to \(150\) and observe how the skewness dissolves into symmetry!

5. Important Conceptual Nuance: What the CLT Does NOT Claim

Students often summarize the Central Limit Theorem casually by asserting: "If the sample size is large enough, everything becomes normally distributed."

CAUTION Common Econometric Fallacy

"Large samples make the data normal."
Correction: Large samples do NOT alter the underlying distribution of the data or the error terms \(\varepsilon_i\). If earnings are Pareto-distributed or wealth is log-normal, collecting 1,000,000 observations will merely give you an exceptionally precise histogram of a Pareto or log-normal distribution.

What becomes normally distributed is the estimator \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}}\) (a sum of independent terms), NOT the original raw variables or disturbances.

QUICK CHECK Diagonal Entries of the Covariance Matrix

In a regression with \(n = 500\), the estimated covariance matrix is \(\widehat{\operatorname{AsyVar}}(\hat{\beta}) = s^2(X'X)^{-1}\). The second diagonal entry is \(0.04\). What is the estimated standard error \(\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\beta}_2)\)? Can you construct a 95% confidence interval using \(\pm 1.96 \times 0.04\)?

Show Answer & Explanation
ANSWER: \(\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\beta}_2) = \sqrt{0.04} = 0.20\). No, you must NEVER multiply by 0.04!

WHY?
  1. The diagonal element \(0.04\) is the estimated variance \(\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat{\beta}_2)\), in squared units.
  2. The standard error is the square root: \(\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\beta}_2) = \sqrt{0.04} = 0.20\).
  3. A 95% confidence interval requires standard error units: \(\hat{\beta}_2 \pm 1.96 \times 0.20\). Using the variance \(0.04\) directly would result in an invalid, dimensionally inconsistent interval.
SUMMARY

What You Should Now Understand

  • Consistency establishes that \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} \xrightarrow{p} \beta\) (point collapse), whereas asymptotic normality describes the Gaussian shape of the rescaled sampling distribution: \(\sqrt{n}(\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} - \beta) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \sigma^2 Q^{-1})\).
  • The practical estimated asymptotic variance-covariance matrix is \(\widehat{\operatorname{AsyVar}}(\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}}) = s^2(X'X)^{-1}\).
  • Diagonal entries of \(s^2(X'X)^{-1}\) are coefficient variances (squared units). Standard errors are their positive square roots: \(\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\beta}_k) = \sqrt{s^2 S_{kk}}\).
  • Under large-sample conditions, disturbance normality is not required. By the Central Limit Theorem, \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}}\) is approximately normally distributed even with non-normal errors.
  • The CLT claims that estimators (averages/sums) become normal, never that raw economic data or disturbances become normal.
