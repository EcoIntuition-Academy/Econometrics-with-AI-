Asymptotic Efficiency

Multiple estimators can be consistent and asymptotically normal for the same economic parameter. Which one should we prefer? Explore the gold standard of large-sample estimation—CAN estimators—and examine the rigorous comparison between OLS and Maximum Likelihood.

OBJECTIVES Learning Objectives

  • Define CAN (Consistent and Asymptotically Normal) estimators as the benchmark class for large-sample inference.
  • State the formal criterion for asymptotic efficiency: achieving the minimum asymptotic covariance matrix.
  • Visualize how two consistent estimators can differ substantially in their large-sample precision.
  • Analyze the OLS vs. Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) trade-off under normal vs. non-normal error distributions.
  • Avoid oversimplified claims like "MLE is always superior to OLS" by identifying the role of distributional misspecification.

1. The Benchmark Class: CAN Estimators

In finite samples (Module 3), Gauss–Markov established that OLS is BLUE: the Best (minimum variance) Linear Unbiased Estimator. But in large samples, we drop the requirement of strict finite-sample unbiasedness.

Instead, econometricians evaluate estimators within the class of CAN Estimators:

What is a CAN Estimator?

An estimator \(\hat{\theta}_n\) is a CAN Estimator if it satisfies two simultaneous properties:

  1. C (Consistent): \(\hat{\theta}_n \xrightarrow{p} \theta\) as \(n \to \infty\).
  2. AN (Asymptotically Normal): \(\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}_n - \theta) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, V)\) for some finite covariance matrix \(V\).

2. Formal Definition: Asymptotic Efficiency

Asymptotic efficiency is formally defined as follows:

Definition: Asymptotic Efficiency
\[\operatorname{AsyVar}(\hat{\theta}_{\text{eff}}) \le \operatorname{AsyVar}(\tilde{\theta}) \quad \text{for any alternative CAN estimator } \tilde{\theta}\]
Matrix criterion: The difference \(\operatorname{AsyVar}(\tilde{\theta}) - \operatorname{AsyVar}(\hat{\theta}_{\text{eff}})\) is a positive semi-definite matrix.

Three conditions must be met simultaneously for an estimator to be asymptotically efficient:

  • It is consistent: It converges to the true parameter.
  • It is asymptotically normally distributed (CAN): Standardized errors follow a normal distribution.
  • It has the smallest asymptotic covariance matrix: No other CAN estimator has a tighter limiting dispersion around the truth.

Core Intuition

"Asymptotic efficiency means: among all estimators that are well-behaved in large samples, the efficient one achieves the minimum asymptotic dispersion."

3. Interactive Lab: Comparing Large-Sample Precision

Consider two competing CAN estimators for a policy impact \(\theta = 3.0\). Both are correctly centered at \(\theta\). However, Estimator B (blue) is asymptotically efficient with normalized variance \(1.00\), whereas Estimator A (amber) is inefficient with a wider dispersion.

Asymptotic Relative Efficiency (ARE) 44.4%
Estimator B — Efficient (\(\operatorname{AsyVar} = 1.00\)) Estimator A — Inefficient (AsyVar = 2.25) Target \(\theta = 3.0\)
WHAT YOU ARE SEEING
Two large-sample estimator distributions centered at true \(\theta = 3.0\). Estimator B (blue) has minimal asymptotic variance (\(1.00\)). Estimator A (amber) is consistent but inefficient with larger variance.
WHAT TO NOTICE
Both curves are centered at \(\theta = 3.0\). But Estimator B is much more concentrated near the truth. The area under Estimator A is dispersed across a wider range of values, leading to wider confidence intervals.
WHY IT MATTERS
An Asymptotic Relative Efficiency of \(44.4\%\) means that using Estimator A requires more than double the sample size (\(1 / 0.444 \approx 2.25 \times\)) to achieve the same precision as Estimator B!
ECONOMETRIC INTERPRETATION
In costly research environments (such as randomized clinical trials or international field surveys), choosing an asymptotically efficient estimator saves substantial data collection costs without sacrificing accuracy.
TRY THIS: Slide Estimator A variance up to \(4.00\). Notice how the amber distribution flattens out, and the relative efficiency plunges to \(25.0\%\)—meaning Estimator A is 4 times noisier!

4. OLS vs. MLE: The Greene (7th ed.) Theorem

In asymptotic theory, how does Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) compare against Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)? Under normality, William Greene (7th ed., p. 110) notes the classic theorem:

Case 1: Normally Distributed Errors
\(\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2) \implies \hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} = \hat{\beta}_{\text{MLE}}\)

When the disturbance is normal, maximizing the log-likelihood function is mathematically equivalent to minimizing the sum of squared residuals. OLS is identically MLE, achieving the Cramér–Rao Lower Bound asymptotically.

Case 2: Non-Normal Distributed Errors
\(\hat{\beta}_{\text{MLE}}\) is More Efficient than \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}}\)

If errors follow a known non-normal distribution (e.g., Student's \(t\), Laplace, or Logistic), MLE exploits the explicit functional form of the density, extracting higher Fisher information. OLS only uses second moments and is less efficient.

CAUTION Econometric Nuance: Never Say "MLE is Always Better"

Why isn't MLE always used?
MLE's efficiency advantage relies entirely on correct specification of the error distribution. If you specify a Laplace error density when the truth is Cauchy or skewed, MLE can become severely inconsistent!

OLS, by contrast, requires no distributional assumption whatsoever to remain consistent and asymptotically normal. Econometricians often accept the modest efficiency penalty of OLS in exchange for its extraordinary robustness against distributional misspecification.

QUICK CHECK Asymptotic Efficiency

Estimator 1 has asymptotic variance \(\operatorname{AsyVar}(\hat{\theta}_1) = \frac{4}{n}\). Estimator 2 has asymptotic variance \(\operatorname{AsyVar}(\hat{\theta}_2) = \frac{9}{n}\). Both are consistent and asymptotically normal. Which estimator is more asymptotically efficient, and how many observations must Estimator 2 use to match the precision of Estimator 1 with \(n = 100\)?

Show Answer & Explanation
ANSWER: Estimator 1 is more efficient. Estimator 2 requires \(n = 225\) observations.

WHY?
  1. Estimator 1 has the smaller asymptotic variance (\(\frac{4}{n} < \frac{9}{n}\)), so it achieves higher large-sample precision.
  2. To achieve the same variance as Estimator 1 at \(n = 100\) (variance \(= 4/100 = 0.04\)), Estimator 2 must solve: \(\frac{9}{n_2} = 0.04 \implies n_2 = \frac{9}{0.04} = 225\). Estimator 2 requires \(2.25\) times as much data!
SUMMARY

What You Should Now Understand

  • A CAN estimator is both Consistent and Asymptotically Normal: \(\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}_n - \theta) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, V)\).
  • An estimator is asymptotically efficient if its asymptotic covariance matrix is smaller than or equal to that of any other CAN estimator.
  • Under Gaussian error disturbances, OLS is identical to MLE: \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} = \hat{\beta}_{\text{MLE}}\), and OLS is asymptotically efficient.
  • Under non-normal errors, correctly specified MLE is more efficient than OLS because it exploits full distributional information.
  • However, MLE is sensitive to distributional misspecification, whereas OLS remains consistent and asymptotically normal regardless of error distribution shape.
