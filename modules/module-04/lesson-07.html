Slutsky’s Theorem & Delta Method Applications

How do we combine an estimator that converges in distribution with an unknown variance that converges in probability? Discover Slutsky’s Theorem across sum, product, and ratio rules, and follow a complete Derivation Journey for the source's worked Delta Method example.

OBJECTIVES

Learning Objectives

  • State Slutsky’s Theorem across separate rules for sums, products, and ratios.
  • Explain why Slutsky’s Theorem is essential for justifying large-sample econometrics and the Delta Method.
  • Follow the connected step-by-step Derivation Journey for \(\hat{\beta}_2 = 2\) and \(\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\beta}_2) = 0.5\) to evaluate \(g(\beta_2) = \beta_2^2\).
  • Calculate the exact transformed standard error: \(\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\beta}_2^2) = 2.0\).
  • Construct the source 95% confidence interval \([0.08, 7.92]\) and observe how curvature expands estimation variance.

1. Slutsky’s Theorem: Combining Probability Limits & Distributions

In asymptotic theory, we constantly need to combine two different types of convergence:

  • A term \(\hat{\theta}_n\) that converges in distribution (e.g., to a normal curve by the CLT).
  • A term \(a_n\) that converges in probability to a constant (e.g., \(s^2 \xrightarrow{p} \sigma^2\) or \(\hat{\theta} \xrightarrow{p} \theta\)).

Slutsky’s Theorem provides the mathematical license to manipulate these limits using standard arithmetic operations:

Definition: Slutsky’s Theorem
\[\text{If } \hat{\theta}_n \xrightarrow{d} \theta \quad \text{and} \quad a_n \xrightarrow{p} a, \quad \text{then:}\]
1. Sum Rule
\(\hat{\theta}_n + a_n \xrightarrow{d} \theta + a\)

Adding a consistent estimator simply shifts the center of the limiting distribution by the constant \(a\).

2. Product Rule
\(\hat{\theta}_n \cdot a_n \xrightarrow{d} \theta \cdot a\)

Multiplying by a consistent estimator scales the limiting random variable by the non-random constant \(a\).

3. Ratio Rule
\(\frac{\hat{\theta}_n}{a_n} \xrightarrow{d} \frac{\theta}{a} \quad (a \neq 0)\)

Dividing by a consistent estimator is asymptotically identical to dividing by the constant limit \(a\), provided \(a \neq 0\).

INTUITION Asymptotic Foundations

"Slutsky’s Theorem lets us combine convergence in distribution (e.g., from Central Limit Theorem) with convergence in probability (e.g., a consistent estimator). It’s essential for justifying the Delta Method, which involves multiplying and dividing asymptotically normal terms by consistent estimates."

2. Connected Derivation: The Delta Method Worked Example

We now work through the exact practical application encountered in applied econometrics:

Given Regression Output

\(\hat{\beta}_2 = 2.0, \quad \operatorname{SE}(\hat{\beta}_2) = 0.5\)

We want the point estimate, approximate standard error, and 95% confidence interval for the nonlinear quantity \(g(\beta_2) = \beta_2^2\).

Derivation Goal

Estimate Uncertainty and Build 95% CI for β₂²

Propagate the regression uncertainty from \(\hat{\beta}_2 = 2.0\) to its quadratic transformation \(g(\beta_2) = \beta_2^2\).

1

Define the nonlinear function and take its derivative

g'(β₂) = 2β₂

Specify \(g(\beta_2)\) and differentiate with respect to \(\beta_2\):

\[g(\beta_2) = \beta_2^2 \implies g'(\beta_2) = \frac{d}{d\beta_2}(\beta_2^2) = 2\beta_2\]
Why this step works The Delta Method relies on a first-order Taylor series tangent-line expansion. We require the analytical first derivative to determine how the curve scales parameter movements.
2

Evaluate point estimate of the nonlinear function

g(2) = 4.0

Plug in the sample point estimate \(\hat{\beta}_2 = 2\):

\[g(\hat{\beta}_2) = \hat{\beta}_2^2 = 2^2 = 4.0\]
Why this step works By the continuous mapping property (Slutsky's theorem), if \(\hat{\beta}_2\) is a consistent estimator of \(\beta_2\), then \(g(\hat{\beta}_2)\) is a consistent estimator of \(g(\beta_2) = 4.0\).
3

Propagate asymptotic variance via the Delta Method

AsyVar = 4.0

Evaluate the derivative at \(\hat{\beta}_2 = 2\) and scale the original parameter variance:

\[\operatorname{AsyVar}(g(\hat{\beta}_2)) \approx \left[ g'(\hat{\beta}_2) \right]^2 \cdot \operatorname{Var}(\hat{\beta}_2) = (2 \cdot 2)^2 \cdot (0.5)^2 = 16 \cdot 0.25 = 4.0\]
Why this step works Because \(\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\beta}_2) = 0.5\), its variance is \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\beta}_2) = 0.5^2 = 0.25\). The derivative at \(\hat{\beta}_2 = 2\) is \(2 \cdot 2 = 4\). Squaring the derivative yields \(4^2 = 16\). Multiplying \(16 \times 0.25\) yields \(4.0\).
4

Take the square root to find the Standard Error

SE = 2.0

Extract the square root of the estimated asymptotic variance:

\[\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\beta}_2^2) = \sqrt{\operatorname{AsyVar}(g(\hat{\beta}_2))} = \sqrt{4.0} = 2.0\]
Why this step works Confidence intervals and test statistics require linear parameter units (standard errors), not squared variance units. Taking the positive square root of \(4.0\) gives \(\operatorname{SE} = 2.0\).
5

Construct the approximate 95% Confidence Interval

[0.08, 7.92]

Apply the standard large-sample normal critical value \(\pm 1.96\):

\[g(\hat{\beta}_2) \pm 1.96 \times \operatorname{SE}(g(\hat{\beta}_2)) = 4.0 \pm 1.96 \times 2.0 = 4.0 \pm 3.92 = [0.08, \; 7.92]\]
Why this step works By the Delta Method and asymptotic normality, \(g(\hat{\beta}_2) \xrightarrow{a} \mathcal{N}(4.0, 2.0^2)\). The 95% interval encompasses \(1.96\) standard errors on either side of the point estimate \(4.0\).
Big Picture: Variance Expands Through Nonlinearity

Notice the profound difference: \(\hat{\beta}_2 = 2.0\) had a tight standard error of \(0.5\) (\(95\%\) CI: \([1.02, 2.98]\)).
Its square \(g(\hat{\beta}_2) = 4.0\) has a standard error of \(2.0\) (\(95\%\) CI: \([0.08, 7.92]\)).
Because the derivative is \(4.0\), the standard error expanded by four times (\(4 \times 0.5 = 2.0\)), and the confidence interval widened dramatically!

WHAT TO NOTICE
The right panel distribution is four times as wide as the left panel distribution. The derivative \(g'(\beta_2) = 2 \times 2 = 4\) scales the original standard error \(0.5\) up to \(2.0\).
WHY IT MATTERS
Even if a linear coefficient is highly statistically significant (\(t = 2.0 / 0.5 = 4.0\)), a nonlinear transformation can have substantial uncertainty if the operating point lies in a high-derivative region.
ECONOMETRIC INTERPRETATION
In empirical economics, policy conclusions must be tested on the transformed parameter of interest (like the peak turning point), not assumed to inherit the precision of the raw linear coefficients.
OBSERVE Notice the lower bound of the 95% interval: \(4.0 - 1.96(2.0) = 0.08\). It barely excludes zero! Although \(\hat{\beta}_2 = 2\) was four standard errors from zero, \(\hat{\beta}_2^2\) is just above the boundary of statistical significance at the 5% level.
QUICK CHECK Ratio Transformation

Suppose an estimator gives \(\hat{\beta}_1 = 10\) with \(\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\beta}_1) = 1.0\). We are interested in \(g(\beta_1) = \frac{1}{\beta_1}\). What is \(g'(\beta_1)\) at \(\hat{\beta}_1 = 10\), and what is the approximate standard error \(\operatorname{SE}(1/\hat{\beta}_1)\)?

Show Answer & Explanation
ANSWER: \(g'(10) = -0.01\), and \(\operatorname{SE}(1/\hat{\beta}_1) = 0.01\).

WHY?
  1. The derivative of \(g(\beta_1) = \beta_1^{-1}\) is \(g'(\beta_1) = -\beta_1^{-2} = -\frac{1}{\beta_1^2}\).
  2. Evaluated at \(\hat{\beta}_1 = 10\), \(g'(10) = -\frac{1}{100} = -0.01\).
  3. By the Delta Method, \(\operatorname{SE}(g(\hat{\beta}_1)) \approx |g'(\hat{\beta}_1)| \times \operatorname{SE}(\hat{\beta}_1) = |-0.01| \times 1.0 = 0.01\). In this case, because \(|g'| < 1\), the transformation compressed uncertainty!
SUMMARY

What You Should Now Understand

  • Slutsky’s Theorem justifies arithmetic operations between sequences converging in distribution and sequences converging in probability across sum, product, and ratio rules.
  • For the source worked example with \(\hat{\beta}_2 = 2.0\) and \(\operatorname{SE} = 0.5\), the derivative of \(g(\beta_2) = \beta_2^2\) is \(g'(\beta_2) = 2\beta_2\).
  • Evaluating at \(\hat{\beta}_2 = 2.0\) yields point estimate \(g(2) = 4.0\) and derivative \(g'(2) = 4.0\).
  • The transformed asymptotic variance is \((4.0)^2 \times (0.5)^2 = 16 \times 0.25 = 4.0\), giving standard error \(\operatorname{SE} = \sqrt{4.0} = 2.0\).
  • The approximate 95% confidence interval is \(4.0 \pm 1.96(2.0) = [0.08, 7.92]\), visually demonstrating how nonlinear functions expand variance.
