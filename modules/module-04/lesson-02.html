Consistency of OLS & Large-Sample Conditions

How does the Classical Linear Regression Model adapt when sample size grows without bound? Unpack the large-sample limit condition \(\lim \frac{X'X}{n} = Q\), inspect the Grenander regularity conditions, and follow the rigorous proof that \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} \xrightarrow{p} \beta\).

OBJECTIVES Learning Objectives

  • State the modified CLRM assumptions for asymptotic analysis with independent observations of \((x_i, \varepsilon_i)\).
  • Explain intuitively why \(\frac{X'X}{n}\) stabilizes to a positive definite limit matrix \(Q\).
  • Understand the Grenander conditions for well-behaved data and why they protect asymptotic identification.
  • Follow the connected step-by-step proof establishing that \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} \xrightarrow{p} \beta\).
  • Visualize how the sampling distribution of \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}}\) collapses around the true parameter as \(n\) increases.

1. The Large-Sample OLS Setup

In finite-sample theory, we held the regressor matrix \(X\) fixed or conditioned upon it. But as \(n \to \infty\), rows are continuously added to \(X\) and \(\varepsilon\). In asymptotic analysis, we maintain Assumptions 1–4 of the Classical Linear Regression Model (linearity, full column rank, and mean-zero disturbances) and modify the sampling assumption:

Modified Assumption 5: Independent Data Generation

For each observation \(i = 1, \dots, n\), the pair \((x_i, \varepsilon_i)\) is drawn as a sequence of independent observations. Exogeneity holds such that \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_i \mid x_i] = 0\) and \(\mathbb{E}[x_i \varepsilon_i] = \mathbf{0}\).

Next, we require a crucial mathematical condition on the regressors as sample size accumulates:

Key Large-Sample Condition: Stabilizing Regressor Moments
\[\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{X'X}{n} = Q \quad \text{where } Q \text{ is a finite, positive definite matrix}\]
The sample average of regressor cross-products converges to a non-singular limit matrix \(Q\), ensuring \(Q^{-1}\) exists.

2. What Does the Matrix Q Mean Intuitively?

Why do we divide \(X'X\) by \(n\)?

Notice that \(X'X = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i x_i'\). Each time a new observation is added, another positive semi-definite term is added to the sum. If we did not divide by \(n\), the entries of \(X'X\) would grow infinitely large as \(n \to \infty\)!

Average Cross-Product Sample Moment

\(\frac{X'X}{n} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i x_i'\) calculates the sample average of regressor cross-products (second moments). By the Law of Large Numbers, this sample average converges to its population expectation: \(\mathbb{E}[x_i x_i'] = Q\).

Positive Definiteness & Identification

Requiring \(Q\) to be positive definite means \(Q\) has full rank \(k\) and is strictly invertible (\(Q^{-1}\) exists). The regressors retain genuine independent variation and do not become collinear even in the infinite limit.

3. Grenander Regularity Conditions for Well-Behaved Data

In modern econometric practice (citing Greene, 7th ed., Table 4.2), researchers often specify the somewhat less restrictive Grenander conditions to guarantee that the data are well-behaved:

REGULARITY

Grenander Regularity Conditions (Informal Summary)

  • No collapsing regressors: None of the \(x\)'s go to a series of zeros. Regressors must continue to provide empirical variation as the sample expands.
  • No dominant observations: No single observation of any \(x\) is too large relative to the sum. That is, \(\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{\max_{i} x_{ik}^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n x_{ik}^2} = 0\). No single outlier dominates the entire dataset.
  • Full rank preservation: The matrix \(X\) remains full column rank (\(\operatorname{rank}(X) = k\)) for all sufficiently large \(n\), preventing asymptotic multicollinearity.
These regularity conditions are virtually always satisfied in well-designed empirical economic datasets.

4. Connected Derivation: Proof of OLS Consistency

We now prove that under these regularity conditions, the OLS estimator converges in probability to the true parameter vector: \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} \xrightarrow{p} \beta\).

Derivation Goal

Prove that \(\operatorname{plim}_{n \to \infty} \hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} = \beta\)

Demonstrate that the sampling error \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} - \beta\) collapses to \(\mathbf{0}\) in probability as \(n \to \infty\).

1

Express the OLS estimation error

Substitute the data generation process \(y = X\beta + \varepsilon\) into the OLS estimator \(\hat{\beta} = (X'X)^{-1}X'y\):

\[\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} - \beta = (X'X)^{-1}X'\varepsilon\]
Why this step works Because \(y = X\beta + \varepsilon\), expanding gives \((X'X)^{-1}X'(X\beta + \varepsilon) = \beta + (X'X)^{-1}X'\varepsilon\). Subtracting \(\beta\) isolates the estimation error.
2

Scale both factors by sample size n

Multiply and divide by \(n\) to convert the matrix sums into sample averages:

\[\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} - \beta = \left(\frac{X'X}{n}\right)^{-1} \left(\frac{X'\varepsilon}{n}\right)\]
Why this step works \((X'X)^{-1} = \frac{1}{n} \left(\frac{X'X}{n}\right)^{-1}\). The factor of \(\frac{1}{n}\) is grouped with \(X'\varepsilon\), forming the sample average vector \(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i \varepsilon_i\).
3

Evaluate the limit of the inverse moment matrix

Limit = Q⁻¹

Apply the large-sample regularity condition \(\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{X'X}{n} = Q\):

\[\operatorname{plim}_{n \to \infty} \left(\frac{X'X}{n}\right)^{-1} = Q^{-1}\]
Why this step works By the continuous mapping theorem (Slutsky's theorem for continuous matrix inversion), the probability limit of an inverse equals the inverse of the probability limit, provided \(Q\) is non-singular.
4

Apply the Law of Large Numbers to disturbance cross-products

Limit = 0

Apply Khinchine's Law of Large Numbers to the sample average of independent cross-products \(x_i \varepsilon_i\):

\[\operatorname{plim}_{n \to \infty} \left(\frac{X'\varepsilon}{n}\right) = \operatorname{plim}_{n \to \infty} \left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i \varepsilon_i\right) = \mathbb{E}[x_i \varepsilon_i] = \mathbf{0}\]
Why this step works Under exogeneity, \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_i \mid x_i] = 0\), so \(\mathbb{E}[x_i \varepsilon_i] = \mathbb{E}[x_i \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_i \mid x_i]] = \mathbf{0}\). The sample average converges in probability to its population zero mean.
5

Combine probability limits (Consistency Result)

Q⁻¹ · 0 = 0

Multiply the limits using Slutsky's theorem:

\[\operatorname{plim}_{n \to \infty} (\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} - \beta) = Q^{-1} \cdot \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0} \implies \hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} \xrightarrow{p} \beta\]
Why this step works The probability limit of a product of matrices equals the product of their probability limits. Multiplying a finite matrix \(Q^{-1}\) by the zero vector produces \(\mathbf{0}\).
The Big Picture: Asymptotic Concentration

Formally, the asymptotic concentration result is stated as: \[\lim_{n \to \infty} \Pr(\beta - \varepsilon < \hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} < \beta + \varepsilon) = 1\] As sample size \(n \to \infty\), the probability that \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}}\) lies within any arbitrarily small interval around the true \(\beta\) approaches 1. OLS is a strictly consistent estimator of \(\beta\).

QUICK CHECK The Role of Matrix Q

What would happen to the consistency of the OLS estimator if as \(n \to \infty\), two regressors become perfectly correlated, causing the limit matrix \(Q = \lim \frac{X'X}{n}\) to have determinant 0 (singular)?

Show Answer & Explanation
ANSWER: The OLS estimator would fail to be consistent for the individual coefficients.

WHY? If \(Q\) is singular, \(Q^{-1}\) does not exist. In Step 3 of our derivation, \(\operatorname{plim}\left(\frac{X'X}{n}\right)^{-1}\) blows up or cannot be defined. Without positive definiteness of \(Q\), the data fail to provide independent variation to distinguish the two regressors, and identification breaks down asymptotically.
SUMMARY

What You Should Now Understand

  • The large-sample OLS framework assumes independent observation pairs \((x_i, \varepsilon_i)\) with exogeneity \(\mathbb{E}[x_i \varepsilon_i] = \mathbf{0}\).
  • \(\frac{X'X}{n}\) averages the regressor cross-products; as \(n \to \infty\), it stabilizes to the finite positive definite matrix \(Q\).
  • Grenander regularity conditions ensure regressors do not collapse to zero, no single observation dominates, and \(X\) retains full rank.
  • The proof of consistency scales \(\hat{\beta} - \beta = \left(\frac{X'X}{n}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{X'\varepsilon}{n}\right)\); by the LLN, \(\frac{X'\varepsilon}{n} \xrightarrow{p} \mathbf{0}\), proving \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} \xrightarrow{p} \beta\).
  • Consistency means \(\Pr(|\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} - \beta| > \varepsilon) \to 0\): the sampling distribution contracts to a single point at the true parameter vector.
