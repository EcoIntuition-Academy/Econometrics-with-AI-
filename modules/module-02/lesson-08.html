Orthogonal Regressors, Randomization & Module Review

Discover what happens when regressors are mutually perpendicular: partialling out leaves coefficients completely unaltered. Connect this mathematical mechanism to the power of randomized experiments, review the unified geometric journey of Module 2, and test your mastery with the comprehensive source quiz.

1. Orthogonal Regressors & Subspace Perpendicularity

Consider again our partitioned regression matrix \(X = [X_1 \quad X_2]\). Suppose that the two sets of regressors are mutually orthogonal in the sample inner product:

CONDITION Sample Regressor Orthogonality
$$X_1' X_2 = \mathbf{0}$$
WHERE
\(X_1'X_2 = \mathbf{0}\) Every column of regressor block \(X_1\) has an inner product of zero with every column of \(X_2\).
\(\operatorname{Col}(X_1) \perp \operatorname{Col}(X_2)\) The two regression subspaces are perpendicular under the standard Euclidean inner product in \(\mathbb{R}^n\).

This is a very strong geometric condition: it means that no component of any column in \(X_2\) lies inside the column space spanned by \(X_1\). Consequently, projecting \(X_2\) onto \(\operatorname{Col}(X_1)\) produces zero: \(P_1 X_2 = \mathbf{0}\).

2. FWL Simplification Under Orthogonality

Let us trace what happens when we apply the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell residual-maker matrix \(M_1\) to \(X_2\):

Step-by-Step Derivation of Coefficient Invariance

Step 1 · Expand the Operator:
$$X_2' M_1 = X_2' \left(I - X_1(X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'\right)$$
Step 2 · Distribute across the Parenthesis:
$$X_2' M_1 = X_2' - X_2' X_1 (X_1'X_1)^{-1} X_1'$$
Step 3 · Apply Orthogonality:

Because \(X_1' X_2 = \mathbf{0} \implies X_2' X_1 = \mathbf{0}'\), the subtracted projection component vanishes completely:

$$\mathbf{0} \cdot (X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1' = \mathbf{0}$$
Step 4 · Residualized Regressor Simplification:
$$X_2' M_1 = X_2' \quad \text{and} \quad M_1 X_2 = X_2$$

Partialling out \(X_1\) does not remove anything from \(X_2\), because \(X_2\) had zero overlap with \(\operatorname{Col}(X_1)\) to begin with!

Step 5 · Plug Back into the FWL Estimator:
$$\hat{\beta}_2 = (X_2' M_1 X_2)^{-1} X_2' M_1 y = (X_2' X_2)^{-1} X_2' y$$
INVARIANCE THEOREM Regression Invariance Under Regressor Orthogonality
$$\hat{\beta}_2 = (X_2' X_2)^{-1} X_2' y$$
WHERE
Multiple Regression The coefficient \(\hat{\beta}_2\) obtained from regressing \(y\) on \([X_1, X_2]\).
Univariate Regression The coefficient obtained from regressing \(y\) on \(X_2\) alone, omitting \(X_1\) entirely.

In words: If \(X_1\) and \(X_2\) are sample-orthogonal, including or omitting \(X_1\) from the regression produces exactly the same coefficient estimate on \(X_2\).

3. Randomization & Experimental Econometrics

This mathematical result provides the primary motivation for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in empirical economics.

Suppose \(X_2 = D\) is a binary treatment indicator (\(D_i \in \{0, 1\}\)), and \(X_1\) contains pre-treatment baseline characteristics (such as age, prior test scores, or baseline income).

DESIGN-BASED INDEPENDENCE

Under random assignment, treatment assignment is determined by a physical randomization mechanism (such as a lottery), so \(D \perp X_{\text{pre}}\) by design. In expectation, treatment status does not systematically depend on pre-treatment covariates:

$$\mathbb{E}[X_{\text{pre}} \mid D = 1] = \mathbb{E}[X_{\text{pre}} \mid D = 0]$$

In finite samples, chance imbalance can still occur. As sample size grows under standard randomized designs, these imbalances typically become small.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE

“Random assignment guarantees exact sample orthogonality between treatment and every baseline covariate.”

Correction: Random assignment guarantees independence by design, not exact equality of realized sample covariate means in every finite sample. Chance imbalance can remain.

Important Mathematical Caveat: Uncentered Variables & the Intercept

If \(X_1\) includes an intercept column of ones \(\iota\), then \(\iota'D = \sum_{i=1}^n D_i = n_1\) (the total number of treated units), which is strictly positive! Furthermore, for an uncentered binary indicator \(D\) and non-negative covariate \(X\), \(\mathbb{E}[XD] = \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[D] = p\mathbb{E}[X] \neq 0\).
What converges to zero under independence is the sample covariance: \[\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n (D_i - \bar{D})(X_i - \bar{X}) \xrightarrow{p} 0\] Therefore, in empirical work, we evaluate orthogonality after mean-centering or partialling out the intercept.

Difference in Means & the Average Treatment Effect

Under randomized treatment assignment, the treated-control difference in mean outcomes provides an unbiased estimator of the average treatment effect under the randomization design:

$$\hat{\tau} = \bar{y}_{D=1} - \bar{y}_{D=0}$$

Why Include Controls in Randomized Experiments?

If the simple difference in means is already unbiased, why do researchers include pre-treatment controls \(X_1\)?

  • Precision & Variance Reduction: Including strong pre-treatment predictors of the outcome can improve precision by explaining outcome variation that is unrelated to treatment. However, the amount of precision improvement depends on the covariates and specification.
  • Adjusting for Chance Imbalance: In any single finite sample, random assignment may leave small chance differences in baseline covariates between treatment and control groups; controlling for \(X_1\) explicitly adjusts for these realized differences.

The Randomization + FWL Connection

FWL tells us that the treatment coefficient depends on the component of treatment remaining after projecting treatment on the controls. Under random assignment, treatment is unrelated in expectation to pre-treatment covariates. Therefore, in well-balanced randomized samples, residualizing treatment on those controls typically changes the treatment variation only modestly. This provides the core intuition for why treatment-effect estimates can remain similar with or without baseline controls, while precision may change.

5. Module 2 Source Quiz & Derivations

These six comprehensive problem sets are taken directly from the primary source document (Econometrics 2.docx). They test rigorous geometric intuition, matrix derivations, and econometric reasoning. Work through each problem before revealing the hints and full derivations.

PROBLEM SET 1 Projection Intuition

1. Geometric Projection & Residual Orthogonality

(a) Explain in your own words why the OLS fitted values \(X\hat{\beta}\) can be viewed as the orthogonal projection of \(y\) onto the space spanned by the columns of \(X\).

(b) Why must the residual vector \(e\) be orthogonal to every column of \(X\) in that picture?

Hint: Think of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) as the universe of all possible outcome vectors. The columns of \(X\) form a flat hyperplane \(\operatorname{Col}(X)\). Under Euclidean distance, what is the shortest path from a point outside a plane to a point on that plane?

Derivation & Academic Explanation

(a) Any candidate fitted vector is a linear combination of the columns of \(X\), so \(Xb \in \operatorname{Col}(X)\). The OLS criterion minimizes the squared Euclidean distance \(\|y - Xb\|^2 = \sum (y_i - x_i'b)^2\). In Euclidean geometry, the unique point on a linear subspace closest to an exterior point \(y\) is the orthogonal projection \(\hat{y}\). Thus, \(\hat{y} = X\hat{\beta}\) is the orthogonal shadow of \(y\) on \(\operatorname{Col}(X)\).

(b) The residual vector is the geometric difference vector \(e = y - \hat{y}\). If \(e\) were not orthogonal to \(\operatorname{Col}(X)\), it would have a non-zero component lying inside \(\operatorname{Col}(X)\). We could then add that component to \(\hat{y}\) and strictly decrease the distance to \(y\), violating the assumption that \(\hat{y}\) is already the closest point. Therefore, the shortest distance vector must be strictly perpendicular to every basis direction of the subspace: \(x_j' e = 0\) for all \(j=1,\dots,k\).

PROBLEM SET 2 Normal Equations & Residuals

2. Normal Equations Derivation & Residual Matrix Algebra

(a) Starting from \(S(b)=(y-Xb)'(y-Xb)\), show every algebraic step needed to reach the normal equations \(X'X\hat{\beta}=X'y\).

(b) Using the normal equations, derive the relation \(X'e=0\), and interpret this condition both algebraically and geometrically.

Hint: For (a), expand the quadratic form using transpose rules and note that \(b'X'y\) is a \(1 \times 1\) scalar. For (b), substitute \(e = y - X\hat{\beta}\) into \(X'e\).

Derivation & Academic Explanation

(a) Expanding the quadratic objective: \[ S(b) = y'y - b'X'y - y'Xb + b'X'Xb \] Since \(b'X'y = (b'X'y)' = y'Xb\) is a scalar, the cross-terms combine: \[ S(b) = y'y - 2b'X'y + b'X'Xb \] Taking the gradient with respect to vector \(b\): \[ \frac{\partial S(b)}{\partial b} = -2X'y + 2X'Xb \] Setting the gradient to zero at the optimal candidate \(b = \hat{\beta}\): \[ -2X'y + 2X'X\hat{\beta} = \mathbf{0} \implies X'X\hat{\beta} = X'y \]

(b) Subtract \(X'X\hat{\beta}\) from both sides or factor: \[ X'y - X'X\hat{\beta} = \mathbf{0} \implies X'(y - X\hat{\beta}) = \mathbf{0} \implies X'e = \mathbf{0} \] Algebraic interpretation: Writing \(X'e\) row by row: \(x_j' e = \sum_{i=1}^n x_{ij} e_i = 0\) for each regressor column \(j=1,\dots,k\). The sample inner product between each regressor and the residual is zero.
Geometric interpretation: The residual vector \(e\) is perpendicular (\(90^\circ\) angle) to every column of \(X\), and thus to the subspace \(\operatorname{Col}(X)\).

PROBLEM SET 3 Means, Constants & Balancing

3. Sample Centroid & Mean Fitted Equality

(a) In a single regressor model with constant, prove that the regression line passes through \((\bar{x}, \bar{y})\).

(b) For a multiple regression with a constant term, derive \(\bar{\hat{y}}=\bar{y}\) and interpret what this says about the relationship between the sample mean of the outcome and the fitted model.

Hint: What equation do you get when you set the derivative with respect to the intercept \(\hat{\beta}_0\) to zero? Use \(\sum e_i = 0\).

Derivation & Academic Explanation

(a) The normal equation corresponding to the constant column \(\iota\) is \(\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{\beta}_0 - \hat{\beta}_1 x_i) = 0\). Dividing the entire sum by sample size \(n\): \[ \frac{1}{n}\sum y_i - \hat{\beta}_0 - \hat{\beta}_1 \left(\frac{1}{n}\sum x_i\right) = 0 \implies \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_0 - \hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x} = 0 \implies \bar{y} = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x} \] This confirms that evaluating the fitted regression line at \(x = \bar{x}\) produces exactly \(\hat{y} = \bar{y}\). The fitted line passes through the sample-mean centroid point.

(b) Each observation decomposes as \(y_i = \hat{y}_i + e_i\). Summing over all \(n\) observations: \[ \sum_{i=1}^n y_i = \sum_{i=1}^n \hat{y}_i + \sum_{i=1}^n e_i \] Because an intercept is included, \(\iota'e = \sum e_i = 0\). Thus: \[ \sum_{i=1}^n y_i = \sum_{i=1}^n \hat{y}_i \implies \frac{1}{n}\sum y_i = \frac{1}{n}\sum \hat{y}_i \implies \bar{y} = \bar{\hat{y}} \] Interpretation: The model neither overpredicts nor underpredicts the outcome in aggregate. The fitted model is globally unbiased on the sample.

PROBLEM SET 4 FWL Mechanics

4. Frisch–Waugh–Lovell Residualization Steps

Consider the model \(y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \beta_2 X_2 + \varepsilon\). Describe in detail:

(a) How to compute the residuals \(y^*\) and \(X_2^*\) that are orthogonal to \(X_1\).

(b) How to obtain \(\hat{\beta}_2\) from a regression of \(y^*\) on \(X_2^*\), and why this is equal to the coefficient on \(X_2\) in the full regression.

Hint: Remember that the control block includes both the constant and \(X_1\): \(W_1 = [\iota \quad X_1]\). Apply its residual-maker matrix \(M_1\).

Derivation & Academic Explanation

(a) Define the control matrix \(W_1 = [\iota \quad X_1]\) and its residual-maker matrix \(M_1 = I - W_1(W_1'W_1)^{-1}W_1'\).

  1. Regress \(y\) on \(W_1\) and collect residuals: \(y^* = M_1 y = y - W_1(W_1'W_1)^{-1}W_1'y\).
  2. Regress \(X_2\) on \(W_1\) and collect residuals: \(X_2^* = M_1 X_2 = X_2 - W_1(W_1'W_1)^{-1}W_1'X_2\).
Both \(y^*\) and \(X_2^*\) are orthogonal to \(\operatorname{Col}(W_1)\) and satisfy \(W_1'y^* = \mathbf{0}\) and \(W_1'X_2^* = \mathbf{0}\).

(b) Regress \(y^*\) on \(X_2^*\) via OLS without controls: \[ \hat{\beta}_2 = (X_2^{*\prime} X_2^*)^{-1} X_2^{*\prime} y^* \] By substituting \(y^* = M_1 y\) and \(X_2^* = M_1 X_2\) and using the idempotence and symmetry of \(M_1\) (\(M_1'M_1 = M_1\)): \[ (X_2' M_1' M_1 X_2)^{-1} X_2' M_1' M_1 y = (X_2' M_1 X_2)^{-1} X_2' M_1 y \] This formula matches the algebraic solution for the second block of coefficients from the partitioned normal equations of the full regression \([W_1 \quad X_2]\). Thus, \(\hat{\beta}_2\) isolates the unique partial association between \(X_2\) and \(y\).

PROBLEM SET 5 Demeaning & Covariance Form

5. Demeaning Operator & Co-Movement Slope Form

(a) Show that regressing \(y\) on \(x\) is equivalent to regressing \(y-\bar{y}\) on \(x-\bar{x}\) without an intercept, and write explicitly how the constant term can be recovered afterward.

(b) Derive the formula \(\hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}[x,y]}{\operatorname{Var}[x]}\) in simple regression and interpret what it means about the slope being a measure of co-movement.

Hint: Apply FWL where the control block is just the constant column \(\iota\). Note that \(M_0 = I - \frac{1}{n}\iota\iota'\). For (b), divide numerator and denominator by \(n-1\).

Derivation & Academic Explanation

(a) In FWL, let the controls consist only of the constant column \(\iota\). The residual-maker is \(M_0 = I - \iota(\iota'\iota)^{-1}\iota' = I - \frac{1}{n}\iota\iota'\). Applying \(M_0\) to any vector \(z\) gives: \[ M_0 z = z - \left(\frac{1}{n}\sum z_i\right)\iota = z - \bar{z}\iota \] By FWL, the slope coefficient on \(x\) in the full model is identical to the univariate regression of \(y^* = M_0 y\) on \(x^* = M_0 x\) without an intercept: \[ \hat{\beta}_1 = (x^{*\prime} x^*)^{-1} x^{*\prime} y^* = \frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2} \] The intercept is recovered afterward from the regression-through-means condition: \(\hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x}\).

(b) Dividing numerator and denominator by \(n-1\): \[ \hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2} = \frac{\widehat{\operatorname{Cov}}(x,y)}{\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(x)} \] Interpretation: The numerator measures sample co-movement between \(x\) and \(y\). The denominator measures the dispersion of \(x\) on its own. The slope is not a unitless correlation; rather, it scales the co-movement per unit of variation in \(x\).

PROBLEM SET 6 Orthogonality & Randomization

6. Regressor Orthogonality & Experimental Causal Identification

(a) Suppose regressors are partitioned into \(X_1\) and a treatment indicator \(D\), and assume \(X_1'D = 0\). Show rigorously that the coefficient on \(D\) is the same in the regressions \(y \sim D\) and \(y \sim X_1 + D\).

(b) Using this, give a careful explanation of why random assignment of a treatment makes simple differences in means a valid estimator of the average treatment effect, even if many other covariates could in principle be controlled for.

Hint: Use \(D'M_1 = D'\) when \(X_1'D = 0\). For (b), explain how design independence ensures omitted variable bias vanishes asymptotically.

Derivation & Academic Explanation

(a) In the multiple regression \(y \sim X_1 + D\), the FWL estimator for the treatment coefficient is: \[ \hat{\beta}_D^{\text{full}} = (D' M_1 D)^{-1} D' M_1 y \] where \(M_1 = I - X_1(X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'\). Expanding \(D'M_1\): \[ D' M_1 = D' - D'X_1(X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1' = D' - \mathbf{0} = D' \] Substituting this back into the estimator: \[ \hat{\beta}_D^{\text{full}} = (D' D)^{-1} D' y = \hat{\beta}_D^{\text{univariate}} \] The coefficient on \(D\) is identically equal to the estimate obtained from regressing \(y\) on \(D\) alone.

(b) When treatment \(D\) is randomly assigned, the assignment process is independent of all pre-treatment characteristics \(X_1\) by design: \(\mathbb{E}[X_1 \mid D=1] = \mathbb{E}[X_1 \mid D=0]\). In expectation and in large samples, the centered covariance between \(D\) and any pre-treatment confounder is zero (\(\frac{1}{n}\sum(D_i - \bar{D})(X_{1i} - \bar{X}_1) \xrightarrow{p} \mathbf{0}\)). Therefore, omitting \(X_1\) does not introduce omitted variable bias into the treatment effect estimator. A simple difference in means (\(\bar{y}_{D=1} - \bar{y}_{D=0}\)) consistently identifies the Average Treatment Effect (ATE). Controlling for \(X_1\) is primarily useful for reducing residual variance and improving estimation precision.
