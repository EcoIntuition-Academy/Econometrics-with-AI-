LESSON 2.5 Centroid Geometry

Means, Fitted Values & Regression Balance

Explore the profound balancing properties of OLS with an intercept: why the fitted regression hyperplane is anchored to the sample centroid \((\bar{x}, \bar{y})\) and why the average predicted outcome always equals the average observed outcome.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Prove that the regression hyperplane crosses the sample centroid: \(\bar{y} = \bar{x}'\hat{\beta}\).
  • Derive why the mean of the fitted values equals the mean of the actual outcomes: \(\bar{\hat{y}} = \bar{y}\).
  • Trace the structural link: \(\text{Intercept Included} \implies \sum e_i = 0 \implies \bar{e} = 0 \implies \bar{\hat{y}} = \bar{y}\).
  • Visualize how the OLS regression line pivots around the centroid point \((\bar{x}, \bar{y})\).

1. The Regression Hyperplane Passes Through Sample Means

In any regression model that contains an intercept constant term, a celebrated property holds: the fitted regression line passes directly through the point of sample means.

To see why this occurs mathematically, start from the zero-sum residual property established in Lesson 2.4:

$$\sum_{i=1}^n e_i = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - x_i'\hat{\beta}) = 0$$

Expanding the summation across both terms:

$$\sum_{i=1}^n y_i - \sum_{i=1}^n x_i'\hat{\beta} = 0 \implies \sum_{i=1}^n y_i = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i'\hat{\beta}$$

Now divide both sides by the sample size \(n\):

$$\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n y_i = \left(\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n x_i'\right) \hat{\beta}$$

Recognizing the sample mean of the outcome \(\bar{y} = \frac{1}{n}\sum y_i\) and the vector of regressor sample means \(\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum x_i\), we arrive at the elegant theorem:

WITH AN INTERCEPT Regression Through the Sample Means
$$\bar{y} = \bar{x}'\hat{\beta}$$
WHERE
\(\bar{y}\) Sample mean of the observed outcome
\(\bar{x}\) Vector of regressor sample means, with leading 1 for the intercept: \([1, \bar{x}_2, \dots, \bar{x}_k]'\)
\(\bar{x}'\hat{\beta}\) Fitted value evaluated at the sample mean of the regressors. In simple regression, \(\hat{y}(\bar{x}) = \bar{y}\), so the fitted line passes directly through the sample-mean point \((\bar{x}, \bar{y})\).

2. Mean of Fitted Equals Mean of Observed Outcomes

A direct consequence of residual balancing with an intercept is that the average fitted prediction always equals the average actual observed value:

$$y = \hat{y} + e$$

Taking sample averages across all \(n\) observations:

$$\bar{y} = \bar{\hat{y}} + \bar{e}$$

Because \(\bar{e} = 0\) when an intercept is included, the residual average vanishes:

WITH AN INTERCEPT Mean Fitted Value Equals Mean Actual Value
$$\bar{\hat{y}} = \bar{y}$$
WHERE
\(\bar{\hat{y}}\) Sample mean of the fitted values: \(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \hat{y}_i\)
\(\bar{y}\) Sample mean of the observed outcomes: \(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n y_i\)

This means an OLS model with an intercept is unconditionally unbiased in sample: it never systematically over-predicts or under-predicts the aggregate level of the outcome.

Knowing that the line crosses \((\bar{x}, \bar{y})\) provides the algebraic key to recovering the intercept: \(\hat{\beta}_1 = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_2\bar{x}\).

📊 ECONOMETRIC INTERPRETATION

In a linear regression with an intercept, evaluating the fitted regression at the vector of sample regressor means gives the sample mean of the fitted outcomes, which equals the sample mean of \(y\). For simple regression, this corresponds geometrically to the fitted line passing through the centroid \((\bar{x}, \bar{y})\).

3. The Intercept Balancing Chain

WITH AN INTERCEPT INCLUDED IN THE MODEL:

1. Vector of ones is in regressor subspace: \(\iota \in \text{Col}(X)\)
↓
2. First row of normal equations forces: \(\iota'e = 0 \iff \sum_{i=1}^n e_i = 0\)
↓
3. Average residual is identically zero: \(\bar{e} = 0\)
↓
4. Mean Fitted = Mean Observed (\(\bar{\hat{y}} = \bar{y}\)) & Line Crosses Centroid (\(\bar{y} = \bar{x}'\hat{\beta}\))
