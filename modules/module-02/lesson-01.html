LESSON 2.1 Geometric Foundations

OLS as Orthogonal Projection

See OLS through the fundamental geometric intuition of the "closest possible shadow": projecting the observed outcome vector \(y\) perpendicularly onto the regressor column space \(\text{Col}(X)\).

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Conceptualize the \(n\)-dimensional observation space \(\mathbb{R}^n\) and the lower-dimensional regressor subspace \(\text{Col}(X)\).
  • Explain OLS as finding the unique point on \(\text{Col}(X)\) that lies closest in Euclidean squared distance to \(y\).
  • Identify the fitted vector \(\hat{y} = X\hat{\beta}\) as the orthogonal shadow of \(y\).
  • Recognize why the residual vector \(e = y - \hat{y}\) points straight out of the surface at a right angle (\(e \perp \text{Col}(X)\)).

1. Seeing OLS as the "Closest Possible Shadow"

Imagine each observation in your sample as one coordinate axis in a giant room: if there are \(n\) observations, the room has \(n\) dimensions (\(\mathbb{R}^n\)).

The outcome vector \(y\) is a single point (or vector from the origin) in this \(n\)-dimensional room: one coordinate per observed unit:

$$y = \begin{bmatrix} y_1 \\ y_2 \\ \vdots \\ y_n \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^n$$

The explanatory variables in the \(n \times k\) design matrix \(X\) define a lower-dimensional flat surface—a linear subspace inside this large room—spanned by the \(k\) column vectors of \(X\). We call this subspace the column space of \(X\), denoted \(\text{Col}(X)\):

$$\text{Col}(X) = \left\{ z \in \mathbb{R}^n : z = Xb \text{ for some } b \in \mathbb{R}^k \right\}$$

Because real-world economic outcomes are not deterministically explained by \(X\), the observed vector \(y\) almost never lies directly inside \(\text{Col}(X)\). It floats somewhere outside, above the subspace.

The Core Geometric Intuition

What OLS does is simple: it finds the point on this flat surface that lies as close as possible to the point \(y\). In other words, you shine a light orthogonally down onto the subspace spanned by \(X\) and look at where the "shadow" of \(y\) lands.

That shadow is the fitted vector \(\hat{y} = X\hat{\beta}\). The residual vector \(e = y - \hat{y}\) is the difference between \(y\) and its shadow; it points straight out of the surface, at a strict right angle (\(90^\circ\)).

GEOMETRIC SPECIFICATION Orthogonal Projection & Residual Decomposition
$$y = \hat{y} + e = X\hat{\beta} + e \quad \text{where } e \perp \text{Col}(X)$$
WHERE
\(y \in \mathbb{R}^n\) Observed outcome vector: Point in \(n\)-dimensional observation space
\(\hat{y} = X\hat{\beta}\) Fitted vector: Orthogonal projection of \(y\) onto the subspace \(\text{Col}(X)\)
\(e = y - \hat{y}\) Residual vector: Shortest distance vector from \(\text{Col}(X)\) to \(y\)
\(e \perp \text{Col}(X)\) Orthogonality: The residual forms a right angle with every vector in the regressor subspace

👁 WHAT YOU ARE SEEING

The shaded surface represents the subspace \(\text{Col}(X)\) formed by all linear combinations of regressors. Point \(y\) floats above it. The blue vector \(\hat{y}\) is the orthogonal projection, connected to \(y\) by the vertical red dashed line \(e\).

🔎 WHAT TO NOTICE

Notice the right-angle marker at the base of the residual line. If you picked any point other than \(\hat{y}\) on the plane, the line connecting it to \(y\) would form a hypotenuse of a right triangle, making the distance strictly longer (Pythagorean Theorem).

◆ WHY IT MATTERS

OLS does not pick an arbitrary point in \(\text{Col}(X)\). It picks the unique point that minimizes Euclidean squared distance. All algebraic formulas in econometrics are simply coordinate calculations of this single geometric fact.

📊 ECONOMETRIC INTERPRETATION

The fitted vector \(\hat{y}\) captures all information in \(y\) that can be linearly accounted for by the included regressors. The residual vector \(e = y - \hat{y}\) contains the idiosyncratic, unmodeled variation orthogonal to every regressor.

2. Common Conceptual Pitfalls

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE: "OLS projects the coefficient vector \(\beta\) onto \(X\)"

Correction: \(\beta\) is a \(k \times 1\) vector of numbers residing in parameter space \(\mathbb{R}^k\). It is never projected! OLS projects the \(n \times 1\) outcome vector \(y\) in observation space \(\mathbb{R}^n\) onto the subspace spanned by the columns of \(X\). The coefficients \(\hat{\beta}\) are simply the weights assigned to each column of \(X\) to construct the fitted projection: \(\hat{y} = \hat{\beta}_1 x_1 + \dots + \hat{\beta}_k x_k\).

✓ KEY GEOMETRIC TAKEAWAY

Once you understand that OLS is perpendicular projection onto a subspace, the algebraic derivation of the Normal Equations in Lesson 2.2 becomes completely intuitive: setting the derivative of distance to zero is simply finding the point where the residual forms a \(90^\circ\) angle with the subspace!
