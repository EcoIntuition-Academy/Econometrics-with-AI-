LESSON 2.6 Master Theorem

The Frisch–Waugh–Lovell Theorem

"Remove the old story, study what's left." Master the fundamental theorem of multiple regression: proving how partialling out control variables transforms multiple regression into a bivariate regression on orthogonalized residuals.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Articulate the FWL intuition: purging the component of both outcome and target regressor explained by controls.
  • Define the residual-maker matrix with respect to controls: \(M_1 = I - X_1(X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'\).
  • Compute residualized variables \(y^* = M_1y\) and \(X_2^* = M_1X_2\).
  • Prove that the coefficient from regressing \(y^*\) on \(X_2^*\) equals the coefficient \(\hat{\beta}_2\) from the full regression of \(y\) on \([X_1, X_2]\).
  • Interpret multiple regression coefficients as relationships within the subspace orthogonal to controls.

1. The Intuition: "Remove the Old Story, Study What's Left"

Suppose our regressors are partitioned into two groups:

$$X = \begin{bmatrix} X_1 & X_2 \end{bmatrix}$$

and the full multiple regression model is:

$$y = X_1\beta_1 + X_2\beta_2 + \varepsilon$$

We want to understand what the coefficient vector \(\beta_2\) on \(X_2\) is really doing when we control for \(X_1\).

The Two Stories About Outcome \(y\)

Conceptually, there are two stories about \(y\): a story told by \(X_1\) (the controls) and a story told by \(X_2\) (our variables of interest).
The Frisch–Waugh–Lovell (FWL) theorem says: if you want to learn the pure contribution of \(X_2\), you must:

  1. Remove the part of the story already explained by \(X_1\) from the outcome \(y\).
  2. Remove the part of the story already explained by \(X_1\) from the regressor \(X_2\).
  3. See how much of what remains in \(y\) can be explained by what remains in \(X_2\).

2. Mathematical Mechanics of Partialling Out

Define the residual-maker matrix with respect to \(X_1\):

$$M_1 = I_n - X_1(X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'$$

Now apply this operator to both the outcome and the target regressor:

  • Residualize \(y\) on \(X_1\): $$y^* = M_1y$$ This is the component of \(y\) that cannot be explained linearly by \(X_1\).
  • Residualize \(X_2\) on \(X_1\): $$X_2^* = M_1X_2$$ This is the component of \(X_2\) that is completely orthogonal to \(X_1\).
FUNDAMENTAL THEOREM The Frisch–Waugh–Lovell (FWL) Theorem
$$\hat{\beta}_2 = (X_2^{*\prime}X_2^*)^{-1}X_2^{*\prime}y^* \quad \text{where } y^* = M_1y \text{ and } X_2^* = M_1X_2$$
WHERE
\(\hat{\beta}_2\) Partial regression coefficient on \(X_2\) from the full regression of \(y\) on \([X_1, X_2]\)
\(y^* = M_1y\) The residual from projecting \(y\) onto \(\operatorname{Col}(X_1)\)
\(X_2^* = M_1X_2\) The component of \(X_2\) orthogonal to \(\operatorname{Col}(X_1)\)
\(M_1\) Residual-maker matrix for \(X_1\): \(I - X_1(X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'\)

Geometric & Econometric Meaning

Geometrically, FWL shows that the coefficient on \(X_2\) depends only on the variation in \(X_2\) that remains after removing its linear projection on \(X_1\), and the corresponding residual variation in \(y\).
Economically, this isolates the partial relationship between \(y\) and \(X_2\) after linearly controlling for \(X_1\). Note that this is a mathematical theorem that holds on any dataset; whether \(\hat{\beta}_2\) reflects a causal effect depends on whether the conditional independence assumption holds.

3. The FWL Three-Step Workflow

STEP 1
Regress \(y\) on \(X_1\) → Save Residuals \(y^* = M_1y\)
Purges outcome of all linear influence from control variables.
↓
STEP 2
Regress \(X_2\) on \(X_1\) → Save Residuals \(X_2^* = M_1X_2\)
Purges regressor of interest of any collinearity with controls.
↓
STEP 3
Regress \(y^*\) on \(X_2^*\) → Estimated Slope \(= \hat{\beta}_2\)
Mathematically identical to the coefficient on \(X_2\) from the full regression \(y \sim [X_1, X_2]\)!

4. Common Misconceptions & Academic Precision

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE: "FWL means we simply delete the controls from the dataset"

Correction: Deleting \(X_1\) gives the omitted variable bias of Stage 1! FWL requires removing the component explained by \(X_1\) from both the outcome \(y\) and the regressor \(X_2\). You only regress the residuals on each other.

⚠️ ACADEMIC CAUTION: Do not automatically call \(\hat{\beta}_2\) a causal effect

FWL is an algebraic theorem that holds for any sample data, regardless of whether the model is causally identified. \(\hat{\beta}_2\) is the partial regression coefficient of \(y\) on \(X_2\) after controlling for \(X_1\). It only reflects a causal effect if the omitted variables in the error term satisfy conditional independence / exogeneity.
