LESSON 2.4 Regression Mechanics

Residual Orthogonality & the Constant Term

Unpack the core algebraic condition \(X'e = 0\) and discover why sample residuals necessarily sum to zero (\(\sum e_i = 0\)) when—and only when—an intercept is included in the model.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Derive the residual orthogonality condition \(X'e = 0\) directly from the normal equations.
  • Interpret \(X'e = 0\) as column-by-column orthogonality: each regressor column has zero inner product with \(e\).
  • Specialize the orthogonality condition to an intercept vector of ones \(\iota\), proving \(\sum_{i=1}^n e_i = 0\).
  • Understand signed residual balancing: why positive and negative deviations cancel around the regression line.
  • Avoid the common fallacy that residuals always sum to zero in models without an intercept.

1. What the Residual Conditions Really Say

Recall the normal equations derived in Lesson 2.2:

$$X'X\hat{\beta} = X'y \implies X'(y - X\hat{\beta}) = \mathbf{0}$$

Because the sample residual vector is defined as \(e = y - X\hat{\beta}\), the normal equations can be written in the fundamental orthogonality form:

ORTHOGONALITY CONDITION Residual Orthogonality to Regressor Columns
$$X'e = \mathbf{0} \iff x_j'e = 0 \quad \text{for all } j=1,\dots,k$$
WHERE
\(X'e = \mathbf{0}\) The residual vector is orthogonal to every column of \(X\).
\(x_j'e = 0\) The residual has zero sample inner product with the \(j\)-th regressor column.

Because \(e\) is orthogonal to every individual column of \(X\), it is necessarily orthogonal to every linear combination of those columns—and therefore strictly perpendicular to the entire regression subspace \(\operatorname{Col}(X)\). This is the exact algebraic counterpart of the closest-shadow projection picture.

2. Sum of Residuals as a Special Case: The Intercept

Now suppose the regression model includes an intercept constant term. In matrix notation, this means the first column of \(X\) is an \(n \times 1\) vector of ones, denoted \(\iota\):

$$X = \begin{bmatrix} \iota & X_2 \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{where } \iota = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ \vdots \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Because the first column of \(X\) is the vector of ones \(\iota\), the corresponding normal-equation condition is:

$$\iota'e = 0$$

The inner product of \(\iota\) with \(e\) is simply the sum of all elements in \(e\):

SPECIAL CASE · WITH AN INTERCEPT Residuals Sum to Zero
$$\iota'e = 0 \implies \sum_{i=1}^n e_i = 0 \iff \bar{e} = 0$$
WHERE
\(\sum e_i = 0\) When an intercept is included, positive and negative residuals balance to zero.
\(\bar{e} = 0\) The arithmetic sample mean of the OLS residuals is identically zero.

The residuals may be positive for some observations and negative for others, but on average they cancel exactly.

In a 2D scatter plot, this means signed residuals balance to zero: the total vertical distance above the line balances with the total vertical distance below the line when a constant is present.

Many introductory textbooks casually state "residuals sum to zero" as a universal law of OLS. It is NOT universal: it holds only when a constant term is included!

The intercept parameter \(\hat{\beta}_1\) acts as an elevator: it moves the entire regression line vertically up or down until the average residual is precisely zero.

3. Common Conceptual Pitfalls

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE: "X'e = 0 means every residual equals zero"

Correction: \(X'e = 0\) is an inner product statement, not an element-wise equality. It means the \(n \times 1\) vector \(e\) is perpendicular to each of the \(k\) column vectors of \(X\). Individual sample residuals \(e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i\) are almost always non-zero in real data!

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE: "Residuals always sum to zero in any regression"

Correction: If a researcher estimates a regression without an intercept (e.g. \(y_i = \beta_2 x_{i2} + e_i\)), \(\iota \notin \text{Col}(X)\). Consequently, \(\iota'e \neq 0\), residuals do not sum to zero, and the sample \(R^2\) can even become negative!
