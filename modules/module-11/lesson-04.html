LESSON 11.4 Simultaneity & Price IV

Simultaneity: Supply, Demand & Instrumenting Price

Formulate simultaneous market equilibrium, prove mathematically why price correlates with demand disturbances, solve for reduced-form equations, and use production cost shifters as instruments.

🎯

Learning Objectives

  • Formulate the structural supply and demand system with price and quantity simultaneously determined.
  • Understand why empirical market data forms a “cloud” of equilibrium points that naive OLS cannot disentangle.
  • Derive the structural market-clearing price equation and show that price contains the demand disturbance \(\varepsilon\).
  • Calculate the reduced-form equation for quantity in terms of the cost instrument \(z\).
  • Understand how an exogenous supply shifter \(z\) traces out the downward-sloping demand curve.
  • Interact with the Supply–Demand Simultaneity Explorer to visualize equilibrium shifts.
01
Structural System

The Supply and Demand Equations

The classic econometric problem of simultaneity arises when prices and quantities are determined at the exact same moment by the intersection of consumer demand and producer supply.

STRUCTURAL DEMAND

\(\beta_1 \le 0\)
\[Q_D = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot \text{price} + \varepsilon\]

Consumers purchase less as price rises (\(\beta_1 \le 0\)). The error \(\varepsilon\) represents unobserved consumer tastes, preferences, and aggregate demand shocks.

STRUCTURAL SUPPLY

\(\gamma_1 \ge 0\)
\[Q_S = \gamma_0 + \gamma_1 \cdot \text{price} + \gamma_2 z + \nu\]

Firms produce more as price rises (\(\gamma_1 \ge 0\)). Variable \(z\) is the per-unit cost of production, and \(\nu\) captures supply-side productivity disturbances.

The Instrument: Production Cost Shifter \(z\)

By economic assumption, per-unit input cost \(z\) is determined in factor markets independently of retail consumer tastes (\(\text{Cov}(z, \varepsilon) = 0\)) and firm-specific productivity shocks (\(\text{Cov}(z, \nu) = 0\)).

02
Identification Failure

The Equilibrium Cloud & OLS Breakdown

In a competitive market, observed transactions occur only where demand equals supply:

\[Q_D = Q_S\]

Every observed data point \((Q_E, P_E)\) is an equilibrium intersection. As both consumer preferences (\(\varepsilon\)) and producer shocks (\(\nu, z\)) fluctuate across time or markets, the intersection shifts in multiple directions simultaneously.

Why Naive OLS Cannot Recover Demand

A naive regression of observed quantity on observed price fits a line through this “cloud” of intersection points. Because both curves move at once, the estimated slope could be positive, negative, or completely flat — reflecting an arbitrary mixture of supply and demand schedules rather than the true causal demand response \(\beta_1\).

03
Mathematical Proof

Derivation Journey: Solving for Price Under Simultaneity

DERIVATION GOAL

Prove that Equilibrium Price Contains the Demand Disturbance \(\varepsilon\)

Assume \(\beta_0 = \gamma_0 = 0\) without loss of generality (by demeaning all variables around their expectations).

STEP 1: IMPOSE MARKET CLEARING

Equate structural demand to structural supply:

\[\beta_1 \cdot \text{price} + \varepsilon = \gamma_1 \cdot \text{price} + \gamma_2 z + \nu\]
STEP 2: COLLECT PRICE TERMS

Group all terms involving price on the left-hand side:

\[(\beta_1 - \gamma_1) \cdot \text{price} = \gamma_2 z + \nu - \varepsilon\]
STEP 3: SOLVE FOR REDUCED-FORM PRICE

Divide by \((\beta_1 - \gamma_1)\) (noting \(\beta_1 - \gamma_1 < 0\)):

\[\text{price} = \frac{\gamma_2}{\beta_1 - \gamma_1} z + \frac{\nu - \varepsilon}{\beta_1 - \gamma_1}\]
KEY INSIGHT: MATHEMATICAL PROOF OF ENDOGENEITY

Look closely at the numerator: the demand disturbance \(\varepsilon\) appears directly in the price equation with coefficient \(-\frac{1}{\beta_1 - \gamma_1} > 0\).

\[\text{Cov}(\text{price}, \varepsilon) = -\frac{\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{\beta_1 - \gamma_1} > 0 \ne 0\]

Because price covaries positively with demand shocks, naive OLS estimates of the demand slope \(\beta_1\) are upward-biased (less negative than true demand).

04
System Completion

The Reduced Form for Quantity

We now substitute the solved reduced-form price into the structural demand equation to obtain the reduced form for quantity:

\[\begin{aligned} Q_D &= \beta_1 \cdot \text{price} + \varepsilon \\ &= \beta_1 \left[ \frac{\gamma_2}{\beta_1 - \gamma_1} z + \frac{\nu - \varepsilon}{\beta_1 - \gamma_1} \right] + \varepsilon \\ &= \frac{\beta_1\gamma_2}{\beta_1 - \gamma_1} z + \frac{\beta_1\nu}{\beta_1 - \gamma_1} - \frac{\beta_1\varepsilon}{\beta_1 - \gamma_1} + \frac{(\beta_1 - \gamma_1)\varepsilon}{\beta_1 - \gamma_1} \\ &= \frac{\beta_1\gamma_2}{\beta_1 - \gamma_1} z + \frac{\beta_1\nu - \gamma_1\varepsilon}{\beta_1 - \gamma_1} \end{aligned}\]

Why Cost Shifter \(z\) Works as an Instrument

Notice that instrument \(z\) shifts supply without entering the demand disturbance \(\varepsilon\). In the first stage, \(z\) predicts price through coefficient \(\frac{\gamma_2}{\beta_1 - \gamma_1}\). In the second stage, taking the ratio of the reduced-form quantity coefficient to the reduced-form price coefficient recovers \(\beta_1\) exactly:

\[\frac{\frac{\beta_1\gamma_2}{\beta_1 - \gamma_1}}{\frac{\gamma_2}{\beta_1 - \gamma_1}} = \beta_1\]

COST SHIFTER TRACES OUT DEMAND

When the cost instrument z moves from Low to High while demand shocks are held constant, the supply curve translates along the demand curve. The resulting equilibrium points trace the true downward-sloping demand curve.

WHAT YOU ARE SEEING

Market supply and demand intersection with an underlying historical cloud of equilibrium outcomes.

WHAT TO NOTICE

Changing ε moves demand right/left. Both P and Q rise together, confusing naive OLS with an upward slope.

HOW IV IDENTIFIES

By holding ε orthogonal and shifting only supply via z, the price movements isolate the true causal demand response.

💡

QUICK CHECK

Why is price endogenous in the demand equation under simultaneous market equilibrium?

HINT: Examine the solved reduced-form equation for price from the market-clearing condition \(Q_D = Q_S\).

ANSWER: Because equilibrium price depends partly on the demand disturbance term \(\varepsilon\).

WHY? Setting \(Q_D = Q_S\) and solving for price yields \(\text{price} = \frac{\gamma_2}{\beta_1 - \gamma_1} z + \frac{\nu - \varepsilon}{\beta_1 - \gamma_1}\). Because the demand shock \(\varepsilon\) enters directly into the determination of price, \(\text{Cov}(\text{price}, \varepsilon) \ne 0\). This violates the OLS exogeneity assumption and creates simultaneity bias.

📋

What You Should Now Understand

  • In simultaneous market equilibrium, observed data represent intersections of supply and demand schedules rather than single curves.
  • Solving \(Q_D = Q_S\) reveals that market price contains the demand error \(\varepsilon\), proving that price is endogenous in naive demand regressions.
  • The reduced-form equations express endogenous price and quantity solely as functions of exogenous instruments and disturbances.
  • An exogenous supply cost shifter \(z\) shifts the supply curve along the demand curve, cleanly identifying the causal demand parameter \(\beta_1\).
