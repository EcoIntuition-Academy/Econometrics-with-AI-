LESSON 11.3 Two-Stage Intuition

Simple IV Examples & Two-Stage Intuition

Study classical empirical illustrations of instrumental variables—incarceration and crime, education and wages—and step through the mechanics of two-stage estimation.

🎯

Learning Objectives

  • Analyze the reverse-causality problem in estimating the effect of incarceration on crime rates.
  • Specify the two-stage model using prison overcrowding legal challenges as an instrument for incarceration.
  • Analyze omitted-variable bias in returns to schooling and formulate the college-distance instrument.
  • Trace the two-stage procedure: first-stage prediction (\(\hat{X}\)) and second-stage causal regression.
  • Recognize the critical software warning: why manual second-stage OLS standard errors are mathematically invalid.
  • Experiment with the interactive Two-Stage IV Builder lab.
01
Case Study 1

The Effect of Incarceration on Crime

Consider the structural relationship between incarceration rates and crime rates across jurisdictions:

\[\text{crime} = \beta_1 + \beta_2 \cdot \text{incarceration rate} + \varepsilon\]

Policymakers want to estimate \(\beta_2\), the causal deterrence and incapacitation effect of incarceration. If incarceration deters crime, we expect \(\beta_2 < 0\).

The Endogeneity Problem: Reverse Causality

While higher incarceration may reduce crime, higher crime rates also lead to more arrests, convictions, and higher incarceration rates. Because crime and incarceration determine each other simultaneously, \(\text{incarceration rate}\) correlates with the error term \(\varepsilon\), rendering OLS biased and inconsistent.

The Source Instrument: Prison Overcrowding Litigation

To break this feedback loop, researchers need exogenous variation in incarceration that is driven by something unrelated to prevailing local crime surges. The lecture notes cite:

Instrument \(Z\): Legal challenges to prison overcrowding that force inmate releases.

A successful court injunction forces a state to release inmates or cap prison populations for constitutional reasons, reducing incarceration independently of whether crime was rising or falling at that moment.

FIRST STAGE
\[\text{incarceration} = \pi_1 + \pi_2 Z + \nu\]

Regress endogenous incarceration on litigation instrument \(Z\) to extract the predicted fitted values \(\widehat{\text{incarceration}}\).

SECOND STAGE
\[\text{crime} = \beta_1 + \beta_2 \widehat{\text{incarceration}} + \varepsilon\]

Regress crime on the instrument-predicted incarceration rate to recover the consistent causal parameter \(\beta_2\).

02
Case Study 2

The Effect of Education on Wages

A second foundational application examines the economic return to higher education:

\[\text{wages} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot \text{college attendance} + \varepsilon\]

Here, \(\beta_1\) measures the causal wage premium from college attendance (evaluated later on in adult working life).

The Endogeneity Problem: Omitted Ability & Background

Individuals choose whether to attend college. Those who attend may possess higher unobserved cognitive ability, ambition, or privileged family networks. Because these factors directly increase adult earnings (\(\varepsilon\)), \(\text{Cov}(\text{college}, \varepsilon) > 0\). Naive OLS estimates conflate the causal return with omitted ability bias.

The Source Instrument: Geographic Distance to College

The lecture notes highlight Card’s celebrated instrument:

Instrument \(Z\): Distance to the nearest college during senior year of high school.

Living near a college lowers the cost of attendance (enabling commuting and reducing living expenses), increasing the likelihood of enrollment (\(\text{Cov}(Z, X) \ne 0\)). However, high school geographic proximity is plausibly uncorrelated with innate adult labor-market earning ability (\(\text{Cov}(Z, \varepsilon) = 0\)).

FIRST STAGE
\[\text{college} = \pi_0 + \pi_1 \cdot \text{distance} + \nu\]

Predict college attendance from geographic accessibility to obtain clean fitted values \(\widehat{\text{college}}\).

SECOND STAGE
\[\text{wages} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \widehat{\text{college}} + \varepsilon\]

Estimate the structural causal return to education \(\beta_1\) purged of unobserved ability confounding.

Pedagogical Perspective:

The lecture notes present these examples as pedagogical illustrations of the IV framework. In real empirical practice, researchers rigorously evaluate potential threats to validity (e.g., whether wealthier families choose to live closer to prestigious universities).

03
Procedure & Inference

Two-Stage Flow & Standard Error Warning

Conceptually, instrumental variables can be envisioned as a sequential two-stage regression procedure:

INSTRUMENT
Exogenous Z
→
FIRST STAGE
Regress X on Z
→
FITTED VALUES
Predicted X̂
→
SECOND STAGE
Regress y on X̂
→
ESTIMATOR
β̂_IV / 2SLS

⚠️ CRITICAL INFERENCE WARNING: DO NOT USE SECOND-STAGE OLS STANDARD ERRORS

The lecture notes emphasize an essential computational principle: standard errors must come from dedicated IV/2SLS estimation routines, never from manual OLS on fitted values \(\hat{X}\).

Why is manual OLS inference wrong? When you manually regress \(y\) on \(\hat{X}\), standard regression software treats \(\hat{X}\) as fixed, error-free data. In reality, \(\hat{X}\) is an estimated generated regressor carrying first-stage estimation uncertainty. Furthermore, the second-stage OLS residuals are \(y - \hat{X}\hat{\beta}\), whereas the true structural disturbances are \(y - X\hat{\beta}\). Proper IV routines automatically adjust for this difference.

WHAT TO NOTICE

Fitted values X̂ eliminate the horizontal spread associated with endogenous error shocks.

WHY IT MATTERS

The slope through X̂ reflects only the exogenous variation transferred from instrument Z.

💡

QUICK CHECK

What is the core purpose of running the first-stage regression in an IV analysis?

HINT: Think about what part of regressor \(X\) is kept and what part is purged when regressing on instrument \(Z\).

ANSWER: To isolate the exogenous variation in \(X\) that is systematically predicted by instrument \(Z\).

WHY? By regressing \(X\) on \(Z\), the first stage decomposes the endogenous regressor into fitted values \(\hat{X}\) (which are orthogonal to the structural error \(\varepsilon\)) and first-stage residuals \(\nu\) (which absorb the endogenous correlation). The second stage uses only \(\hat{X}\) to achieve consistent estimation.

📋

What You Should Now Understand

  • Reverse causality biases naive OLS regressions of crime on incarceration; prison overcrowding litigation provides an exogenous shifter.
  • Omitted ability biases naive regressions of wages on schooling; geographic distance to college in senior year serves as a cost shifter.
  • The first stage regresses the endogenous regressor on the instrument to extract predicted values \(\hat{X}\).
  • The second stage regresses the outcome on \(\hat{X}\) to uncover the unconfounded structural causal parameter.
  • Never compute standard errors from manual second-stage OLS regressions; proper IV/2SLS software is mandatory to account for generated regressor variance.
