LESSON 11.2 Instrument Criteria

Instrumental Variables & the Three Instrument Criteria

Discover how an instrumental variable isolates clean, exogenous variation from an endogenous regressor, and master the three core criteria: validity, relevance, and monotonicity.

🎯

Learning Objectives

  • Define an instrumental variable \(Z\) and understand how it isolates exogenous variation in an endogenous regressor \(X\).
  • Interpret the IV Ballantine diagram, distinguishing the valid purple variation from the red endogenous overlap.
  • Master Criterion 1 (Exogeneity / Validity) and formulate the exclusion restriction: \(Z\) affects \(y\) only through \(X\).
  • Master Criterion 2 (Strength / Relevance) and understand why \(Z\) must be a statistically significant predictor of \(X\).
  • Master Criterion 3 (Monotonicity) and see how ruling out “defiers” enables causal interpretation in heterogeneous treatment settings.
  • Evaluate candidate instruments using the interactive quality diagnostic lab.
01
Core Concept

The Idea Behind Instrumental Variables

When an explanatory variable \(X\) is correlated with the regression error \(\varepsilon\), OLS conflates the causal impact of \(X\) with unobserved confounding. Instrumental variables solve this challenge by finding an external variable \(Z\) that acts as a “lever” on \(X\).

DEFINITION: INSTRUMENTAL VARIABLE (IV)

An Instrumental Variable \(Z\) is an observable variable that satisfies two fundamental properties:

  • Relevance: \(Z\) predicts variation in the endogenous regressor \(X\) (\(\text{Cov}(Z, X) \ne 0\)).
  • Exogeneity: \(Z\) is uncorrelated with the unobserved error term \(\varepsilon\) (\(\mathbb{E}[Z\varepsilon] = 0\)).

THE CENTRAL TAKEAWAY OF INSTRUMENTAL VARIABLES

Instrumental variables replace all variation in \(X\) with only the exogenous variation in \(X\) induced by a valid instrument \(Z\).

Because the instrument-induced variation in \(X\) is orthogonal to the disturbance \(\varepsilon\), the resulting estimator is consistent.

Efficiency Trade-off: Why IV Standard Errors Are Larger

The exogenous variation isolated by \(Z\) represents only a subset of the total variance in \(X\). Because the IV estimator discards all endogenous variation and relies on a smaller slice of information, IV estimators typically have larger standard errors than OLS. We accept higher variance to eliminate asymptotic bias.

02
Geometric Intuition

The IV Ballantine Diagram

The lecture notes extend the Ballantine diagram by introducing instrument circle \(Z\) alongside \(X\), \(y\), and disturbance \(\varepsilon\):

Purple Area (Valid IV Variation):

The variation in \(Z\) that overlaps with \(X\) but has zero area in common with the red or yellow error. This is the clean, uncorrupted variation used to identify \(\beta\).

Red Area (Endogenous Variation):

The overlap between \(X\) and \(\varepsilon\). IV estimation completely purges this component by isolating only the variation driven by \(Z\).

Critical Boundary Note from Lecture:

“Z should have no area in common with the red or yellow areas (error). If Z explains y without going through X, it is correlated with \(\varepsilon\) — bad IV.”

03
Formal Requirements

The Three Criteria for Instruments

For a candidate variable \(Z\) to be a valid econometric instrument, it must satisfy three rigorous conditions:

1. Exogeneity / Validity (The Exclusion Restriction)

\(\mathbb{E}[Z\varepsilon] = 0\)

\(Z\) must affect outcome \(y\) only through \(X\) and not directly. Equivalently, \(Z\) must be uncorrelated with the disturbance term \(\varepsilon\) in the structural outcome equation. If \(Z\) exerted any direct effect on \(y\), it would belong in the equation as an omitted regressor, making \(\text{Cov}(Z, \varepsilon) \ne 0\).

2. Strength / Relevance

\(\text{Cov}(Z, X) \ne 0\)

\(Z\) must be a strong predictor of the endogenous regressor \(X\). It does not need to be a perfect predictor (which would be impossible if \(X\) is endogenous), but it must be statistically significant. A weak instrument generates unstable second-stage estimates and massive standard errors.

3. Monotonicity

No Defiers

For all individuals in the sample, an increase in \(Z\) must affect \(X\) in the same direction (weakly). This condition rules out “defiers” — individuals who deliberately move their behavior in the opposite direction of the instrument.

Structural Causal Diagram

Monotonicity in This Course

The lecture notes connect this assumption to Local Average Treatment Effect (LATE) frameworks. For example, if \(X\) is college attendance and \(Z\) is geographic distance to the nearest college, living closer should always weakly increase (or leave unchanged) the probability of attending college.

WHAT YOU ARE EVALUATING

The interplay among relevance, direct effects on outcome \(y\), and confounding correlation with error \(\varepsilon\).

WHAT TO NOTICE

Violating either the exclusion restriction or error orthogonality destroys validity immediately, regardless of strength.

ECONOMETRIC LIMITATION

Relevance can be tested statistically via the first stage; validity depends primarily on theoretical defense and institutional design.

💡

QUICK CHECK

Why is a variable that strongly predicts \(X\) but directly affects \(y\) not a valid instrument?

HINT: Review the definition of the exclusion restriction and where a direct determinant of \(y\) belongs in a regression equation.

ANSWER: Because it violates the exclusion restriction / validity condition.

WHY? An instrument must affect \(y\) exclusively through its influence on \(X\). If \(Z\) exerts an independent direct effect on \(y\), it belongs directly in the structural outcome model. Excluding \(Z\) forces its direct effect into the residual disturbance \(\varepsilon\), causing \(\text{Cov}(Z, \varepsilon) \ne 0\).

📋

What You Should Now Understand

  • An instrumental variable \(Z\) isolates variation in \(X\) that is completely uncorrelated with the unobserved error \(\varepsilon\).
  • In Ballantine geometry, the purple intersection between \(Z\) and \(X\) represents clean, valid identifying variation.
  • Validity (exclusion restriction) requires that \(Z\) affects \(y\) solely through \(X\), so that \(\mathbb{E}[Z\varepsilon] = 0\).
  • Relevance requires that \(Z\) strongly predicts \(X\); weak instruments lead to inflated variance and unreliable inference.
  • Monotonicity ensures that the instrument moves all individuals in the same direction, ruling out defiers in causal interpretation.
