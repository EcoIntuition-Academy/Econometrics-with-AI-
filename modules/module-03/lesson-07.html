LESSON 3.7 Capstone & Asymptotic Foundations

Normality, t Statistics, CLTs & Module Review

Distinguish exact finite-sample inference under normal disturbances from large-sample Central Limit Theorem asymptotics, explore the transition from the theoretical \(z\) to the feasible \(t_{n - k}\) statistic, examine the Lindeberg–Lévy and Lindeberg–Feller theorems, and review the unified curriculum of Module 3.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State the exact finite-sample distribution of OLS under normal errors: \(\hat{\beta} \mid X \sim \mathcal{N}(\beta, \sigma^2(X'X)^{-1})\).
  • Distinguish the theoretical \(z_k\) statistic (using true \(\sigma^2\)) from the feasible \(t_k\) statistic (using \(s^2\)) with \(n - k\) degrees of freedom.
  • Separate exact normal distribution theory from large-sample Central Limit Theorem (CLT) motivations.
  • State and contrast the Lindeberg–Lévy CLT (i.i.d.) and the Lindeberg–Feller CLT (independent with unequal variances).
  • Review all 15 foundational concepts of Module 3 using the integrated knowledge map and standardized review cards.

1. Part A: Exact Finite-Sample Normal Theory

What is this section about? When disturbances are assumed to be multivariate normal conditional on \(X\), the linearity of OLS guarantees that the estimator vector is exactly normally distributed in finite samples of any size.

🎯 Classical Normal Linear Regression Model (CNLRM)

Assume the unobserved disturbances follow a conditional multivariate normal distribution: $$\varepsilon \mid X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \sigma^2 I_n)$$ Because \(\hat{\beta} = \beta + (X'X)^{-1}X'\varepsilon\) is an affine linear transformation of the Gaussian vector \(\varepsilon\), the OLS estimator is exactly normally distributed: $$\hat{\beta} \mid X \sim \mathcal{N}\left(\beta, \sigma^2(X'X)^{-1}\right)$$

For any single regression coefficient \(\hat{\beta}_k\) (the \(k\)-th coordinate of \(\hat{\beta}\)): \[ \hat{\beta}_k \mid X \sim \mathcal{N}\left(\beta_k, \sigma^2 s^{kk}\right) \] where \(s^{kk} = [(X'X)^{-1}]_{kk}\) denotes the \(k\)-th diagonal element of \((X'X)^{-1}\).

THEORETICAL \(z\)-STATISTIC (KNOWN \(\sigma^2\))

If the disturbance variance \(\sigma^2\) were known, standardizing the estimation error produces a standard Gaussian:

$$z_k = \frac{\hat{\beta}_k - \beta_k}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 s^{kk}}} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$$

Infeasible in practice because true population disturbance variance \(\sigma^2\) is never known.

FEASIBLE \(t\)-STATISTIC (ESTIMATED \(s^2\))

Replacing \(\sigma^2\) with the unbiased sample estimator \(s^2 = \frac{e'e}{n - k}\) yields a Student's \(t\) ratio:

$$t_k = \frac{\hat{\beta}_k - \beta_k}{\sqrt{s^2 s^{kk}}} \sim t_{n - k}$$

Has exact Student's \(t\) distribution with \(n - k\) degrees of freedom under normal errors.

KEY EQUATION Finite-Sample Student's t Statistic
$$t_k = \frac{\hat{\beta}_k - \beta_k}{\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\beta}_k)} = \frac{\hat{\beta}_k - \beta_k}{\sqrt{s^2 [(X'X)^{-1}]_{kk}}} \sim t_{n - k}$$
WHERE
\(\hat{\beta}_k - \beta_k\)
Estimation Error: Difference between sample OLS estimate and true hypothesized parameter.
\(\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\beta}_k) = \sqrt{s^2 s^{kk}}\)
Estimated Standard Error: Sample uncertainty measured in identical coefficient units.
\(n - k\)
Degrees of Freedom: Inherited directly from the denominator of \(s^2 = \frac{e'e}{n - k}\).
Heavy Tails
Sampling Dispersion: Student's \(t\) has wider tails than normal to account for estimation uncertainty in \(s^2\).

3. Part B: Large-Sample Central Limit Motivation

What if the true disturbances \(\varepsilon_i\) are not normally distributed? Econometrics relies on Central Limit Theorems (CLTs) to motivate why properly normalized sums and estimators converge approximately to Gaussian distributions in large samples, even when the underlying data are non-normal.

⚠️ CRITICAL DISTINCTION: EXACT FINITE-SAMPLE THEORY VS ASYMPTOTIC CLT

Do NOT confuse the two results! Exact \(t_{n - k}\) distribution requires the assumption of conditional Gaussian disturbances \(\varepsilon \mid X \sim \mathcal{N}\). The Central Limit Theorem does not prove an exact \(t\) distribution in finite samples; rather, CLTs show that as \(n \to \infty\), properly scaled estimators become asymptotically normal under general error distributions.

THEOREM Lindeberg–Lévy Central Limit Theorem (i.i.d.)
Assumptions: Let \(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n\) be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables with mean \(\mathbb{E}[x_i] = \mu\) and finite variance \(\operatorname{Var}(x_i) = \sigma^2 < \infty\).
$$\sqrt{n}\left(\bar{x}_n - \mu\right) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}\left(0, \sigma^2\right)$$
WHERE
\(\bar{x}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i\)
Sample Mean: Simple arithmetic average of \(n\) i.i.d. observations.
\(\sqrt{n}\)
Asymptotic Rate: Standard normalization factor preventing the variance from collapsing to zero.
\(\xrightarrow{d}\)
Convergence in Distribution: The cumulative distribution function converges pointwise to the standard Gaussian CDF.
\(\sigma^2 < \infty\)
Finite Variance: The essential regularity condition ensuring no infinite-variance tails.
THEOREM Lindeberg–Feller Central Limit Theorem (Unequal Variances)
Setup: Let \(x_1, \dots, x_n\) be independent (not necessarily identically distributed) with \(\mathbb{E}[x_i] = \mu_i\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(x_i) = \sigma_i^2\).
Definitions: Average mean \(\bar{\mu}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \mu_i\) and average variance \(\bar{\sigma}_n^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \sigma_i^2\).
Lindeberg Condition: \(\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{\max_{1 \le i \le n} \sigma_i}{n \bar{\sigma}_n} = 0\). (Ensures no single observation dominates the sum).
$$\sqrt{n}\left(\bar{x}_n - \bar{\mu}_n\right) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}\left(0, \bar{\sigma}^2\right)$$
WHERE
\(\bar{\sigma}^2 = \lim_{n \to \infty} \bar{\sigma}_n^2\)
Limiting Average Variance: The asymptotic limit of the average individual variances.
Heteroskedastic Settings
Why This Matters: Permits observations to have non-identical variances, accommodating realistic empirical microdata.
\(\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{\max(\sigma_i)}{n \bar{\sigma}_n} = 0\)
Regularity Condition: Guarantees the maximum individual variance is asymptotically negligible compared to the total variance.
Econometric Application
Large-Sample Inference: Forms the mathematical bridge justifying asymptotic \(t\) and \(F\) tests in large cross-sections.
LINDEBERG–LÉVY CLT
  • Core Assumption: Strictly identical distribution (i.i.d.) across all observations.
  • Structure: Common mean \(\mu\) and common variance \(\sigma^2 < \infty\).
  • Intuition: Repeated random sampling from a single homogeneous population.
LINDEBERG–FELLER CLT
  • Core Assumption: Independence without requiring identical distributions.
  • Structure: Observation-specific means \(\mu_i\) and variances \(\sigma_i^2\).
  • Intuition: Adds a condition ensuring no single observation dominates the aggregate sum.

5. Module 3 Review Cards

These 15 focused review study cards synthesize the core definitions, formulas, and theorems from Econometrics (3).pdf:

1. Goodness of Fit

Measures how well variation in regressors \(X\) accounts for variation in observed outcomes \(y\) around their sample mean.

2. SST (Total Sum of Squares)

\(\sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2 = y'M_0 y\). Total sample variation of \(y\) around its mean.

3. SSR (Regression Sum of Squares)

\(\sum (\hat{y}_i - \bar{y})^2 = \hat{\beta}'X'M_0 X \hat{\beta}\). Variation explained by the fitted regression.

4. SSE (Sum of Squared Residuals)

\(\sum e_i^2 = e'e\). Unexplained variation minimized by the OLS projection.

5. R² (Coefficient of Determination)

\(R^2 = 1 - \frac{\text{SSE}}{\text{SST}} = \frac{\text{SSR}}{\text{SST}}\). High \(R^2\) measures in-sample fit, NOT causality.

6. Adjusted R̄²

\(\bar{R}^2 = 1 - \frac{\text{SSE}}{\text{SST}}\left(\frac{n-1}{n-K}\right)\). Penalizes model expansion by degrees of freedom.

7. F-Test via R²

\(F = \frac{(R^2 - R_r^2)/J}{(1 - R^2)/(n - k)}\). Evaluates joint significance of \(J\) linear restrictions.

8. OLS Unbiasedness

\(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta} \mid X] = \beta\). Guaranteed by strict exogeneity \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid X] = \mathbf{0}\).

9. Omitted Variable Bias

\(\text{Bias} = (X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'X_2\beta_2\). Nonzero if \(\beta_2 \neq \mathbf{0}\) and \(X_1'X_2 \neq \mathbf{0}\).

10. OLS Variance Matrix

\(\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\beta} \mid X) = \sigma^2(X'X)^{-1}\). Requires spherical disturbance assumption \(\sigma^2 I_n\).

11. Gauss–Markov & BLUE

\(\operatorname{Var}(\tilde{\beta}) = \operatorname{Var}(\hat{\beta}) + \sigma^2 DD'\). OLS has minimum variance among all linear unbiased rules.

12. Estimated Error Variance (s²)

\(s^2 = \frac{e'e}{n-k}\). Corrects for the \(k\) degrees of freedom consumed by the OLS normal equations.

13. Student's t Statistic

\(t_k = \frac{\hat{\beta}_k - \beta_k}{\sqrt{s^2 s^{kk}}} \sim t_{n-k}\). Replaces true \(\sigma^2\) with sample estimate \(s^2\).

14. Lindeberg–Lévy CLT

\(\sqrt{n}(\bar{x}_n - \mu) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\). Establishes asymptotic normality for i.i.d. sums.

15. Lindeberg–Feller CLT

\(\sqrt{n}(\bar{x}_n - \bar{\mu}_n) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, \bar{\sigma}^2)\). Extends asymptotic normality to unequal variances.

🧠

Can You Explain It? Why Standardize by Standard Error?

Why do we divide the raw estimation error \(\hat{\beta}_k - \beta_k\) by its estimated standard error \(\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\beta}_k)\) when forming the test statistic? What would happen if we used the raw difference directly?

Show Conceptual Explanation
Answer:
  1. Scale Invariance: The raw difference \(\hat{\beta}_k - \beta_k\) depends entirely on the arbitrary units in which \(y\) and \(x_k\) are measured (e.g., dollars vs cents). Dividing by \(\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\beta}_k)\) cancels the measurement units, creating a unitless, standardized ratio.
  2. Pivotal Quantity: Under the null hypothesis, the distribution of the ratio \(t_k = \frac{\hat{\beta}_k - \beta_{k,0}}{\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\beta}_k)}\) depends only on the degrees of freedom \(n - k\), not on the unknown variance \(\sigma^2\) or regressor scaling. This "pivotal" property allows us to tabulate universal critical values (such as \(\pm 1.96\) or \(\pm 2.04\)) to conduct exact hypothesis testing.
📖 WHAT YOU SHOULD NOW UNDERSTAND (LESSON 3.7 & MODULE 3 REVISION)
  • Exact Normal Inference: Under Gaussian disturbances \(\varepsilon \mid X \sim \mathcal{N}\), OLS is exactly normally distributed in finite samples: \(\hat{\beta} \mid X \sim \mathcal{N}(\beta, \sigma^2(X'X)^{-1})\).
  • z vs t Distinction: The \(z\)-statistic requires known disturbance variance \(\sigma^2\). The feasible test replaces \(\sigma^2\) with \(s^2\), creating a Student's \(t_{n - k}\) statistic whose heavier tails reflect uncertainty in estimating \(\sigma^2\).
  • Asymptotics vs Finite Samples: Central Limit Theorems explain large-sample normality without requiring normal errors. A CLT does not provide an exact finite-sample \(t\) distribution.
  • Lindeberg–Lévy vs Lindeberg–Feller: Lindeberg–Lévy applies to i.i.d. observations with finite variance; Lindeberg–Feller extends normality to independent observations with unequal variances under a condition ensuring no single observation dominates.
  • Unified Module Progression: In-sample fit (\(R^2\)) \(\to\) Model Complexity (\(\bar{R}^2\)) \(\to\) Restrictions (\(F\)) \(\to\) Unbiasedness \(\to\) OVB \(\to\) Precision \(\to\) BLUE \(\to\) Unknown \(\sigma^2\) (\(s^2\)) \(\to\) Exact Inference \(\to\) Asymptotic Normality.
