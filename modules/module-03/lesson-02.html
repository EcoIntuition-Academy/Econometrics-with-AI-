LESSON 3.2 Model Diagnostics

Adjusted R-Squared & Model Comparison

Explore why ordinary \(R^2\) mechanically rewards the addition of uninformative variables, see how adjusted \(\bar{R}^2\) penalizes loss of degrees of freedom, and master the rules for comparing competing specifications.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain geometrically why adding regressors can never decrease ordinary \(R^2\).
  • State and compute the adjusted \(R^2\) formula: \(\bar{R}^2 = 1 - \frac{\text{SSE}}{\text{SST}}\left(\frac{n-1}{n-K}\right)\).
  • Analyze how the degrees-of-freedom penalty can cause \(\bar{R}^2\) to fall when adding useless regressors.
  • Apply the model comparison rule: models must have identical dependent variables \(y\).
  • Identify why comparing \(R^2\) between level \(y = f(x)\) and log \(\log y = f(x)\) is completely invalid.

1. The Mechanical Defect of Ordinary R²

Adjusted \(R^2\) solves a critical weakness of ordinary \(R^2\): adding regressors can never reduce \(R^2\), even when those additional variables provide zero genuine explanatory power.

⚠️

Why Ordinary R² Can Mislead

Adding regressors cannot reduce \(R^2\). Therefore, an irrelevant regressor can make \(R^2\) look slightly better even when the model has not meaningfully improved.

Geometric Proof: OLS minimizes the sum of squared residuals \(\text{SSE} = e'e\) by projecting \(y\) onto the column space \(\operatorname{Col}(X)\). When new regressor columns are appended, the subspace expands: \(\operatorname{Col}(X_{\text{old}}) \subseteq \operatorname{Col}(X_{\text{new}})\). Because OLS searches over a strictly larger parameter space, the optimal residual length can never increase:
\(\text{SSE}_{\text{new}} \le \text{SSE}_{\text{old}} \implies R^2_{\text{new}} \ge R^2_{\text{old}}\)

2. Adjusted R-Squared: The Degrees-of-Freedom Penalty

To counteract this artificial inflation, adjusted \(R^2\) (denoted \(\bar{R}^2\)) scales the residual and total sums of squares by their respective degrees of freedom rather than simple observation counts.

KEY EQUATION

Adjusted Coefficient of Determination (\(\bar{R}^2\))

$$\bar{R}^2 = 1 - \frac{\text{SSE}}{\text{SST}} \cdot \frac{n - 1}{n - K} = 1 - (1 - R^2)\left(\frac{n - 1}{n - K}\right)$$
WHERE
\(\bar{R}^2\)
Adjusted \(R^2\): Goodness-of-fit metric penalized for model complexity and loss of degrees of freedom.
\(n - 1\)
SST Degrees of Freedom: Sample size minus 1 used to calculate the benchmark sample mean \(\bar{y}\).
\(n - K\)
SSE Degrees of Freedom: Residual degrees of freedom after estimating \(K\) parameters (including intercept).
\(\frac{n - 1}{n - K} > 1\)
Complexity Penalty Multiplier: Inflates the unexplained variance ratio as regressors are added.
⚖️

What the Penalty Multiplier Does

The fraction \(\frac{n - 1}{n - K}\) penalizes model complexity. When you add another regressor, \(K\) increases by 1, which reduces \(n - K\) and makes the penalty multiplier strictly larger.

• If the new regressor improves \(\text{SSE}\) only marginally, the increase in \(\frac{n - 1}{n - K}\) dominates and \(\bar{R}^2\) falls.
• In a single-variable addition, \(\bar{R}^2\) rises if and only if the absolute \(t\)-statistic on that regressor exceeds \(1\) (\(|t| > 1\)).

4. The Fundamental Rules of Model Comparison

The source document (Econometrics (3).pdf, page 2) specifies strict boundaries on when \(R^2\) and \(\bar{R}^2\) can be validly compared across candidate specifications.

Rule: Identical Dependent Variable Scale and Sample

You may compare \(R^2\) or \(\bar{R}^2\) values only when the dependent variable is defined on the exact same scale for the exact same observations.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Comparing R² Across Levels vs. Logs

Never compare \(R^2\) between a level model and a log-transformed model:

\(\text{Level Model: } y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \varepsilon \quad \text{vs.} \quad \text{Log Model: } \log(y) = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 x + u\)

In the level model, \(\text{SST} = \sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2\) measures squared physical dollars (or units). In the log model, \(\text{SST} = \sum (\log y_i - \overline{\log y})^2\) measures squared proportional movements. Because the total variation denominators (\(\text{SST}\)) are fundamentally on different mathematical scales, an \(R^2 = 0.82\) in the log model does not imply a better fit than an \(R^2 = 0.70\) in the level model!

Understanding Negative Adjusted R²: The Sample-Mean Benchmark

While ordinary \(R^2 \in [0, 1]\) with an intercept, adjusted \(\bar{R}^2\) can be negative. A negative \(\bar{R}^2\) does not just mean "the model is bad"—specifically, it means the model's complexity penalty outweighs its marginal improvement in SSE relative to the sample-mean benchmark (predicting the flat average \(\bar{y}\) for every observation, which has \(\text{SSE} = \text{SST}\) and zero estimated slopes).

🧠

Can You Explain It? Conceptual Check

Question: An empirical researcher adds 10 random variables to a wage regression of 50 workers. Ordinary \(R^2\) rises from 0.45 to 0.49, but \(\bar{R}^2\) drops from 0.43 to 0.36. Why did these two metrics move in opposite directions?

Show Explanation
Explanation: Ordinary \(R^2\) mechanically rose because expanding the column space of regressors allows OLS to fit idiosyncratic sample noise, reducing SSE from the larger parameter space. In contrast, adjusted \(\bar{R}^2\) fell because the penalty factor \(\frac{n-1}{n-K}\) rose dramatically (from \(\frac{49}{48} \approx 1.02\) to \(\frac{49}{38} \approx 1.29\)), severely penalizing the loss of 10 residual degrees of freedom for regressors with \(|t| < 1\).

✅ Quick Check: Computing Adjusted R²

A regression with \(n = 31\) observations and \(K = 6\) estimated parameters yields \(R^2 = 0.50\). What is the adjusted \(\bar{R}^2\)?

Show Answer & Calculation
ANSWER: \(\bar{R}^2 = 0.40\).

WHY? Apply the formula: \(\bar{R}^2 = 1 - (1 - R^2)\left(\frac{n - 1}{n - K}\right) = 1 - (1 - 0.50)\left(\frac{31 - 1}{31 - 6}\right) = 1 - 0.50\left(\frac{30}{25}\right) = 1 - 0.50(1.20) = 1 - 0.60 = 0.40\). The complexity penalty multiplier (1.20) reduces the goodness-of-fit measure from 0.50 to 0.40.
📋

What You Should Now Understand

  • Adding regressors can never decrease ordinary \(R^2\) because OLS minimizes SSE over an expanded subspace (\(\text{SSE}_{\text{new}} \le \text{SSE}_{\text{old}}\)).
  • Adjusted \(R^2\) penalizes model complexity via the factor \(\frac{n - 1}{n - K}\), increasing if and only if added regressors improve fit by more than the loss of degrees of freedom (for 1 regressor, \(|t| > 1\)).
  • A negative \(\bar{R}^2\) indicates that the regression model performs worse than the simple sample-mean benchmark (\(\bar{y}\)).
  • \(R^2\) and \(\bar{R}^2\) can only be compared across models sharing the exact same dependent variable scale; comparing levels \(y\) to logs \(\log(y)\) is invalid because their \(\text{SST}\) denominators are fundamentally different.
