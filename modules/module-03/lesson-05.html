LESSON 3.5 Sampling Dispersion & Precision

Variance of the OLS Estimator

Knowing that OLS is unbiased on average is not enough—we must quantify how tightly the estimates cluster around the true parameter across repeated samples. Derive the variance matrix \(\sigma^2(X'X)^{-1}\) and explore the three levers of econometric precision.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Derive the general conditional variance formula: \(\operatorname{Var}(b \mid X) = (X'X)^{-1}X'\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon \mid X)X(X'X)^{-1}\).
  • Simplify the variance-covariance matrix to \(\operatorname{Var}(b \mid X) = \sigma^2(X'X)^{-1}\) under spherical errors.
  • Inspect the source's 2-regressor diagonal matrix example and derive \(\operatorname{Var}(b_1) = \frac{\sigma^2}{N \cdot \operatorname{var}(x_1)}\).
  • Strictly distinguish between sampling variance and the standard error (\(\operatorname{SE} = \sqrt{\text{Var}}\)).
  • Explain the three direct levers for shrinking standard errors: increasing \(N\), expanding \(\operatorname{var}(x_1)\), and reducing \(\sigma^2\).

1. Deriving the OLS Variance-Covariance Matrix

Unbiasedness guarantees that our estimator is centered on the truth, but it tells us nothing about how widely individual estimates vary from sample to sample. We need to evaluate the sampling variance-covariance matrix.

Lever 1: Disturbance Noise
\(\sigma^2 \downarrow \implies \operatorname{Var}(\hat{\beta}_k) \downarrow\)

Less unobserved noise in outcomes produces tighter clustering of sample estimates around the true parameter.

Lever 2: Sample Size
\(N \uparrow \implies \operatorname{Var}(\hat{\beta}_k) \downarrow\)

More sample information averages out random idiosyncrasies, shrinking sampling spread at the rate \(1/\sqrt{N}\).

Lever 3: Variation in X
\(\operatorname{Var}(x_k) \uparrow \implies \operatorname{Var}(\hat{\beta}_k) \downarrow\)

A wider spread in the explanatory variable provides a longer leverage baseline to accurately identify the slope.

Matrix Derivation

Variance-Covariance Matrix of the OLS Estimator

What are we trying to show?

Derive the general sampling covariance matrix of \(\hat{\beta}\) conditional on \(X\), and show that under spherical disturbances it simplifies to \(\sigma^2(X'X)^{-1}\).

1

Express variance in terms of estimation error

Substitute the structural identity \(\hat{\beta} = \beta + (X'X)^{-1}X'\varepsilon\) into the variance operator:

\(\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\beta} \mid X) = \operatorname{Var}\left(\beta + (X'X)^{-1}X'\varepsilon \mid X\right) = \operatorname{Var}\left((X'X)^{-1}X'\varepsilon \mid X\right)\)
Why this step works The true population vector \(\beta\) is a constant parameter; adding a constant vector does not change variance.
2

Apply the linear variance transformation rule

For any non-random matrix \(A\), \(\operatorname{Var}(A \varepsilon \mid X) = A \operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon \mid X) A'\):

\(\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\beta} \mid X) = (X'X)^{-1}X' \operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon \mid X) \left[(X'X)^{-1}X'\right]'\)
Why this step works Conditioning on \(X\) treats \((X'X)^{-1}X'\) as a fixed deterministic transformation matrix.
3

Transpose the right-hand factor

Using \((A B)' = B' A'\) and symmetry of \((X'X)^{-1}\):

\(\left[(X'X)^{-1}X'\right]' = (X')' \left((X'X)^{-1}\right)' = X (X'X)^{-1}\)
Why this step works The matrix \(X'X\) is symmetric, so its inverse \((X'X)^{-1}\) is also symmetric: \(((X'X)^{-1})' = (X'X)^{-1}\).
4

Impose spherical disturbances

Homoskedasticity & No Autocorrelation

Substitute \(\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon \mid X) = \sigma^2 I_n\) and factor out the scalar \(\sigma^2\):

\(\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\beta} \mid X) = (X'X)^{-1}X' (\sigma^2 I_n) X (X'X)^{-1} = \sigma^2 (X'X)^{-1} (X'X) (X'X)^{-1}\)
Why this step works Spherical errors assume every observation has constant variance \(\sigma^2\) and zero correlation across different sample units.
5

Cancel inner inverse product to reach the fundamental formula

Fundamental Result

Cancel \((X'X)^{-1}(X'X) = I_k\):

\(\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\beta} \mid X) = \sigma^2 I_k (X'X)^{-1} = \sigma^2 (X'X)^{-1}\)
Why this step works Matrix inversion cancellation collapses the expression to a clean, compact \(k \times k\) variance-covariance matrix.
💡

Big Picture: The Structure of Var(β̂ | X)

The matrix \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\beta} \mid X) = \sigma^2 (X'X)^{-1}\) bundles all estimation uncertainty: the \(k\) diagonal elements \(\sigma^2 [(X'X)^{-1}]_{jj}\) represent the individual variances of each coefficient \(\hat{\beta}_j\), while the off-diagonal elements represent sampling covariances between different coefficient estimates.

KEY RESULT

Variance-Covariance Matrix of OLS

$$\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\beta} \mid X) = \sigma^2(X'X)^{-1}$$
WHERE
\(\sigma^2\)
Disturbance Variance: Variance of the unobserved population error term \(\varepsilon_i\).
\((X'X)^{-1}\)
Precision Matrix: Encodes sample size, spread of regressors, and multicollinearity.
\(\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\beta}_k \mid X)\)
Diagonal Elements: Individual parameter variances \(\sigma^2 [(X'X)^{-1}]_{kk}\).
\(\operatorname{Cov}(\hat{\beta}_j, \hat{\beta}_k \mid X)\)
Off-Diagonal Elements: Sampling covariances between pairs of estimated regression slopes.

2. Simplified Orthogonal-Regressor Example

To make the matrix expression concrete, the source document (Econometrics (3).pdf, page 4) analyzes a simplified setting where two regressors \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) are mutually orthogonal and centered, producing a diagonal cross-product matrix:

Simplified Orthogonal-Regressor Example
\[ \begin{aligned} X'X &= N \begin{bmatrix} \operatorname{var}(x_1) & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \operatorname{var}(x_2) & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \\[8pt] (X'X)^{-1} &= \frac{1}{N} \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{\operatorname{var}(x_1)} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \frac{1}{\operatorname{var}(x_2)} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} \]
Multiplying by scalar \(\sigma^2\) yields the individual slope variance: \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\beta}_1) = \frac{\sigma^2}{N \cdot \operatorname{var}(x_1)}\)
📌 Clarification: Multicollinearity in the General Case

The scalar formula \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\beta}_1) = \frac{\sigma^2}{N \operatorname{var}(x_1)}\) holds exactly only when regressors are mutually orthogonal. In general multiple regression with correlated predictors, the diagonal element of \((X'X)^{-1}\) is inflated by \(\frac{1}{1 - R_1^2}\) (the Variance Inflation Factor), where \(R_1^2\) is the fit from regressing \(x_1\) on all other regressors.

Crucial Distinction: Variance vs. Standard Error

Variance
\(\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\beta}_k \mid X)\)
Measured in squared coefficient units (e.g., \(\text{dollars}^2 / \text{year}^2\)). Used in mathematical derivations, proofs, and matrix algebra.
Standard Error
\(\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\beta}_k) = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\beta}_k \mid X)}\)
Measured in the exact same units as the coefficient (e.g., \(\text{dollars} / \text{year}\)). Used to form confidence intervals and compute \(t\)-statistics.

According to the source intuition, researchers can lower the standard error \(\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\beta}_1)\) through three distinct mechanisms: (1) increasing sample size \(N\), (2) increasing regressor spread \(\operatorname{var}(x_1)\), or (3) reducing error variance \(\sigma^2\) (e.g., by adding explanatory controls). ◆ Why It Matters

📊 Econometric Interpretation
The standard error represents statistical uncertainty. A smaller \(\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\beta}_1)\) yields a larger \(t\)-statistic (\(t = \hat{\beta}_1 / \operatorname{SE}\)), converting noisy point estimates into highly statistically significant findings.

Can You Explain It? Conceptual Check

Question: Why does having greater sample variation in \(X\) reduce the variance of the slope estimate \(\hat{\beta}_1\)?

Show Explanation
Explanation: A slope is defined as change in \(Y\) divided by change in \(X\). If all observations are tightly clustered around a single \(x\) value, the denominator is near zero, making the line wobble wildly in response to slight random disturbance shocks \(\varepsilon\). Spreading observations widely across \(X\) provides a long, stable lever arm that anchors the regression line and sharply suppresses slope estimation uncertainty.

✅ Quick Check: Precision Scaling

If an investigator quadruples the sample size from \(N = 100\) to \(N = 400\), by what factor does the standard error \(\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\beta}_1)\) change?

Show Answer & Explanation
ANSWER: The standard error is halved (\(\times 0.5\)).

WHY? The standard error scales inversely with the square root of sample size: \(\operatorname{SE} \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\). Increasing \(N\) fourfold multiplies the denominator by \(\sqrt{4} = 2\), dividing the standard error by 2 (a 50% reduction in uncertainty).
📋

What You Should Now Understand

  • Under spherical disturbances \(\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon \mid X) = \sigma^2 I_n\), the conditional covariance matrix of OLS is \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\beta} \mid X) = \sigma^2(X'X)^{-1}\).
  • Sampling variance is measured in squared units of the coefficient, whereas standard error (\(\operatorname{SE} = \sqrt{\text{Var}}\)) is in the original linear units of the coefficient.
  • Three levers directly reduce coefficient uncertainty: decreasing error variance \(\sigma^2\), expanding regressor spread \(\operatorname{Var}(x_k)\), and increasing sample size \(N\).
  • The scalar formula \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\beta}_1) = \frac{\sigma^2}{N \operatorname{Var}(x_1)}\) applies to orthogonal regressors; in general multiple regressions, multicollinearity further inflates individual variances.
