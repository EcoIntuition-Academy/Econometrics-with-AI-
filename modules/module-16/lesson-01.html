LESSON 16.1 Binary Response Foundations

Binary Outcomes & the Linear Probability Model

Understanding discrete economic choices, formulating the Linear Probability Model, proving inherent heteroskedasticity, and analyzing the failure of unbounded straight-line predictions.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Formulate binary response models where \(Y \in \{0, 1\}\) and understand why \(\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X] = \Pr(Y = 1 \mid X)\).
  • Estimate and interpret the Linear Probability Model (LPM) via Ordinary Least Squares.
  • Formally derive the conditional variance \(\text{Var}(\varepsilon \mid X) = X\beta(1 - X\beta)\) and prove that LPM errors are heteroskedastic by construction.
  • Identify the fatal limitation of the LPM: predicting nonsensical probabilities outside the interval \([0, 1]\).
  • Establish the mathematical necessity for nonlinear cumulative distribution functions (Probit and Logit).

1. Binary Outcomes in Economic Reality

In traditional linear regression, the dependent variable \(Y\) is continuous, taking any value along the real number line (e.g., wage rates, GDP growth, firm profits). However, many of the most consequential decisions in economics and public policy are inherently qualitative and discrete:

Labor Economics

Does an individual participate in the labor force (\(Y = 1\)) or remain out of the labor force (\(Y = 0\))? Does a job seeker accept an offer or continue searching?

Financial Economics

Does a corporate borrower default on debt obligations (\(Y = 1\)) or honor repayments (\(Y = 0\))? Does a bank approve a mortgage or reject the applicant?

Health & Public Policy

Does a person purchase private health insurance (\(Y = 1\)) or remain uninsured (\(Y = 0\))? Does an individual smoke cigarettes or abstain?

Industrial Organization

Does an airline enter a contested metropolitan route (\(Y = 1\)) or stay out (\(Y = 0\))? Does a consumer purchase electric vehicle Brand A or Brand B?

2. Conditional Expectations as Probabilities

When \(Y\) is a binary dummy variable, an essential mathematical identity emerges connecting the regression conditional expectation function \(\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X]\) directly to conditional probability:

Fundamental Identity Conditional Expectation of a Binary Variable
\[ \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X] = 1 \cdot \Pr(Y = 1 \mid X) + 0 \cdot \Pr(Y = 0 \mid X) = \Pr(Y = 1 \mid X) \]
WHERE:
\(Y \in \{0, 1\}\) Binary indicator variable representing event occurrence (\(1\)) or non-occurrence (\(0\))
\(\Pr(Y = 1 \mid X)\) Conditional probability of success given observable regressor vector \(X = (1, x_1, \dots, x_k)\)
\(\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X]\) Conditional expectation; for binary data, modeling the conditional mean is modeling probability!

A general binary response model posits that the conditional probability is obtained by passing a linear index \(X\beta\) through a mapping function \(F(\cdot)\):

General Specification CDF Mapping Function
\[ \Pr(Y = 1 \mid X) = F(X\beta), \qquad \Pr(Y = 0 \mid X) = 1 - F(X\beta) \]
WHERE:
\(F(\cdot)\) Mapping function: identity for LPM, normal CDF \(\Phi\) for Probit, logistic CDF \(\Lambda\) for Logit
\(1 - F(X\beta)\) Complementary probability of the non-event (\(Y = 0\))

3. The Linear Probability Model (LPM)

The simplest possible specification assumes that \(F\) is the identity function. In other words, we write the binary outcome directly as a linear regression model:

LPM Specification Linear Regression on Binary Outcome
\[ Y = X\beta + \varepsilon = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \dots + \beta_k x_k + \varepsilon \]
WHERE:
\(Y \in \{0, 1\}\) Observed binary outcome variable
\(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid X] = 0\) Zero conditional mean assumption, implying \(\Pr(Y = 1 \mid X) = X\beta\)

The primary appeal of the Linear Probability Model lies in its immediate, transparent interpretability. Because the specification is linear:

Marginal Effect Constant Slope Interpretation
\[ \frac{\partial \Pr(Y = 1 \mid X)}{\partial x_j} = \beta_j \]
WHERE:
\(\beta_j\) Constant probability slope: a 1-unit increase in \(x_j\) shifts probability by \(\beta_j\) (or \(\beta_j \times 100\) percentage points)
WORKED EMPIRICAL EXAMPLE · CIGARETTE EXCISE TAXES

Suppose a public health researcher estimates an LPM predicting whether an adult smokes cigarettes (\(Y = 1\)) as a function of the state tax rate per pack (in dollars):

\[ \widehat{\Pr}(\text{Smoker}_i = 1 \mid \text{Tax}_i) = 0.35 - 0.08 \cdot \text{Tax}_i \]
  • Baseline (Tax = $0): Predicted smoking probability is \(35\%\) (\(\hat{\beta}_0 = 0.35\)).
  • Marginal Effect: Each \(\$1.00\) tax hike reduces the probability of smoking by \(8\) percentage points (\(\hat{\beta}_1 = -0.08\)).
  • If Tax = $2.00: Predicted probability is \(0.35 - 0.08(2) = 0.19\) (\(19\%\)).

4. Problem 1: Inherent Heteroskedasticity

Despite its computational simplicity, the LPM suffers from two fatal structural statistical flaws. The first is that homoskedasticity is violated by mathematical definition.

FORMAL DERIVATION · INHERENT HETEROSKEDASTICITY IN THE LPM

Goal: Prove that the conditional error variance \(\text{Var}(\varepsilon \mid X) = X\beta(1 - X\beta)\), demonstrating that homoskedasticity is violated by construction.

STEP 1 Identify the Discrete Two-Point Error Distribution

Since \(Y = X\beta + \varepsilon\), the error is \(\varepsilon = Y - X\beta\). Because \(Y\) can only equal \(1\) or \(0\), the disturbance takes only two discrete values:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{If } Y = 1: &\quad \varepsilon = 1 - X\beta \quad \text{with probability } \Pr(Y = 1 \mid X) = X\beta \\ \text{If } Y = 0: &\quad \varepsilon = -X\beta \quad \text{with probability } \Pr(Y = 0 \mid X) = 1 - X\beta \end{aligned} \]

Why: In any regression model with a binary dependent variable, the residual cannot be continuous; it is restricted to two values.

STEP 2 Apply the Definition of Conditional Variance

Since \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid X] = 0\), the conditional variance is the expected value of \(\varepsilon^2\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Var}(\varepsilon \mid X) &= \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon^2 \mid X] \\ &= (1 - X\beta)^2 \cdot \Pr(Y = 1 \mid X) + (-X\beta)^2 \cdot \Pr(Y = 0 \mid X) \\ &= (1 - X\beta)^2 \cdot X\beta + (X\beta)^2 \cdot (1 - X\beta) \end{aligned} \]

Why: We compute the weighted sum of squared deviations using the respective Bernoulli probabilities.

STEP 3 Factor the Polynomial Expression
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Var}(\varepsilon \mid X) &= X\beta(1 - X\beta) \left[ (1 - X\beta) + X\beta \right] \\ &= X\beta(1 - X\beta) \cdot [1] \\ &= X\beta(1 - X\beta) \end{aligned} \]

Why: The term inside brackets sums cleanly to \(1\), leaving \(X\beta(1 - X\beta) = P(1 - P)\).

KEY RESULT · LPM CONDITIONAL VARIANCE
\[ \text{Var}(\varepsilon_i \mid X_i) = X_i\beta(1 - X_i\beta) = P_i(1 - P_i) \]

Because the variance depends directly on the regressor values \(X_i\), the disturbance is heteroskedastic by definition for any \(\beta \neq 0\). Conventional OLS standard errors are invalid.

5. Problem 2: Out-of-Bounds Probability Predictions

The second and fatal flaw of the Linear Probability Model is that straight lines cannot be bounded. By the axioms of probability (Kolmogorov 1933), any legitimate probability must satisfy \(0 \le \Pr(A) \le 1\).

Super-Unitary Probabilities

\[ X\beta > 1 \implies \widehat{\Pr}(Y = 1 \mid X) > 1 \]

A probability greater than \(1.0\) (e.g., \(120\%\)) is mathematically nonsensical and cannot be used in decision theory.

Negative Probabilities

\[ X\beta < 0 \implies \widehat{\Pr}(Y = 1 \mid X) < 0 \]

A probability less than zero (e.g., \(-15\%\)) is impossible. Furthermore, it causes the variance \(X\beta(1 - X\beta)\) to turn negative!

◆ WHY IT MATTERS

Because true probabilities are strictly bounded in \([0, 1]\), linear projections onto binary outcomes inevitably break down. Predicting negative probabilities or probabilities exceeding \(100\%\) violates basic probability axioms and produces impossible negative error variances.

📊 ECONOMETRIC INTERPRETATION

The LPM is frequently used in applied economics as a quick rough approximation of average treatment effects, but its inherent heteroskedasticity and out-of-bounds predictions make it unsuitable for predicting individual probabilities or conducting structural policy analysis.

Quick Knowledge Check

Question: What are the two fundamental statistical problems with the Linear Probability Model?

Consider what happens to the error variance when \(Y\) takes only two discrete values, and what happens to a straight-line function as the regressor \(x\) moves to extreme values.

1. Inherent Heteroskedasticity: The disturbance variance \(\text{Var}(\varepsilon \mid X) = X\beta(1 - X\beta)\) depends directly on the regressor values \(X\), violating Gauss-Markov Assumption A4 and invalidating conventional OLS standard errors.

2. Out-of-Bounds Predictions: Because straight lines are unbounded, for sufficiently large or small values of \(x\), the model inevitably predicts probabilities \(< 0\) or \(> 1\), violating the fundamental axioms of probability and producing negative variance values.

Lesson 16.1 Summary

Binary response models evaluate discrete outcomes where \(Y \in \{0, 1\}\), interpreting the conditional expectation \(\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X]\) as the probability \(\Pr(Y = 1 \mid X)\). While the Linear Probability Model offers straightforward coefficient interpretation, its errors are heteroskedastic by definition and straight-line projections inevitably produce invalid probabilities outside \([0, 1]\). These limitations motivate passing the linear index through a nonlinear cumulative distribution function. In the next lesson, we introduce the foundational nonlinear binary response models: Probit and Logit.
