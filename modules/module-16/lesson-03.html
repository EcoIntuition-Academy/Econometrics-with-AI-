LESSON 16.3 Nonlinear Economic Interpretation

Marginal Effects in Binary Choice Models

Mastering partial derivatives along the S-curve, deriving the quotient-rule logistic slope, computing discrete differences for dummy variables, and contrasting MEM versus AME.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Understand why raw coefficients \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) in Probit and Logit do not represent changes in probability.
  • Apply the chain rule to derive the general marginal effect formula \(\frac{\partial P}{\partial x_j} = g(\mathbf{x}'\boldsymbol{\beta})\beta_j\).
  • Derive the elegant Logit marginal effect \(P(1 - P)\beta_j\) using the calculus quotient rule.
  • Differentiate continuous partial derivatives from discrete differences required for dummy variables.
  • Compare Marginal Effects at the Means (MEM) with Average Marginal Effects (AME) in modern empirical practice.

1. The Core Nonlinear Dilemma

In linear regression, the marginal effect of any regressor \(x_j\) is constant: \(\frac{\partial Y}{\partial x_j} = \beta_j\). In nonlinear binary choice models, however, probability is bounded by a nonlinear S-curve \(G(\mathbf{x}'\boldsymbol{\beta})\). By the calculus chain rule:

Chain Rule Formula General Marginal Effect in Binary Response
\[ \frac{\partial \Pr(Y_i = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}_i)}{\partial x_{ij}} = \frac{dG(z_i)}{dz_i} \cdot \frac{\partial z_i}{\partial x_{ij}} = g(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}) \cdot \beta_j \]
WHERE:
\(g(z) = \frac{dG(z)}{dz}\) Probability density function evaluated at the specific linear index \(z_i = \mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}\)
\(\beta_j\) Parameter weight; determines the direction (sign) and relative magnitude across regressors
\(\mathbf{x}_i\) The baseline characteristics of the observation; the marginal effect varies across individuals!
⚠️ Common Student Mistake
MISCONCEPTION: "The estimated coefficient \(\hat{\beta}_j\) in a Probit or Logit model represents the percentage-point change in probability from a 1-unit increase in \(x_j\)."
CORRECTION: A coefficient \(\beta_j\) represents the change in the unobserved continuous index \(z\) (or log-odds in Logit), not the change in probability. To obtain the marginal effect on probability, you must multiply \(\beta_j\) by the probability density \(g(\mathbf{x}'\boldsymbol{\beta})\) at that specific point. The sign of \(\beta_j\) matches the sign of the marginal effect because \(g(z) > 0\), but the magnitude varies across individuals.

2. Geometric Intuition: Center vs. Tails on the S-Curve

The magnitude of the marginal effect is governed entirely by where an individual sits along the S-curve:

Center of the Distribution (\(z = 0, P = 0.50\))

The density \(g(z)\) reaches its absolute maximum. Here, individuals are on the knife-edge of indecision, so an increase in \(x_j\) produces the largest possible shift in probability.

Logit: \(\lambda(0) = 0.25 \implies \text{Max Slope} = 0.25\beta_j\)

Extreme Tails (\(|z| > 2.5, P \approx 0\) or \(P \approx 1\))

The density \(g(z)\) rapidly decays to zero. For individuals whose outcome is almost certain, the S-curve is nearly horizontal, so an increase in \(x_j\) produces almost zero change in probability.

\(g(z) \to 0 \implies \text{Marginal Effect} \to 0\)

3. Deriving the Logit Marginal Effect via the Quotient Rule

A celebrated mathematical result in nonlinear econometrics is that the logistic density function can be written directly in terms of predicted probabilities: \(\lambda(z) = P(1 - P)\). We formally derive this using the calculus quotient rule.

FORMAL DERIVATION · THE LOGISTIC DENSITY IDENTITY

Goal: Prove that the partial derivative of \(\Lambda(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}\) satisfies \(\frac{d\Lambda(z)}{dz} = \Lambda(z)\big(1 - \Lambda(z)\big)\).

STEP 1 Recall the Function and Complement
\[ \Lambda(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}, \qquad 1 - \Lambda(z) = \frac{e^{-z}}{1 + e^{-z}} \]

Why: Writing both \(\Lambda(z)\) and its complement with the common denominator \(1 + e^{-z}\) sets up factoring in Step 3.

STEP 2 Apply the Quotient Rule of Differentiation
\[ \frac{d}{dz}\left[\frac{u}{v}\right] = \frac{u'v - uv'}{v^2} \implies \frac{d\Lambda(z)}{dz} = \frac{0 \cdot (1 + e^{-z}) - 1 \cdot (-e^{-z})}{(1 + e^{-z})^2} = \frac{e^{-z}}{(1 + e^{-z})^2} \]

Why: The numerator is constant \(u = 1\) (\(u' = 0\)), and \(\frac{d}{dz}(1 + e^{-z}) = -e^{-z}\), yielding a clean positive numerator.

STEP 3 Factor into the Product of Probabilities
\[ \frac{d\Lambda(z)}{dz} = \left(\frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}\right) \cdot \left(\frac{e^{-z}}{1 + e^{-z}}\right) = \Lambda(z)\big(1 - \Lambda(z)\big) \]

Why: The denominator squared \((1 + e^{-z})^2\) factors neatly into the two terms defining \(P\) and \(1 - P\).

KEY RESULT · LOGIT MARGINAL EFFECT
\[ \frac{\partial \Pr(Y_i = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}_i)}{\partial x_{ij}} = P_i(1 - P_i)\beta_j \]

In Logit models, you never need numerical integration to compute marginal effects! You simply multiply the predicted probability \(P_i\) by its complement \((1 - P_i)\) and the estimated coefficient \(\beta_j\).

BIG PICTURE · THE DIVIDE-BY-FOUR RULE

Because \(P(1 - P)\) attains its maximum value of \(0.25\) at \(P = 0.50\), the maximum possible marginal effect for regressor \(j\) under a Logit model is always \(\frac{\beta_j}{4}\). If an empirical paper reports a Logit coefficient of \(\hat{\beta}_1 = 0.60\), you immediately know that the regressor can shift probability by at most \(15\) percentage points.

4. Nonlinear Marginal Effects with Interaction Terms

In linear regression, including an interaction term \(x_1 x_2\) with coefficient \(\beta_{12}\) implies that the interaction effect is simply \(\frac{\partial^2 Y}{\partial x_1 \partial x_2} = \beta_{12}\). In a landmark econometric paper, Ai and Norton (2003) demonstrated that this intuition fails completely in nonlinear models.

Ai & Norton (2003) Cross-Partial Derivative in Nonlinear Models
\[ \frac{\partial^2 \Pr(Y_i = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}_i)}{\partial x_{i1} \partial x_{i2}} = g'(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta})\beta_1 \beta_2 + g(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta})\beta_{12} \]
WHERE:
\(g'(z) = \frac{dg(z)}{dz}\) Derivative of the density function; changes sign across the midpoint (\(g'(z) > 0\) for \(z < 0\), \(g'(z) < 0\) for \(z > 0\))
\(\beta_{12}\) Coefficient on the interaction term \(x_1 x_2\)
\(g'(\mathbf{x}'\boldsymbol{\beta})\beta_1 \beta_2\) Nonlinear interaction component; can be non-zero even if \(\beta_{12} = 0\)!

Key Takeaways for Applied Researchers

  • The interaction effect can be non-zero even if the interaction coefficient is exactly zero (\(\beta_{12} = 0\)).
  • The true interaction effect can have a different sign from \(\beta_{12}\) for some individuals in the sample.
  • Testing the statistical significance of \(\beta_{12}\) via a standard t-test is invalid for testing whether the economic interaction effect is significant.

5. Aggregating Marginal Effects: MEM vs. AME

Because the marginal effect \(g(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta})\beta_j\) varies for every individual \(i\) in the sample, researchers need a summary measure to report in empirical tables. Econometrics provides two competing aggregation methodologies:

Marginal Effect at the Means (MEM)

\[ \text{MEM}_j = g(\bar{\mathbf{x}}'\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) \cdot \hat{\beta}_j \]

Evaluates the marginal effect at the average regressor vector \(\bar{\mathbf{x}}\). Computationally fast, but creates an artificial "average person" that might not exist in reality (e.g., female = 0.52, married = 0.68).

Average Marginal Effect (AME) · PREFERRED

\[ \text{AME}_j = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N g(\mathbf{x}_i'\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) \cdot \hat{\beta}_j \]

Calculates the individual marginal effect for every person in the sample and then computes the sample average. Highly recommended in modern empirical economics.

Discrete Treatment Discrete Difference for Binary / Indicator Variables
\[ \Delta P_i = G(\mathbf{x}_{i(-d)}'\boldsymbol{\beta}_{(-d)} + \beta_d) - G(\mathbf{x}_{i(-d)}'\boldsymbol{\beta}_{(-d)}) \]
WHERE:
\(\Delta P_i\) Finite probability shift from turning dummy variable \(d_i\) from \(0\) to \(1\)
\(\mathbf{x}_{i(-d)}'\boldsymbol{\beta}_{(-d)}\) Linear index evaluated with all regressors other than the indicator held fixed

Quick Knowledge Check

Question: Under the Logit model with estimated slope coefficient \(\hat{\beta}_1 = 0.80\), what is the maximum possible marginal effect on the probability of a one-unit change in \(x_1\)?

Recall that the logistic density \(\lambda(z) = P(1-P)\) attains its maximum possible value when \(P = 0.50\).

Solution: The maximum marginal effect occurs at \(P = 0.5\), where \(P(1 - P) = (0.5)(0.5) = 0.25\). Multiplying by \(\hat{\beta}_1 = 0.80\) yields: \[ \text{Max Marginal Effect} = 0.25 \times 0.80 = 0.20 \quad (20\text{ percentage points}) \] This is known as the practical "divide by four" rule of thumb for Logit coefficients.

Lesson 16.3 Summary

Marginal effects bridge the gap between abstract nonlinear coefficients and actionable economic probabilities:

  • Chain Rule Identity: \(\frac{\partial P}{\partial x_j} = g(\mathbf{x}'\boldsymbol{\beta})\beta_j\). The coefficient gives the sign, but the density dictates the magnitude.
  • Logit Formula: By the quotient rule, the logistic density factors cleanly into \(P(1 - P)\), making the marginal effect \(P(1 - P)\beta_j\).
  • Divide-by-Four Rule: The maximum marginal effect in a Logit model is strictly bounded by \(\frac{\beta_j}{4}\).
  • Interaction Pitfall: In nonlinear models, cross-partial derivatives depend on density slopes. Testing \(\beta_{12} = 0\) is invalid for testing the economic interaction effect (Ai & Norton 2003).
  • MEM vs. AME: Average Marginal Effects (AME) average individual effects across the true sample distribution, avoiding the artificial "average person" problem of MEM.
