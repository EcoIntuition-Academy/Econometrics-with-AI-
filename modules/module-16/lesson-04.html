LESSON 16.4 Microeconomic Choice Foundations

Latent Variables & Threshold Models

Interpret binary outcomes as observed realizations of an underlying continuous latent variable crossing a critical threshold, bridging economic utility theory with empirical estimation.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Formulate the latent variable model \(y^* = \mathbf{x}'\boldsymbol{\beta} + \varepsilon\) with observation rule \(y = \mathbf{1}(y^* > 0)\).
  • Interpret latent variables as unobserved utility, net benefits, or underlying propensities.
  • Formally derive the choice probability \(\Pr(y=1 \mid \mathbf{x}) = \Pr(\varepsilon > -\mathbf{x}'\boldsymbol{\beta})\) under symmetric distributions.
  • Connect error distributions (standard normal vs. standard logistic) directly to Probit and Logit specifications.
  • Visualize the shifting threshold \(-\mathbf{x}'\boldsymbol{\beta}\) and the resulting changes in tail probability mass.

1. The Microeconomic Foundations of Binary Decisions

Why do economic agents make discrete choices? Microeconomic theory models decision-making using continuous latent propensities. An individual weighs benefits and costs along a continuous spectrum, but the econometrician observes only the final discrete action:

CONSUMER DEMAND

Net Utility of Purchase

A consumer contemplating whether to buy insurance compares the utility of coverage versus remaining uninsured. The continuous difference in utility is unobserved, but the purchase decision is binary (\(y = 1\) if purchase, \(0\) otherwise).

LABOR SUPPLY

Wage Offer vs. Reservation Wage

A worker compares an offered market wage to their internal reservation wage. The individual enters the labor force (\(y = 1\)) if and only if the continuous net benefit (offered wage minus reservation wage) is positive.

We formalize this continuous, unobservable propensity as the latent variable \(y^*\):

Structural Model Latent Variable Linear Specification
\[ y_i^* = \mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta} + \varepsilon_i \]
WHERE:
\(y_i^*\) Unobserved continuous latent propensity (e.g., net utility, reservation difference)
\(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}\) Deterministic portion of propensity explained by observed individual characteristics
\(\varepsilon_i\) Unobserved idiosyncratic taste, ability, or random shock independent of \(\mathbf{x}_i\)

2. The Observational Threshold Rule

The econometrician cannot directly observe the continuous score \(y_i^*\). Instead, we observe only whether \(y_i^*\) crosses an administrative or behavioral threshold (traditionally normalized to zero without loss of generality):

Observation Rule Threshold Mapping Mechanism
\[ y_i = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } y_i^* > 0 \\ 0 & \text{if } y_i^* \le 0 \end{cases} \]
WHERE:
\(y_i \in \{0, 1\}\) Observed binary outcome in your empirical dataset
\(y_i^* > 0\) Latent propensity exceeds threshold, triggering the observable action (\(y_i = 1\))
⚠️ Common Student Mistake
MISCONCEPTION: "In latent variable models, we observe both the continuous index \(y^*\) and the discrete outcome \(y\) in our dataset."
CORRECTION: The latent variable \(y^*\) is completely unobservable. Your empirical dataset contains only the discrete zeroes and ones of \(y\). Binary choice models use the probability distribution of the error \(\varepsilon\) to bridge the gap between continuous theoretical utility and discrete observational data.

3. Deriving Choice Probabilities from Error Distributions

Using the threshold rule, we can express the probability of observing \(y = 1\) directly in terms of the random disturbance \(\varepsilon\):

Probability Derivation Threshold Probability Integral
\[ \Pr(y_i = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}_i) = \Pr(y_i^* > 0 \mid \mathbf{x}_i) = \Pr(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta} + \varepsilon_i > 0 \mid \mathbf{x}_i) = \Pr(\varepsilon_i > -\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}) \]
WHERE:
\(-\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}\) The critical cutoff threshold that the random shock \(\varepsilon_i\) must exceed
\(\Pr(\varepsilon_i > -\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta})\) Area under the error density function to the right of the cutoff point

Because standard probability distributions are symmetric around zero (\(\Pr(\varepsilon > -z) = \Pr(\varepsilon < z) = F(z)\)), this immediately yields the standard binary choice specifications:

PROBIT SPECIFICATION

Standard Normal Error

If the unobserved disturbance follows a standard normal distribution \(\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\):

\[ \Pr(y_i = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}_i) = \Phi(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}) \]
LOGIT SPECIFICATION

Standard Logistic Error

If the unobserved disturbance follows a standard logistic distribution \(\varepsilon_i \sim \text{Logistic}(0, 1)\):

\[ \Pr(y_i = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}_i) = \Lambda(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}) \]

4. Geometric Representation: Threshold at \(-\mathbf{x}'\boldsymbol{\beta}\)

The latent variable formulation provides an intuitive geometric picture:

  • The density curve \(f(\varepsilon)\) remains stationary, centered at zero.
  • As an individual's deterministic score \(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}\) increases, the threshold \(-\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}\) shifts to the left.
  • Shifting the threshold to the left captures more area under the curve to the right, expanding the probability that \(\varepsilon > -\mathbf{x}'\boldsymbol{\beta}\) and thus increasing \(\Pr(y = 1)\).

📊 ECONOMETRIC INTERPRETATION

Setting the cutoff threshold at \(0\) is standard because any non-zero threshold \(c\) is absorbed into the intercept: \(\mathbf{x}'\boldsymbol{\beta} - c = \beta_0^* + \beta_1 x_1 + \dots\).

Quick Knowledge Check

Question: What do we observe in an empirical dataset if the underlying behavioral process is driven by a continuous latent variable \(y^*\)?

Remember that latent variables represent unobserved utility or propensities.

Solution: We observe only the binary choice indicator \(y \in \{0, 1\}\), indicating whether the latent score exceeded the critical threshold (\(y^* > 0\)). The continuous index \(y^*\) itself is never directly observed.

Lesson 16.4 Summary

Latent variable models ground empirical binary regressions in microeconomic choice theory:

  • Latent Index: Continuous propensity \(y_i^* = \mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta} + \varepsilon_i\) captures net benefits, utility, or reservation wage differences.
  • Observation Threshold: We observe \(y_i = 1\) if \(y_i^* > 0\) and \(0\) otherwise.
  • Probability Derivation: Choice probability equals \(\Pr(\varepsilon_i > -\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta})\), which evaluates to \(F(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta})\) under symmetric error distributions.
  • Error Distribution Link: Normal error generates Probit; logistic error generates Logit.
