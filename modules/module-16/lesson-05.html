LESSON 16.5 Maximum Likelihood Methodology

Maximum Likelihood for Binary Outcomes

Construct the Maximum Likelihood objective function for binary choice models, evaluate Bernoulli observation contributions, and understand why econometricians maximize the log-likelihood.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Articulate the core intuition of Maximum Likelihood Estimation: choosing parameters that maximize the probability of observing the sample data.
  • Formulate the individual Bernoulli likelihood contribution \(p_i^{y_i}(1 - p_i)^{1 - y_i}\).
  • Derive the sample likelihood product and convert it into the additive log-likelihood function \(\ln L(\boldsymbol{\beta})\).
  • Trace the exact numerical likelihood calculation for a candidate parameter \(\beta = 1.5\) on a 3-observation empirical dataset.
  • Understand how observations with \(y_i = 0\) and high predicted probabilities heavily penalize the joint likelihood.

1. The Intuition of Maximum Likelihood Estimation

In linear regression, Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) chooses parameters that minimize the sum of squared vertical residuals: \(\min \sum e_i^2\). However, in nonlinear probability models like Probit and Logit, the error variance depends on the unknown parameters and probabilities are nonlinear S-curves, rendering linear projection unsuitable.

Instead, econometricians adopt the premier estimation framework in modern statistics: Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE).

Optimization Objective Maximum Likelihood Estimator Definition
\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{ML}} = \arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} L(\boldsymbol{\beta}) \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{ML}} = \arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \ln L(\boldsymbol{\beta}) \]
WHERE:
\(L(\boldsymbol{\beta})\) Joint probability of observing the sample data, viewed as a function of the parameter vector \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\)
\(\ln L(\boldsymbol{\beta})\) Natural logarithm of the likelihood function; computationally stable and strictly concave for Probit and Logit
⚠️ Common Student Mistake
MISCONCEPTION: "The likelihood \(L(\boldsymbol{\beta})\) is the probability that the parameter \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) is true."
CORRECTION: In classical (frequentist) econometrics, the true population parameter \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) is a fixed, non-random constant, not a random variable. Therefore, \(L(\boldsymbol{\beta})\) is never a probability of the parameter. Rather, it is the probability of having generated the sample data that actually landed in your dataset, evaluated as a function of candidate parameter vectors.

2. Why Optimize the Log-Likelihood?

In practice, applied researchers and computational algorithms almost never maximize the raw likelihood \(L(\boldsymbol{\beta})\). Instead, they maximize its natural logarithm, \(\ln L(\boldsymbol{\beta})\):

RAW LIKELIHOOD \(L(\boldsymbol{\beta})\)

A Product of Probabilities

For independent observations, joint probability is the product of individual probabilities:

\[ L(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = P_1 \times P_2 \times \dots \times P_N \]

Computational Underflow: Multiplying thousands of probabilities strictly between \(0\) and \(1\) causes computers to round the product down to absolute zero (\(0.0000\)).

LOG-LIKELIHOOD \(\ln L(\boldsymbol{\beta})\)

A Sum of Log-Probabilities

Taking natural logarithms converts cumbersome products into additive sums:

\[ \ln L(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \sum_{i=1}^N \ln \Pr(y_i \mid \mathbf{x}_i, \boldsymbol{\beta}) \]

Numerical Stability: Because \(\ln(\cdot)\) is a strictly monotonically increasing transformation, the parameter \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) that maximizes \(\ln L\) is mathematically identical to the one that maximizes \(L\).

3. Likelihood Formulation for Binary Outcomes

Let \(y_i \in \{0, 1\}\) be an independent binary outcome. Let \(p_i = F(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta})\) denote the predicted probability of the event. Because each observation is either a success (\(y_i = 1\)) or a failure (\(y_i = 0\)), we write the individual probability distribution compactly using Bernoulli exponents:

Bernoulli Contribution Individual Likelihood Factor
\[ \Pr(y_i \mid \mathbf{x}_i, \boldsymbol{\beta}) = p_i^{y_i} (1 - p_i)^{1 - y_i} \]
WHERE:
\(y_i = 1\) Evaluates to \(p_i^1 (1 - p_i)^0 = p_i\) (probability of success)
\(y_i = 0\) Evaluates to \(p_i^0 (1 - p_i)^1 = 1 - p_i\) (probability of non-event)

Multiplying across all \(N\) independently observed individuals yields the full sample likelihood:

Sample Formulation Sample Likelihood and Log-Likelihood Functions
\[ L(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \prod_{i=1}^N p_i^{y_i} (1 - p_i)^{1 - y_i} \implies \ln L(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \sum_{i=1}^N \left[ y_i \ln p_i + (1 - y_i) \ln(1 - p_i) \right] \]
WHERE:
\(y_i \ln p_i\) Contribution from observed positive outcomes (\(y_i = 1\))
\((1 - y_i) \ln(1 - p_i)\) Contribution from observed negative outcomes (\(y_i = 0\))

For the Logit model, substituting \(p_i = \Lambda(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta})\) and simplifying yields an elegant closed-form log-likelihood:

Logit Link Logit Log-Likelihood Simplified Representation
\[ \ln L(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \sum_{i=1}^N \left[ y_i \mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta} - \ln(1 + e^{\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}}) \right] \]
WHERE:
\(-\ln(1 + e^{\mathbf{x}'\boldsymbol{\beta}})\) Normalizing denominator term ensuring mathematical concavity across all parameter space

4. Numerical Worked Example from Primary Source

To trace the mechanical calculation step by step, consider the exact three-observation dataset presented in primary econometric source notes:

Observation \(i\) Regressor \(x_i\) Observed Outcome \(y_i\)
1 \(0.5\) \(1\)
2 \(1.0\) \(1\)
3 \(1.5\) \(0\)

Suppose an econometrician tests a candidate parameter value \(\beta = 1.5\) under the Logit model \(F = \Lambda\):

Step 1: Compute Predicted Probabilities \(p_i = \Lambda(x_i \cdot \beta)\)

  • \(p_1 = \Lambda(0.5 \times 1.5) = \Lambda(0.75) = \frac{e^{0.75}}{1 + e^{0.75}} \approx \mathbf{0.679}\)
  • \(p_2 = \Lambda(1.0 \times 1.5) = \Lambda(1.50) = \frac{e^{1.50}}{1 + e^{1.50}} \approx \mathbf{0.818}\)
  • \(p_3 = \Lambda(1.5 \times 1.5) = \Lambda(2.25) = \frac{e^{2.25}}{1 + e^{2.25}} \approx \mathbf{0.904}\)

Step 2: Calculate Likelihood \(L(\beta)\)

Since observations 1 and 2 have \(y_i = 1\), their contributions are \(p_1\) and \(p_2\). Because observation 3 has \(y_3 = 0\), its contribution is \(1 - p_3\):

\[ L(\beta) = p_1^1 \times p_2^1 \times (1 - p_3)^1 = 0.679 \times 0.818 \times (1 - 0.904) = 0.679 \times 0.818 \times 0.096 \approx \mathbf{0.053} \]

Step 3: Calculate Log-Likelihood \(\ln L(\beta)\)

\[ \ln L(\beta) = \ln(0.679) + \ln(0.818) + \ln(0.096) \approx -0.387 - 0.201 - 2.341 \approx \mathbf{-2.93} \]
⚠️ The Penalty of Mismatched Predictions
OBSERVATION: Notice how observation 3 behaves: the individual has \(x_3 = 1.5\) and realized \(y_3 = 0\). Setting candidate \(\beta = 1.5\) caused the model to predict a high \(90.4\%\) probability of being a \(1\).
IMPACT ON LIKELIHOOD: Because the actual outcome was \(0\), its likelihood contribution is \(1 - 0.904 = 0.096\), which contributes a massive negative penalty of \(\ln(0.096) \approx -2.341\) to the total log-likelihood! The true MLE balances these competing forces to find the parameter that maximizes joint agreement with data.

◆ WHY IT MATTERS

Because the log-likelihood function of standard Probit and Logit models is strictly concave everywhere, numerical optimization algorithms will always converge to the unique global maximum.

📊 ECONOMETRIC INTERPRETATION

Statistical software finds \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{ML}}\) by iteratively climbing the hill of the log-likelihood using the gradient (Score) and curvature (Hessian).

Quick Knowledge Check

Question: For a binary observation with \(y_i = 0\), which probability enters its likelihood contribution?

Use the Bernoulli formula \(\Pr(y_i) = p_i^{y_i} (1 - p_i)^{1 - y_i}\) and evaluate it at \(y_i = 0\).

Solution: When \(y_i = 0\), the term evaluates to \(p_i^0 (1 - p_i)^{1 - 0} = 1 \cdot (1 - p_i) = 1 - p_i\). Thus, only the probability of non-occurrence (\(1 - p_i\)) enters the likelihood product for failure units.

Lesson 16.5 Summary

Maximum Likelihood Estimation provides a rigorous mathematical framework for binary choice models:

  • Bernoulli Likelihood: Each unit contributes \(p_i^{y_i}(1 - p_i)^{1 - y_i}\) to the sample joint product.
  • Log-Likelihood Transformation: Converting products into sums prevents floating-point underflow and preserves the identical maximum.
  • Penalty Mechanism: Observations where \(y_i = 0\) strongly penalize high candidate probabilities \(p_i \approx 1\), forcing \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) to balance conflicting sample demands.
  • Global Concavity: Probit and Logit log-likelihoods are strictly concave, ensuring algorithms reach the unique global maximum.
