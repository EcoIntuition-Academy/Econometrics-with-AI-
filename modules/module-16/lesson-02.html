LESSON 16.2 Nonlinear Probability Curves

Probit, Logit & Probability Curves

Overcoming the bounding and linear limitations of the LPM through cumulative distribution functions, standard normal versus logistic curves, and extreme tail behavior.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Understand how cumulative distribution functions (CDFs) guarantee predicted probabilities stay strictly bounded inside \([0, 1]\).
  • Distinguish the mathematical structure of the Probit model (\(\Phi\)) from the Logit model (\(\Lambda\)).
  • Derive the relationship between probability density functions (PDFs) and cumulative distribution functions (CDFs).
  • Analyze the practical equivalence of Probit and Logit in empirical research and understand the \(1.6\) coefficient scaling rule.

1. The Solution to Unbounded Probabilities

In Lesson 16.1, we discovered that the Linear Probability Model inevitably predicts probabilities outside \([0, 1]\) because a linear function is unbounded. To solve this dilemma, econometrics maps the linear index \(z_i = \mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}\) through a continuous, monotonically increasing cumulative distribution function (CDF), denoted \(G(z)\):

Nonlinear Specification Cumulative Distribution Function Mapping
\[ \Pr(Y_i = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}_i) = G(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}), \qquad \Pr(Y_i = 0 \mid \mathbf{x}_i) = 1 - G(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}) \]
WHERE:
\(G(\cdot)\) A continuous cumulative distribution function satisfying \(0 < G(z) < 1\) for all real \(z\)
\(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}\) The linear index \(z_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \dots + \beta_k x_{ik}\)
\(1 - G(z_i)\) The complementary probability of non-occurrence (\(Y_i = 0\))

Because \(G(z)\) is a valid cumulative probability distribution, it possesses two essential mathematical properties that permanently fix the LPM defect:

Asymptote at Zero

\[ \lim_{z \to -\infty} G(z) = 0 \]

As the linear index becomes infinitely negative, the probability gently flattens and approaches \(0\) asymptotically. It can never drop below zero.

Asymptote at One

\[ \lim_{z \to +\infty} G(z) = 1 \]

As the linear index becomes infinitely positive, the probability gently flattens and approaches \(1\) asymptotically. It can never exceed one.

2. Geometric Relationship: Density Function vs. Cumulative Probability

To understand how probabilities change when regressors change, we examine the connection between the cumulative distribution function \(G(z)\) and its underlying probability density function (PDF), denoted \(g(z)\). By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus:

Calculus Foundation Density Derivative & Cumulative Integral
\[ g(z) \equiv \frac{dG(z)}{dz} > 0 \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad G(z) = \int_{-\infty}^{z} g(u)\, du \]
WHERE:
\(g(z)\) Probability density function; represents the slope of the cumulative S-curve at point \(z\)
\(G(z)\) Cumulative distribution function; equal to the area under the density curve \(g(u)\) from \(-\infty\) up to \(z\)

Because densities are strictly positive (\(g(z) > 0\)), the cumulative function \(G(z)\) is strictly monotonically increasing. The steeper the density curve at \(z\), the faster the probability rises along the S-curve.

3. The Probit Model & Scale Normalization

The Probit model selects the standard normal distribution function as its mapping function \(G(\cdot)\). In this specification, we denote the CDF as \(\Phi(z)\) and the PDF as \(\phi(z)\).

Probit PDF Standard Normal Density Function
\[ \phi(z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left(-\frac{z^2}{2}\right) \]
WHERE:
\(\phi(z)\) Standard normal bell curve with mean \(\mu = 0\) and variance \(\sigma^2 = 1\)
\(\phi(0)\) Maximum density height of \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \approx 0.3989\) attained at the center (\(z = 0\))
Probit Specification Standard Normal Cumulative Distribution Function
\[ \Pr(Y_i = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}_i) = \Phi(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \int_{-\infty}^{\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left(-\frac{u^2}{2}\right) du \]
WHERE:
\(\Phi(z)\) The standard normal cumulative distribution integral (no closed-form elementary formula exists)
\(\Phi(0) = 0.50\) Symmetry condition: when the linear index equals zero, the predicted probability is exactly 50%
⚠️ Common Student Mistake
MISCONCEPTION: "We can estimate the variance of the error term \(\sigma^2\) in a binary Probit model from sample data."
CORRECTION: Because we only observe a discrete binary outcome \(Y \in \{0, 1\}\), the scale of the underlying continuous process is inherently unidentified. Setting \(\sigma^2 = 1\) in the Probit model is an identifying normalization, not an empirical discovery or restrictive assumption. Any scaling factor would simply rescale all coefficients \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) proportionally without altering predicted probabilities.

4. The Logit Model

The Logit model specifies \(G(\cdot)\) as the standard logistic cumulative distribution function, denoted \(\Lambda(z)\). Unlike the Gaussian normal distribution, the logistic CDF has an exact, closed-form algebraic expression.

Logit Specification Standard Logistic Cumulative Distribution Function
\[ \Pr(Y_i = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}_i) = \Lambda(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}}} = \frac{e^{\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}}}{1 + e^{\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}}} \]
WHERE:
\(\Lambda(z)\) Standard logistic sigmoid function; maps \((-\infty, +\infty)\) smoothly into \((0, 1)\)
\(e^{-z}\) Natural exponential function of the negative linear index
Symmetry Property Logistic Complementary Probability
\[ 1 - \Lambda(z) = 1 - \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} = \frac{e^{-z}}{1 + e^{-z}} = \frac{1}{1 + e^z} = \Lambda(-z) \]
WHERE:
\(1 - \Lambda(z)\) Probability of non-event (\(Y = 0\)); equals \(\Lambda(-z)\) by rotational symmetry
Logit Link Odds Ratio & the Log-Odds Transformation
\[ \text{Odds}_i = \frac{P_i}{1 - P_i} = e^{\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}} \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad \ln\left(\frac{P_i}{1 - P_i}\right) = \mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta} \]
WHERE:
\(\frac{P_i}{1 - P_i}\) Odds of the event occurring relative to not occurring
\(\ln(\text{Odds}_i)\) The "logit" or log-odds; directly linear in the parameters \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\)
🤖 AI Insight: Machine Learning Sigmoids vs. Econometric Link Functions

In modern artificial intelligence and machine learning architectures, the logistic CDF \(\Lambda(z)\) is known as the sigmoid activation function. While computer scientists prize the sigmoid for its simple derivative in backpropagation, econometricians prize it because its log-odds transformation linearizes the relationship, permitting clear structural economic interpretation of odds ratios.

5. Probit vs. Logit: Thick Tails & Practical Equivalence

Both the standard normal CDF \(\Phi(z)\) and standard logistic CDF \(\Lambda(z)\) are symmetric S-curves centered at \(z = 0\) with \(G(0) = 0.50\). However, their underlying error distributions have distinct variances and tail thickness:

Standard Normal Distribution

  • Variance: \(\sigma^2 = 1.00\)
  • Kurtosis: \(3.00\) (standard mesokurtic benchmark)
  • Tail Decay: Exponentially fast decay (\(e^{-z^2/2}\)), leading to thin tails.

Standard Logistic Distribution

  • Variance: \(\sigma^2 = \frac{\pi^2}{3} \approx 3.29\)
  • Kurtosis: \(4.20\) (leptokurtic, heavier tails)
  • Tail Decay: Power-like slower decay (\(e^{-z}\)), retaining higher probability in the extremes.

Because the logistic distribution has a larger variance (\(\pi^2/3 \approx 3.29\) compared to \(1.00\)), its standard deviation is \(\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{3}} \approx 1.81\). In practice, this difference produces a universal empirical rule of thumb:

PRACTICAL RULE OF THUMB · COEFFICIENT SCALING
\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{logit}} \approx 1.6 \times \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{probit}} \]

Estimated Logit coefficients are consistently about \(1.6\) to \(1.7\) times larger than Probit coefficients estimated on the identical dataset. This reflects the difference in scale normalization, not economic magnitude.

⚠️ Common Student Mistake
MISCONCEPTION: "Because Logit coefficients are 1.6 times larger than Probit coefficients, the Logit model implies a substantially larger economic impact."
CORRECTION: Once coefficients are transformed into predicted probabilities \(G(\mathbf{x}'\boldsymbol{\beta})\) or marginal effects, Probit and Logit produce almost identical empirical results across typical ranges of data. The \(1.6\) ratio is simply an artifact of the scale identification convention.

Quick Knowledge Check

Question: Why do Probit and Logit models automatically avoid predicted probabilities below 0 or above 1?

Consider the mathematical properties of cumulative distribution functions (CDFs) as the linear index \(z\) approaches \(-\infty\) and \(+\infty\).

Solution: Both \(\Phi(z)\) and \(\Lambda(z)\) are legitimate cumulative distribution functions whose mathematical codomains are strictly bounded within \((0, 1)\). As \(z \to -\infty\), probabilities approach \(0\) asymptotically; as \(z \to +\infty\), probabilities approach \(1\) asymptotically. Thus, negative probabilities and values greater than \(1\) are mathematically impossible by construction.

Lesson 16.2 Summary

Cumulative distribution functions provide an elegant mathematical solution to the boundary dilemma of the Linear Probability Model:

  • CDF Mapping: By passing the linear index \(\mathbf{x}'\boldsymbol{\beta}\) through a CDF \(G(\cdot)\), probabilities are strictly bounded inside \([0, 1]\).
  • Probit: Uses the Gaussian normal CDF \(\Phi(z)\); requires numerical integration and adopts \(\sigma^2 = 1\) as an identifying normalization.
  • Logit: Uses the logistic CDF \(\Lambda(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}\); features a closed-form formula and linearizes the log-odds ratio.
  • Empirical Equivalence: Except in extreme tails where the logistic distribution's heavier tails matter, Probit and Logit produce almost identical substantive economic findings, with \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{logit}} \approx 1.6 \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{probit}}\).
