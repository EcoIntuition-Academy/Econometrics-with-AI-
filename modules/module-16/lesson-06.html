LESSON 16.6 Asymptotic Inference & Synthesis

MLE Properties, Variance & Inference

Explore the large-sample properties of Maximum Likelihood estimators, derive the Score vector and Fisher Information, conduct asymptotic inference, and review the complete binary choice workflow.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State and interpret the large-sample properties of MLE: consistency, asymptotic normality, and asymptotic efficiency under regularity conditions.
  • Define the Score vector \(\mathbf{s}_i(\boldsymbol{\beta})\) and evaluate the expected Fisher Information matrix \(\mathbf{I}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\).
  • Calculate the asymptotic covariance matrix \(\text{Var}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{ML}}) = \frac{1}{n}\mathbf{I}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\) by inverting the sample Hessian.
  • Construct large-sample Wald confidence intervals and compute asymptotic \(Z\)-test statistics.
  • Synthesize the end-to-end binary choice econometrics workflow from LPM limitations to MLE inference.

1. Large-Sample Properties of Maximum Likelihood Estimators

What makes Maximum Likelihood Estimation the premier estimation method in modern econometrics? Under standard mathematical regularity conditions (correct specification, interior parameter space, smooth differentiability), MLE possesses three foundational asymptotic properties:

PROPERTY 1

Consistency

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{ML}} \xrightarrow{p} \boldsymbol{\beta} \]

As sample size \(n \to \infty\), the probability that \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) differs from the true population vector by any amount converges to zero.

PROPERTY 2

Asymptotic Normality

\[ \sqrt{n}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} - \boldsymbol{\beta}) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{V}) \]

Regardless of finite-sample distribution, the estimation error converges to a multivariate Gaussian distribution centered at zero.

PROPERTY 3

Asymptotic Efficiency

\[ \text{Var}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) \to \text{CRLB} \]

MLE achieves the theoretical Cramér–Rao Lower Bound in large samples; no other consistent estimator has smaller asymptotic variance.

⚠️ Essential Requirement: Regularity Conditions
MISCONCEPTION: "MLE is always consistent and efficient regardless of whether our assumed probability distribution matches reality."
CORRECTION: MLE consistency relies strictly on correct parametric specification of the data-generating process. If the true error distribution is Cauchy or skewed but you specify Probit, the MLE estimator can be asymptotic biased and inconsistent.

2. The Score Vector: Gradient of the Log-Likelihood

Let \(\ell_i(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \ln f(y_i \mid \mathbf{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\beta})\) denote the log-likelihood contribution of observation \(i\). The vector of first partial derivatives with respect to the parameter vector \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) is called the Score:

Gradient Vector Individual Score Function Definition
\[ \mathbf{s}_i(\boldsymbol{\beta}) \equiv \frac{\partial \ell_i(\boldsymbol{\beta})}{\partial \boldsymbol{\beta}} \]
WHERE:
\(\mathbf{s}_i(\boldsymbol{\beta})\) Gradient vector of log-likelihood for unit \(i\); satisfies \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{s}_i(\boldsymbol{\beta})] = \mathbf{0}\) at the true parameter

To find the Maximum Likelihood Estimate for the entire sample, we sum the scores across all \(n\) observations and set the total gradient equal to zero (the First-Order Condition):

First-Order Condition Sample Score Optimization Condition
\[ \mathbf{S}_n(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{ML}}) = \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{s}_i(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{ML}}) = \frac{\partial \ln L(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{ML}})}{\partial \boldsymbol{\beta}} = \mathbf{0} \]
WHERE:
\(\mathbf{S}_n(\boldsymbol{\beta})\) Total sample score; setting it to \(\mathbf{0}\) identifies the stationary peak of the log-likelihood hill

3. Fisher Information & the Expected Hessian

How precisely can we estimate \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\)? The answer depends entirely on the curvature of the log-likelihood function at its peak. The expected negative second derivative is the Fisher Information Matrix:

Curvature Matrix Fisher Information Definition & Equality
\[ \mathbf{I}(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = -\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\partial^2 \ell_i(\boldsymbol{\beta})}{\partial \boldsymbol{\beta} \partial \boldsymbol{\beta}'}\right] = \mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{s}_i(\boldsymbol{\beta})\mathbf{s}_i(\boldsymbol{\beta})'\right] \]
WHERE:
\(\mathbf{I}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\) Fisher Information matrix; represents the average curvature of the log-likelihood function
\(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{s}_i \mathbf{s}_i']\) Information Matrix Equality: under correct specification, negative Hessian equals score variance

Sharply Peaked Likelihood (High Information)

The curve bends sharply down from its peak. Second derivatives are large in magnitude, Fisher Information is high, and the estimator's standard errors are very small and precise.

Flat, Broad Likelihood (Low Information)

The curve is nearly flat around its peak. Second derivatives are close to zero, Fisher Information is low, and standard errors are large and imprecise.

4. Statistical Inference: Inverting the Sample Hessian

In empirical research, we estimate the asymptotic covariance matrix by inverting the observed sample Hessian matrix:

Sample Variance Inverted Hessian Covariance Estimator
\[ \widehat{\text{Avar}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{ML}}) = \left[ -\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\partial^2 \ell_i(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{ML}})}{\partial \boldsymbol{\beta} \partial \boldsymbol{\beta}'} \right]^{-1} = \left( -\mathbf{H}_n(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{ML}}) \right)^{-1} \]
WHERE:
\(\mathbf{H}_n(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\) Sample Hessian matrix of second partial derivatives evaluated at the maximum
\(\text{SE}(\hat{\beta}_j)\) Standard error: square root of the \(j\)-th diagonal element of \((-\mathbf{H}_n)^{-1}\)

Asymptotic Confidence Interval

\[ \hat{\beta}_j \pm z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \text{SE}(\hat{\beta}_j) \]

Constructed using the standard normal distribution (\(z_{0.025} = 1.96\) for 95% confidence).

Asymptotic Z-Test

\[ z = \frac{\hat{\beta}_j - 0}{\text{SE}(\hat{\beta}_j)} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1) \]

Because MLE properties are asymptotic, we use the standard normal \(z\)-test rather than Student's \(t\)-distribution.

6. Comprehensive Module 16 Review & Synthesis

Across Module 16, we traced the evolution of binary choice econometrics from the linear model to nonlinear maximum likelihood estimation:

Dimension Linear Probability Model (LPM) Probit Model Logit Model
Functional Form \(\Pr(Y=1 \mid X) = X\beta\) \(\Pr(Y=1 \mid X) = \Phi(X\beta)\) \(\Pr(Y=1 \mid X) = \Lambda(X\beta)\)
Range of Predictions Unbounded: can predict \(<0\) or \(>1\) Strictly bounded: \((0, 1)\) Strictly bounded: \((0, 1)\)
Marginal Effects Constant: \(\beta_j\) Nonlinear: \(\phi(X\beta)\beta_j\) Nonlinear: \(P(1-P)\beta_j\)
Error Variance Inherent heteroskedasticity \(p(1-p)\) Normalized: \(\sigma^2 = 1\) Normalized: \(\sigma^2 = \pi^2/3 \approx 3.29\)
Estimation Method OLS with robust SEs Maximum Likelihood (MLE) Maximum Likelihood (MLE)
Primary Tradeoff Simple coefficients, bad boundary predictions Gaussian utility, no closed-form integral Closed-form odds ratio, slightly fatter tails
PILLAR 1

Binary Response Basics

The LPM offers simple direct interpretation but suffers from bounded range violations and inherent heteroskedasticity.

PILLAR 2

Nonlinear Models & Latent Variables

Probit (\(\Phi\)) and Logit (\(\Lambda\)) map index values into bounded probabilities, representing latent propensities crossing a threshold.

PILLAR 3

Marginal Effects & Interpretation

Raw coefficients cannot be interpreted as changes in probability. Report Average Marginal Effects (AME) or apply the divide-by-four rule for Logit.

PILLAR 4

Maximum Likelihood & Inference

MLE provides consistent, asymptotically normal, and efficient estimates. Inverting the sample Hessian provides standard errors for Wald inference.

Quick Knowledge Check

Question: Why can we not generally interpret a Probit or Logit coefficient \(\beta_j\) directly as the change in \(\Pr(Y=1 \mid X)\) resulting from a 1-unit increase in \(x_j\)?

Remember the chain rule: what is the derivative of the nonlinear CDF \(G(X\beta)\) with respect to \(x_j\)?

Solution: Because \(G(\cdot)\) is a nonlinear function, the marginal effect is \(\frac{\partial P}{\partial x_j} = g(X\beta)\beta_j\). The coefficient \(\beta_j\) must be scaled by the probability density \(g(X\beta)\), which varies across individuals depending on their baseline regressor values.

Lesson 16.6 Summary

Maximum Likelihood Estimation crowns the microeconometric binary response framework:

  • Large-Sample Optimality: Under standard regularity conditions, MLE estimators are consistent, asymptotically normally distributed, and achieve the Cramér–Rao Lower Bound (asymptotic efficiency).
  • Information Matrix Equality: Expected outer product of scores equals the expected negative Hessian matrix under correct specification.
  • Asymptotic Covariance: Inverting the sample Hessian yields the asymptotic covariance matrix \(\widehat{\text{Avar}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) = (-\mathbf{H}_n)^{-1}\).
  • End-to-End Synthesis: From the linear probability model to Probit, Logit, latent thresholds, and MLE inference, you now possess the complete toolkit for modern empirical binary outcome analysis!
