LESSON 13.6 Precision & Practice

Covariate Adjustment, Precision & Best Practices

In randomized field experiments, random assignment ensures identification, so why control for pre-treatment covariates? Discover how baseline variables absorb residual variation to boost precision, avoid dangerous post-treatment controls, and master the end-to-end experimental workflow.

LEARNING OBJECTIVES
  • Formulate the covariate-adjusted regression model \(Y_i = \alpha + \beta_1 T_i + \beta_2 X_i + \varepsilon_i\).
  • Contrast the purpose of covariates in observational studies (identification) vs. experiments (precision).
  • Explain how predictive baseline covariates reduce residual variance and narrow standard errors.
  • State the fundamental rule against controlling for post-treatment variables or colliders.
  • Synthesize the four pillars of field experiment best practices from pre-registration to inference.

1. Covariate Adjustment: Precision over Identification

Consider estimating treatment effect \(\beta_1\) by augmenting the regression with pre-treatment covariate vector \(X_i\):

COVARIATE-ADJUSTED REGRESSION
$$Y_i = \alpha + \beta_1 T_i + \beta_2 X_i + \varepsilon_i$$
where \(X_i\) represents baseline characteristics strictly measured before treatment assignment.
WITHOUT PRE-TREATMENT COVARIATES

Unbiased but Higher Variance

The bivariate regression \(\hat{\beta}_1 = \bar{Y}_1 - \bar{Y}_0\) is protected from systematic selection bias by the randomized design. However, the residual disturbance \(\sigma^2\) includes all variation in \(Y\) explained by \(X\), leading to wider confidence intervals.

WITH PREDICTIVE BASELINE COVARIATES

Sharper Precision & Narrower CIs

Because \(X_i\) is pre-treatment, it cannot be affected by \(T\). Adding \(X_i\) absorbs predictable outcome variation, reducing the residual variance: \(\sigma^2_{\varepsilon} \approx \sigma^2(1 - R_X^2)\). This directly reduces \(\text{SE}(\hat{\beta}_1)\) and amplifies statistical power.

METHODOLOGICAL DISTINCTION

Identification vs. Efficiency

In observational econometrics, control variables are required to achieve identification (satisfying unconfoundedness). In randomized field experiments, random assignment provides identification by design; pre-treatment covariates are included primarily for precision.

2. What NOT to Control For: Post-Treatment Variables

CRITICAL DANGER · POST-TREATMENT CONTROLS

Never Control for Post-Treatment Variables

Variables measured after treatment assignment may themselves be affected by the intervention. Controlling for an intermediate outcome blocks part of the causal mechanism and introduces selection bias (bad controls).

METHODOLOGICAL CAUTION · COLLIDER BIAS

Conditioning on Colliders Induces Spurious Correlation

If a post-treatment variable is mutually influenced by treatment and an unobserved outcome shock, conditioning on it opens a non-causal path (collider stratification), actively corrupting an otherwise valid experiment.

3. Four Pillars of Field-Experiment Best Practice

1. PRE-REGISTER

Specify primary outcomes, subgroup analyses, and regression specifications before collecting or unblinding experimental data.

2. BLOCK WHEN USEFUL

Use stratified randomization on key prognostic baseline attributes to guarantee balance and absorb between-group variance.

3. RESPECT THE DESIGN

Account for strata indicators in estimation. Analyze the data in alignment with how random assignment was administered.

4. COVARIATES FOR PRECISION

Select baseline covariates that predict the outcome to reduce residual variance and narrow standard errors.

INTERNAL VALIDITY PRINCIPLE

Proper Random Assignment Supports Internal Validity

Proper random assignment supports internal validity by breaking systematic treatment selection under the experimental design. However, researchers must remain vigilant regarding downstream experimental threats such as differential attrition, non-compliance, and spillovers.

Module 13 Concept Dictionary

Field Experiment: Real-world study with randomly assigned interventions.
Treatment Group: Units receiving the experimental intervention.
Control Group: Unexposed counterfactual benchmark units.
Selection Bias: Distortion caused by self-selection into treatment.
Random Assignment: Lottery assignment breaking link to omitted factors.
Difference in Means: \(\hat{\beta}_1 = \bar{Y}_1 - \bar{Y}_0\), the OLS slope.
Power: Probability of detecting a true nonzero effect.
Sample Size (4n): Quadrupling group sample size cuts SE in half.
Randomization Dist.: Distribution generated by reassigning treatments.
Sharp Null: Zero effect for every single individual.
Blocked Randomization: Lottery conducted separately within subgroups.
Strata: Homogeneous baseline subgroups for blocking.
Fisher Exact Test: Hypergeometric test on \(2 \times 2\) tables.
Balance: Comparability of pre-treatment distributions.
Standardized Diff.: Mean gap normalized by pooled standard deviation.
Pre-Treatment Covariate: Baseline variable measured before assignment.
Post-Treatment Variable: Variable affected by treatment; never control.

6. Quick Check

QUICK CHECK

Why should researchers NOT control for variables measured after treatment is assigned?

HINT: Consider whether the variable itself could be an outcome of the treatment or a collider.

ANSWER: Because variables measured after treatment may themselves be affected by treatment, and conditioning on them introduces selection bias or collider stratification bias.

WHY? Controlling for an intermediate outcome blocks part of the causal channel linking treatment to the final outcome. Furthermore, if the post-treatment variable is influenced by unobserved factors that also affect the outcome, conditioning on it opens a non-causal spurious pathway (collider bias), ruining the experimental identification guaranteed by randomization.

TAKEAWAYS

What You Should Now Understand

  • In experiments, pre-treatment covariates are included to improve precision rather than achieve identification.
  • Predictive baseline variables absorb outcome variation, lowering residual variance \(\sigma^2\) and shrinking SEs.
  • Never control for post-treatment variables or intermediate outcomes, which introduce bad control bias.
  • Conditioning on variables affected by treatment can open spurious non-causal paths via collider bias.
  • Adhere to the four pillars: pre-register, block when useful, respect strata in analysis, and control for precision.
