LESSON 13.1 Field Experiments

Field Experiments, Selection Bias & Random Assignment

Observational evaluations are routinely confounded when individuals choose their own treatments. Learn how randomized field experiments eliminate systematic selection by design, breaking the statistical correlation between treatment status and omitted factors.

LEARNING OBJECTIVES
  • Define a field experiment and distinguish the roles of treatment and control groups.
  • Explain the mechanism of self-selection and why it confounds causal attribution in observational data.
  • Formulate the selection bias regression model and show how omitted factors enter the composite error.
  • Contrast causal pathways under self-selection versus random assignment.
  • Recognize that random assignment prevents systematic selection by design, while finite samples can still exhibit chance imbalance.

1. What is a Field Experiment?

CORE DEFINITION

Field Experiment

A field experiment is an empirical study conducted in a natural, real-world setting where a specific intervention—the treatment—is randomly assigned to a subset of the sample, while the remaining observations form the unexposed control group.

In empirical economics and policy analysis, common randomized interventions include:

Diet & Health Programs

Subsidized nutrition or fitness interventions offered to randomly selected households.

Advertisements & Nudges

Marketing campaigns or informational mailings sent to randomly chosen customer segments.

Neighborhood Mobility

Housing vouchers provided via lottery to help families move to lower-poverty neighborhoods.

Discounts & Subsidies

Price reductions or credit offers randomly distributed to test price elasticity of demand.

Study-Abroad Opportunities

Fellowships or travel grants assigned by lottery to measure labor market returns to international study.

2. The Core Challenge: Self-Selection & Omitted Factors

INTUITION

Why Observational Comparisons Deceive

In the absence of an experimental protocol, individuals choose whether or not to adopt a treatment based on expected costs, personal preferences, and unobserved attributes. Consequently, treated individuals and untreated individuals systematically differ before any treatment occurs.

Education & Earnings

People who acquire more education may possess higher baseline ability, family wealth, or ambition. A simple earnings gap reflects both schooling and underlying ability.

Exercise & Longevity

People who choose regular exercise may already be healthier, smoke less, and have superior diets. Their better health cannot be attributed solely to the gym.

Weight-Loss Programs

Voluntary enrollees possess exceptionally strong personal motivation at the time of signup. Non-participants differ in baseline readiness to alter habits.

Tax Audits

Tax agencies select returns that display red flags for noncompliance. Finding high fraud rates among audited returns does not imply audits cause fraud.

Selection Bias in the Regression Framework

Consider the data-generating process for outcome \(y\), where \(T\) indicates treatment and \(x_2\) represents an unobserved or omitted baseline characteristic (such as ability or motivation):

TRUE DATA-GENERATING PROCESS
$$y = \alpha + \beta_1 T + \beta_2 x_2 + \varepsilon$$
where \(T \in \{0,1\}\) is the treatment indicator, \(x_2\) is the omitted characteristic, and \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid T, x_2] = 0\).

When an analyst estimates a simple bivariate regression omitting \(x_2\), the omitted factor is subsumed into the composite error term \(\omega\):

ESTIMATED REGRESSION WITH COMPOSITE ERROR
$$\omega = \beta_2 x_2 + \varepsilon \implies y = \alpha + \beta_1 T + \omega$$
If individuals self-select into treatment based on \(x_2\), then \(\text{Cov}(T, x_2) \neq 0\), which guarantees \(\text{Cov}(T, \omega) \neq 0\).
KEY ECONOMETRIC RESULT

Because \(T\) is correlated with the composite regression error \(\omega\), ordinary least squares (OLS) attribution to \(\beta_1\) is contaminated by selection bias: $$\hat{\beta}_1^{\text{OLS}} \xrightarrow{p} \beta_1 + \beta_2 \frac{\text{Cov}(T, x_2)}{\text{Var}(T)}$$ The estimated coefficient conflates the true causal treatment effect \(\beta_1\) with baseline differences in \(x_2\).

3. Visual Intuition: Breaking the Selection Link

MODE 1 · OBSERVATIONAL SELF-SELECTION

Confounded by Baseline Attributes

Baseline Ability / Motivation (\(x_2\))
↙                    ↘
Treatment Choice (\(T\))  →  Realized Outcome (\(y\))

Because \(x_2\) drives both treatment adoption and outcomes, treated and untreated units differ prior to the intervention. Causal attribution fails.

MODE 2 · RANDOM ASSIGNMENT

Systematic Selection Broken by Design

Physical Randomizer (Coin / RNG)
↓
Treatment Status (\(T\))  →  Realized Outcome (\(y\))
(Baseline \(x_2\) no longer determines assignment)

Random assignment forces \(\text{Cov}(T, x_2) = 0\) in expectation. Baseline characteristics are no longer systematically used to determine assignment.

IMPORTANT DISTINCTION

Design Exogeneity vs. Finite-Sample Realization

Randomization removes systematic selection into treatment by construction. However, in any realized finite sample, small differences in baseline characteristics can still arise purely through random chance. This reality motivates baseline balance testing (examined in Lesson 13.5) and precision-improving covariate adjustments (examined in Lesson 13.6).

5. Quick Check

QUICK CHECK

Why can self-selection make the treatment coefficient in an observational regression biased?

HINT: Consider how individual choices link treatment status to unobserved characteristics that independently affect the outcome.

ANSWER: Because individuals who select into treatment often differ in outcome-relevant characteristics, making treatment correlated with the composite regression error.

WHY? When an unobserved factor \(x_2\) influences both the decision to adopt treatment \(T\) and the outcome \(y\), it enters the composite error \(\omega = \beta_2 x_2 + \varepsilon\). As a result, \(\text{Cov}(T, \omega) \ne 0\), violating OLS exogeneity and conflating the true treatment effect with pre-existing selection differences.

TAKEAWAYS

What You Should Now Understand

  • Field experiments test interventions in natural settings with randomized treatment and control groups.
  • Self-selection causes treatment status to correlate with unobserved attributes that also influence outcomes.
  • In observational regressions, omitted baseline factors enter the composite error, biasing OLS estimates.
  • Random assignment severs the systematic connection between baseline traits and treatment status.
  • Randomization guarantees exogeneity in expectation, though finite samples may still show chance imbalance.
