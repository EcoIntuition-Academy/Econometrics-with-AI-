LESSON 13.2 Experimental Precision

Power Calculations, Sample Size & Precision

Randomization guarantees unbiasedness, but how large must an experiment be to reliably detect a meaningful treatment effect? Derive the variance of the treatment effect and master the mathematical relationship linking sample size to experimental precision.

LEARNING OBJECTIVES
  • Formulate the treatment-effect regression model and write the difference-in-means estimator.
  • Explain the core question of statistical power: determining data requirements to detect nonzero effects.
  • Derive the variance formula \(\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_1) = 2\sigma^2 / n\) under equal allocation.
  • Explain why standard errors shrink with the inverse square root of sample size per group (\(1/\sqrt{n}\)).
  • Apply the quadrupling rule: prove why sample size must quadruple to cut the standard error in half.

1. The Treatment-Effect Regression

In a randomized experiment with an intervention indicator \(T_i\), the baseline linear model is specified as:

EXPERIMENTAL SPECIFICATION
$$Y_i = \alpha + \beta_1 T_i + \varepsilon_i$$
where \(T_i = 1\) if subject \(i\) receives treatment, \(T_i = 0\) if assigned to control, and \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_i \mid T_i] = 0\).

Under this standard binary setup, the OLS estimator of \(\beta_1\) simplifies to the intuitive sample difference in group means:

\(\bar{Y}_1\)

\(\hat{\beta}_1\)
\(\hat{\beta}_1 = \bar{Y}_1 - \bar{Y}_0\)

2. The Design Problem: Sample Size & Power

DESIGN QUESTION

How Large a Sample Do We Need?

While random assignment protects against selection bias, an experiment with too few participants will yield estimates with massive sampling variability. Statistical power calculations address how much data are needed to detect a true underlying effect with adequate precision and confidence.

Precision vs. Power Principles

  • Larger sample sizes (\(n\)) directly enhance precision by reducing the standard error of the estimated treatment effect.
  • Smaller standard errors (\(\text{SE}\)) increase the statistical power to distinguish genuine treatment effects from zero.
  • Outcome variability (\(\sigma^2\)) acts in the opposite direction: more volatile outcomes require larger samples to achieve the same precision.

3. Derivation Journey: Treatment-Effect Variance

DERIVATION JOURNEY

Variance of the Difference in Means

Demonstrate mathematically why experimental precision scales with sample size per group under equal allocation.

1

Express Estimator as Difference in Means

$$\hat{\beta}_1 = \bar{Y}_1 - \bar{Y}_0$$
Setup The OLS slope in a bivariate binary regression equals the difference between the treated sample mean and the control sample mean.
2

Apply Variance Operator across Independent Groups

$$\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_1) = \text{Var}(\bar{Y}_1 - \bar{Y}_0) = \text{Var}(\bar{Y}_1) + \text{Var}(\bar{Y}_0)$$
Why? Under independent randomized assignment across distinct individuals, \(\text{Cov}(\bar{Y}_1, \bar{Y}_0) = 0\). The variance of the difference equals the sum of the group variances.
3

Evaluate Individual Group Variances

$$\text{Var}(\bar{Y}_1) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}, \quad \text{Var}(\bar{Y}_0) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}$$
Assumption Each group contains \(n\) independent observations with common outcome disturbance variance \(\sigma^2\).
4

Sum Group Variances

$$\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_1) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} + \frac{\sigma^2}{n} = \frac{2\sigma^2}{n}$$
Key Result The variance of the estimated treatment effect decreases directly in proportion to \(1/n\).

4. The Quadrupling Rule for Standard Errors

Taking the square root of \(\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_1)\) yields the estimator's standard error:

STANDARD ERROR FORMULA
$$\text{SE}(\hat{\beta}_1) = \sqrt{\frac{2\sigma^2}{n}} = \frac{\sigma\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{n}}$$
Because \(n\) enters under the square root in the denominator, the standard error decays at rate \(1/\sqrt{n}\).
THE QUADRUPLING RULE

To Halve the Standard Error, Quadruple Sample Size

Suppose sample size per group is multiplied by \(4\) (\(n \to 4n\)). Then:

$$\text{SE}_{\text{new}} = \sqrt{\frac{2\sigma^2}{4n}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4}}\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma^2}{n}} = \frac{1}{2}\text{SE}_{\text{original}}$$

Halving the confidence interval width requires four times as many participants per group—a fundamental principle of experimental economics.

6. Quick Check

QUICK CHECK

If you want to halve the standard error of the treatment-effect estimate under this equal-allocation setup, how must the sample size per group change?

HINT: Recall how \(n\) enters into the standard error formula \(\text{SE}(\hat{\beta}_1) = \sqrt{2\sigma^2/n}\).

ANSWER: Quadruple the sample size per group (\(4n\)).

WHY? Because the standard error scales inversely with the square root of sample size per group (\(\text{SE} \propto 1/\sqrt{n}\)), cutting \(\text{SE}\) in half requires a factor of \(1/2\) on the right-hand side. Squaring this factor indicates that the denominator inside the radical must increase by a factor of \(4\) (\(\sqrt{4n} = 2\sqrt{n}\)).

TAKEAWAYS

What You Should Now Understand

  • Under equal allocation, the treatment effect estimator is the difference in group sample means.
  • Independent randomized groups ensure \(\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_1) = \text{Var}(\bar{Y}_1) + \text{Var}(\bar{Y}_0) = 2\sigma^2/n\).
  • Experimental variance decreases directly as \(1/n\), while standard error decreases as \(1/\sqrt{n}\).
  • To cut the standard error in half, the sample size per group must quadruple (\(4n\)).
  • Increasing sample size exhibits diminishing marginal precision gains as \(n\) becomes very large.
