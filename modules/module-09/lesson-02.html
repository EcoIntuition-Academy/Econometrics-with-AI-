LESSON 9.2 Testing & Known-ρ GLS

Detecting Autocorrelation & GLS with Known ρ

Explore the classic Durbin–Watson diagnostic test for first-order serial correlation, understand its mathematical link to the sample autocorrelation coefficient, and derive the Generalized Least Squares quasi-differencing transformation when \(\rho\) is known.

LEARNING OBJECTIVES
  • State and compute the Durbin–Watson (DW) test statistic using OLS sample residuals.
  • Explain the intuition behind the DW numerator (squared residual changes) versus denominator (total residual variance).
  • Apply the source decision benchmarks: \(\text{DW} \approx 2\) (no correlation), \(\text{DW} < 2\) (positive), and \(\text{DW} > 2\) (negative).
  • Derive the large-sample asymptotic heuristic link \(\text{DW} \approx 2(1 - \widehat{\rho})\).
  • Formulate the GLS quasi-differencing transformation to convert AR(1) disturbances into spherical innovations.

1. Part A: The Durbin–Watson Diagnostic Test

Before applying an estimator adjustment, econometricians test whether residuals exhibit first-order serial correlation. The classic diagnostic in time series econometrics is the Durbin–Watson (DW) test:

DURBIN–WATSON TEST STATISTIC
$$\text{DW} = \frac{\sum_{t=2}^n (\widehat{\varepsilon}_t - \widehat{\varepsilon}_{t-1})^2}{\sum_{t=1}^n \widehat{\varepsilon}_t^2}$$

where \(\widehat{\varepsilon}_t = y_t - \mathbf{x}_t'\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{OLS}}\) are the conventional OLS sample residuals.

MATHEMATICAL INTUITION · RATIO OF CHANGES TO TOTAL VARIATION

The Durbin–Watson statistic is a normalized comparison between how much residuals change from one period to the next versus their overall squared magnitude:

Numerator: \(\sum_{t=2}^n (\widehat{\varepsilon}_t - \widehat{\varepsilon}_{t-1})^2\)

Measures step-to-step volatility. If adjacent residuals are positively correlated, \(\widehat{\varepsilon}_t\) and \(\widehat{\varepsilon}_{t-1}\) are close in value, making their difference small and shrinking the numerator.

Denominator: \(\sum_{t=1}^n \widehat{\varepsilon}_t^2\)

The total sum of squared residuals (SSR). Normalizes the statistic so that scale variations in the original data cancel out.

Source Decision Benchmarks

\(\text{DW} \approx 2\)

No Autocorrelation

Errors behave like independent white noise. Conventional OLS inference is valid.

\(\text{DW} < 2\)

Positive Autocorrelation

Consecutive residuals are similar (\(\widehat{\rho} > 0\)), shrinking numerator differences.

\(\text{DW} > 2\)

Negative Autocorrelation

Consecutive residuals alternate signs (\(\widehat{\rho} < 0\)), inflating numerator differences.

LARGE-SAMPLE ASYMPTOTIC HEURISTIC

In large samples, expanding the numerator yields the direct relation between the Durbin–Watson statistic and the sample autocorrelation coefficient:

$$\text{DW} \approx 2(1 - \widehat{\rho})$$

Notice: If \(\widehat{\rho} = 0 \implies \text{DW} \approx 2\). If \(\widehat{\rho} = +1 \implies \text{DW} \approx 0\). If \(\widehat{\rho} = -1 \implies \text{DW} \approx 4\).

3. Part B: GLS Quasi-Differencing with Known ρ

When autocorrelation is present, we can purge the serial dependence through quasi-differencing if the parameter \(\rho\) is known.

DERIVATION JOURNEY

THE 6 STEPS OF QUASI-DIFFERENCING

GOAL

Transform the time-series regression to purge first-order serial correlation and recover spherical disturbances when \(\rho\) is known.

1

Step 1: Write Current-Period Regression Model

Start with the standard linear regression model observed at period \(t\):

$$y_t = \mathbf{x}_t'\boldsymbol{\beta} + \varepsilon_t$$
2

Step 2: Write Lagged Regression Model

Write the identical relationship for the immediately preceding time period \(t-1\):

$$y_{t-1} = \mathbf{x}_{t-1}'\boldsymbol{\beta} + \varepsilon_{t-1}$$
3

Step 3: Multiply Lagged Model by \(\rho\)

Scale both sides of the lagged regression by the known autocorrelation parameter \(\rho\):

$$\rho y_{t-1} = \rho \mathbf{x}_{t-1}'\boldsymbol{\beta} + \rho \varepsilon_{t-1}$$
4

Step 4: Subtract Scaled Lagged Model from Current Model

Subtract the scaled lagged equation from the current-period equation:

$$y_t - \rho y_{t-1} = (\mathbf{x}_t - \rho \mathbf{x}_{t-1})'\boldsymbol{\beta} + (\varepsilon_t - \rho \varepsilon_{t-1})$$
5

Step 5: Substitute AR(1) Error Definition: \(\varepsilon_t - \rho \varepsilon_{t-1} = u_t\)

Recognize that the parenthesized disturbance difference exactly equals the white-noise innovation:

$$y_t - \rho y_{t-1} = (\mathbf{x}_t - \rho \mathbf{x}_{t-1})'\boldsymbol{\beta} + u_t$$
WHY? By definition of AR(1), \(u_t = \varepsilon_t - \rho \varepsilon_{t-1}\) is white noise with \(\mathbb{E}[u_t u_s] = 0\) for \(t \neq s\).
6

Step 6: Define Transformed Variables & Apply OLS

Define transformed quasi-differenced variables \(y_t^* = y_t - \rho y_{t-1}\) and \(\mathbf{x}_t^* = \mathbf{x}_t - \rho \mathbf{x}_{t-1}\):

$$y_t^* = \mathbf{x}_t^{*\prime}\boldsymbol{\beta} + u_t, \quad u_t \sim \text{iid}(0, \sigma^2)$$
RESULT Applying OLS to the transformed equation produces the GLS estimator, which is BLUE under known \(\rho\).

THE BIG PICTURE · WHAT DID THE TRANSFORMATION ACCOMPLISH?

The transformation does not change the structural slope vector \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\). Rather, it transforms the data representation so that the transformed disturbance term behaves like the spherical innovation \(u_t\). Because \(u_t\) satisfies Gauss–Markov assumptions (\(\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{u}) = \sigma^2 \mathbf{I}\)), standard OLS applied to the transformed variables is BLUE!

MODULE 8 CONNECTION · MATRIX GLS ESTIMATOR

Quasi-differencing is the algebraic equivalent of the matrix Generalized Least Squares estimator:

$$\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{GLS}} = (\mathbf{X}'\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{-1}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}'\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{-1}\mathbf{y}$$

When \(\rho\) is known, \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\) is completely determined, making this estimator Best Linear Unbiased (BLUE).

CONCEPT CHECK

4. Quick Check 9.2

CHECK 9.2 Durbin–Watson & Quasi-Differencing
Question: In a time-series regression with 100 observations, the OLS residuals yield a Durbin–Watson statistic of \(\text{DW} = 0.50\). What does this imply about the residual autocorrelation, and what would the quasi-differencing transformation look like?
HINT: Use the asymptotic heuristic link \(\text{DW} \approx 2(1 - \widehat{\rho})\) to solve for the approximate sample correlation \(\widehat{\rho}\).

ANSWER: \(\text{DW} = 0.50\) is substantially less than 2, providing strong evidence of positive autocorrelation. Solving the heuristic gives \(\widehat{\rho} \approx 1 - \frac{0.50}{2} = 0.75\).

WHY? The quasi-differenced regression subtracts \(0.75\) times the lagged value from each current observation: \(y_t - 0.75 y_{t-1} = (\mathbf{x}_t - 0.75 \mathbf{x}_{t-1})'\boldsymbol{\beta} + u_t\). This subtraction eliminates the \(0.75\)-degree temporal persistence, restoring white-noise innovations \(u_t\).

📋

What You Should Now Understand

  • Durbin–Watson Formula: \(\text{DW} = \frac{\sum (\widehat{\varepsilon}_t - \widehat{\varepsilon}_{t-1})^2}{\sum \widehat{\varepsilon}_t^2}\) compares squared period-to-period changes against total residual variance.
  • Source Decision Rules: \(\text{DW} \approx 2\) indicates zero serial correlation; \(\text{DW} < 2\) indicates positive autocorrelation; \(\text{DW} > 2\) indicates negative autocorrelation.
  • Asymptotic Heuristic: In large samples, \(\text{DW} \approx 2(1 - \widehat{\rho})\), establishing a direct relationship between DW and the first-order serial correlation coefficient.
  • Quasi-Differencing: Subtracting \(\rho\) times the lagged regression from the current regression purges AR(1) persistence, leaving uncorrelated white-noise innovations \(u_t\).
  • Known-\(\rho\) GLS Optimality: Applying OLS to transformed variables \(y_t^* = y_t - \rho y_{t-1}\) and \(\mathbf{x}_t^* = \mathbf{x}_t - \rho \mathbf{x}_{t-1}\) achieves the minimum variance bound (BLUE).
