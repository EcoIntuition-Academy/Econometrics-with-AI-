The Hausman Test & Panel-Model Choice

Formulating the specification test statistic comparing Fixed Effects and Random Effects, diagnosing power limitations, and navigating the panel estimation decision tree.

LEARNING OBJECTIVES
  • Understand the econometric logic of the Hausman (1978) specification test comparing consistent vs. efficient estimators.
  • Derive the quadratic form test statistic \(H = \mathbf{q}' [\widehat{\text{Var}}(\mathbf{q})]^{-1} \mathbf{q}\) and its asymptotic \(\chi^2(k)\) distribution.
  • Identify critical caveats: why non-rejection of \(H_0\) does not prove unobserved heterogeneity is orthogonal to regressors, and when finite-sample power is low.
  • Synthesize the entire Module 10 curriculum into an actionable econometric decision framework for choosing between Pooled OLS, RE, FE, and FD with cluster-robust inference.
1

The Hausman Specification Principle

When estimating panel data, applied researchers face a fundamental tension between Fixed Effects (FE) and Random Effects (RE):

Fixed Effects Estimator (\(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{FE}}\))

Always consistent regardless of whether \(\text{Cov}(c_i, \mathbf{x}_{it}) = \mathbf{0}\) or \(\text{Cov}(c_i, \mathbf{x}_{it}) \neq \mathbf{0}\), but less efficient when orthogonality holds, and cannot estimate time-invariant regressors.

Random Effects Estimator (\(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{RE}}\))

Fully efficient (BLUE/GLS) under the null hypothesis \(H_0: \text{Cov}(c_i, \mathbf{x}_{it}) = \mathbf{0}\), but inconsistent under the alternative \(H_1: \text{Cov}(c_i, \mathbf{x}_{it}) \neq \mathbf{0}\).

The Hausman Core Question: Do \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{FE}}\) and \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{RE}}\) differ by more than what would be expected from random sampling variation alone? \[ \mathbf{q} \equiv \widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{FE}} - \widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{RE}} \] Under \(H_0\), both estimators converge to the true parameter \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\), so \(\text{plim}(\mathbf{q}) = \mathbf{0}\). Under \(H_1\), \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{RE}}\) diverges due to omitted variable bias, making \(\mathbf{q}\) systematically non-zero.
2

The Hausman Test Statistic

To test whether the difference vector \(\mathbf{q}\) is statistically indistinguishable from zero, we require its sampling variance \(\text{Var}(\mathbf{q})\). By elementary probability: \[ \text{Var}(\mathbf{q}) = \text{Var}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{FE}}) + \text{Var}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{RE}}) - 2\text{Cov}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{FE}}, \widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{RE}}) \]

💡 Hausman's (1978) Fundamental Lemma

Under the null hypothesis where \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{RE}}\) is the asymptotically efficient estimator, the covariance between the efficient estimator and the difference between itself and any other consistent estimator is identically zero: \[ \text{Cov}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{RE}}, \widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{FE}} - \widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{RE}}) = \mathbf{0} \implies \text{Cov}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{FE}}, \widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{RE}}) = \text{Var}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{RE}}) \] Substituting this back into the variance of \(\mathbf{q}\) yields the famous result: \[ \text{Var}(\mathbf{q}) = \text{Var}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{FE}}) - \text{Var}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{RE}}) \]

THE HAUSMAN TEST STATISTIC FORMULA
\[ H = (\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{FE}} - \widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{RE}})' \left[\widehat{\text{Var}}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{FE}}) - \widehat{\text{Var}}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{RE}})\right]^{-1} (\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{FE}} - \widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{RE}}) \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2(k) \]

where \(k\) is the number of time-varying explanatory variables compared between the two models. If \(H > \chi^2_{1-\alpha}(k)\), we reject \(H_0\) and conclude that Random Effects is inconsistent.

3

Critical Caveats: Low Power & Non-Positive Definiteness

Applied economists must interpret Hausman test outcomes with extreme methodological caution:

⚠️ 1. Non-Rejection Does NOT Equal Orthogonality

The Hausman test frequently exhibits low statistical power in finite samples, particularly when the correlation between \(c_i\) and \(\mathbf{x}_{it}\) is weak or when within-unit variation is small relative to between-unit variation. Failing to reject \(H_0\) does NOT prove that \(c_i\) is uncorrelated with \(\mathbf{x}_{it}\); it only means the data lacked sufficient power to detect the discrepancy.

⚠️ 2. Finite-Sample Matrix Inversion Failures

In finite samples, the difference matrix \([\widehat{\text{Var}}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{FE}}) - \widehat{\text{Var}}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{RE}})]\) is not guaranteed to be positive definite, occasionally yielding negative test statistics or inversion crashes. In modern practice, researchers often substitute artificial regression tests (Mundlak approach or Wooldridge auxiliary regressions) which are numerically stable and robust to clustering.

4

The Complete Panel-Model Decision Guide

Follow this structured econometric workflow when deciding among panel estimators:

STAGE 1: EVALUATE UNOBSERVED HETEROGENEITY (c_i)

Is \(c_i\) likely correlated with any regressor \(\mathbf{x}_{it}\)? In microeconomic settings (ability, managerial skill, firm culture), correlation is virtually ubiquitous.
→ If correlation is likely, Pooled OLS and Random Effects are inconsistent. Proceed to purge \(c_i\).

STAGE 2: CHOOSE BETWEEN FIXED EFFECTS & FIRST DIFFERENCES

Both eliminate \(c_i\). If \(T = 2\), they are algebraically identical. If \(T > 2\):
• Choose Fixed Effects if idiosyncratic errors \(\varepsilon_{it}\) are serially uncorrelated (FE is BLUE).
• Choose First Differences if idiosyncratic errors \(\varepsilon_{it}\) exhibit strong persistence or unit roots (differencing purges the random walk).

STAGE 3: SPECIFY CLUSTER-ROBUST INFERENCE

Always account for within-unit serial correlation and heteroskedasticity.
• If number of clusters \(G \ge 40\) → Apply standard Cluster-Robust Standard Errors.
• If number of clusters is small (\(G < 30\)) → Apply the Wild Cluster Bootstrap (Cameron et al. 2008, Roodman et al. 2019) to avoid severe downward bias.

6

Concept Check

CHECK 10.5 Model Specification Choice

If the Hausman test decisively rejects the null hypothesis at \(p < 0.001\), which estimator should the econometrician select, and why?

Hint: Which estimator maintains consistency regardless of whether \(c_i\) is correlated with the regressors?
ANSWER:

Select Fixed Effects (or First Differences).

WHY?

A rejection of the null hypothesis demonstrates that the difference \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{FE}} - \widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{RE}}\) is statistically significant, indicating that unobserved individual heterogeneity \(c_i\) is correlated with the explanatory variables. Under this condition, the Random Effects estimator is inconsistent due to omitted variable bias. Fixed Effects (or First Differences) purges \(c_i\) and remains consistent.

📋

What You Should Now Understand

  • The Hausman Principle: Compares a consistently robust estimator (\(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{FE}}\)) with an efficient estimator under orthogonality (\(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{RE}}\)).
  • Zero Covariance Lemma: Hausman's lemma establishes \(\text{Var}(\mathbf{q}) = \text{Var}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{FE}}) - \text{Var}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{RE}})\), enabling the quadratic form \(\chi^2(k)\) test statistic.
  • Power Caveat: Non-rejection does not prove absence of endogeneity; weak variation or small samples frequently lead to low test power.
  • Panel Decision Rule: Use Fixed Effects or First Differences whenever \(c_i\) may correlate with regressors, and always apply cluster-robust standard errors (or wild cluster bootstrap if \(G < 30\)).
