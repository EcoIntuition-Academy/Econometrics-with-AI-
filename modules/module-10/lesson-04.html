Choosing the Cluster Level & Small-Cluster Issues

Aligning clustering levels with policy variation, diagnosing finite-sample asymptotic breakdown, and navigating modern wild bootstrap solutions.

LEARNING OBJECTIVES
  • Apply the core econometric rule: clustering should match the level of treatment assignment or policy variation.
  • Identify the catastrophic consequences of clustering too finely (e.g., individual instead of state).
  • Analyze the small-cluster problem: why having few clusters biases cluster-robust standard errors downward.
  • Survey modern econometric literature (Cameron-Gelbach-Miller 2008, Djogbenou et al. 2019, Roodman et al. 2019) supporting inference with as few as 6 clusters via wild cluster bootstrap.

1. What Should We Cluster On?

There is no universally accepted automatic rule on what level to cluster at. However, applied econometrics provides a foundational guiding principle:

CORE GUIDELINE Align with Policy Variation

Clustering should ideally match the level of treatment assignment or policy variation.

County-Level Policy

If health clinic subsidies vary across counties, clustering at the county level accounts for common local economic and demographic shocks affecting all individuals in that county.

State-Level Policy

If minimum wage laws or tax reforms vary at the state level, clustering at the individual or household level erroneously assumes zero correlation across residents of the same state, severely understating standard errors.

CAUTION Randomization Within Groups

Even when treatments are randomly assigned to individuals or schools within a district, unobserved district-wide policies or institutional shocks can induce correlation. In many cases, clustering at the broader group level remains essential.

2. The Small Number of Clusters Problem

Standard cluster-robust covariance matrices rely on large-cluster asymptotics (as the number of clusters \(G \to \infty\)). When the number of clusters is small in practice, several serious econometric issues arise:

1. Downward Bias of Standard Errors: When the number of clusters is small, cluster residuals are mechanically constrained by OLS first-order conditions, causing sample outer products to systematically underestimate the true population score variance.

2. Worse Asymptotic Approximation: As cluster count decreases, the sampling distribution of the variance estimator exhibits heavy skewness and excess variance, causing \(t\)-statistics to reject the null hypothesis far too frequently (e.g., rejecting at 20% when nominal size is 5%).

The Clustering Dilemma

Broader clustering: captures wider dependence but may leave fewer effective clusters.

Finer clustering: creates more clusters but can incorrectly assume independence across broader groups.

FURTHER READING
  • Cameron, Gelbach & Miller (2008) — Bootstrap-Based Improvements for Inference with Clustered Errors, Rev. Econ. Stat.
  • Djogbenou, MacKinnon & Nielsen (2019) — Asymptotic Theory and Wild Bootstrap for High-Dimensional Clustering, J. Econometrics
  • Hess (2017) — Randomization Inference with Few Clusters, Stata J.
  • Roodman, Nielsen, Webb & MacKinnon (2019) — Fast and Wild: Bootstrap Inference in Stata, Stata J.
KEY TAKEAWAY

Specialized small-cluster methods are discussed in the cited literature; ordinary large-cluster approximations become less reliable when the number of clusters is limited.

3. Concept Check

CHECK 10.4 Policy Variation Principle

Why is clustering at the individual level inappropriate when the policy being evaluated varies at the state level?

Hint: Are individuals living in the same state exposed to common state-level economic conditions and shocks?
ANSWER:

Clustering at the individual level erroneously assumes zero correlation among individuals residing within the same state.

WHY?

Because the policy applies uniformly to all residents in a state, their outcomes share common state-level unobserved shocks. Clustering only at the individual level ignores this within-state correlation, treating 1,000 individuals in Texas as 1,000 independent policy experiments. This drastically understates standard errors and creates spurious statistical significance.

📋

What You Should Now Understand

  • Policy Alignment Rule: The level of clustering should match the level of treatment assignment or policy variation.
  • Finer Clustering Pitfall: Clustering at smaller units (e.g., household or individual) erroneously assumes zero correlation across broader groups sharing common policy shocks.
  • Small Number of Clusters: When the number of clusters is small, asymptotic formulas break down, underestimating standard errors.
  • Modern Bootstrap Solutions: Literature (Cameron et al. 2008, Djogbenou et al. 2019, Roodman et al. 2019) validates inference with as few as 6 clusters using wild cluster bootstrap methods.
