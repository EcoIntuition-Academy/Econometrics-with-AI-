LESSON 1.2 Causal Identification

Correlation, Causation & Economic Questions

Why do students in smaller classrooms score higher on exams? Is it the smaller class size itself, or the hidden advantages of affluent districts?

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why an observed association between class size and test scores does not by itself establish causation.
  • Identify teacher quality and parental involvement as possible confounders that can affect both class size and student achievement.
  • Read a simple causal diagram and distinguish the relationship of interest from confounding pathways.
  • Explain why causal identification matters for policy decisions.

1. The Observed Empirical Pattern

Across public school districts, observational data reveals a consistent statistical pattern:

Smaller Class Size ⟷ Higher Average Test Score
This is an observed association, not yet a causal conclusion.

If a researcher runs a naive bivariate linear regression:

Bivariate Specification The Naive Class Size Regression
\[ \text{TestScore}_i = \beta_1 + \beta_2 \text{ClassSize}_i + \varepsilon_i \]
WHERE:
\(\text{TestScore}_i\)District average standardized exam score
\(\text{ClassSize}_i\)Student-to-teacher classroom ratio
\(\beta_1\)Regression intercept (baseline score)
\(\beta_2\)Marginal association of class size
\(\varepsilon_i\)Unobserved influences (teacher quality, family wealth, tutoring)

The estimated slope \(\hat{\beta}_2\) typically turns out to be negative and statistically significant (\(\hat{\beta}_2 < 0\)). A school board might conclude: "If we spend $20 million hiring 150 new teachers to lower our class sizes from 26 to 19, student test scores will automatically rise by 12 points."

Is this conclusion economically and scientifically justified?

2. Enter the Confounders: Omitted Variables

School districts are not randomly assigned class sizes. In real economic systems:

  • Affluent School Districts: Have higher property tax revenues, allowing them to fund smaller classrooms. Simultaneously, parents in these districts often have higher levels of education, provide home tutoring, and purchase supplementary learning materials.
  • Teacher Quality Sorting: Top-tier, experienced educators often choose to teach in well-funded schools with smaller class sizes and better administrative support.

Therefore, factors such as Teacher Quality and Parental Involvement simultaneously influence both the regressor (\(\text{ClassSize}\)) and the outcome (\(\text{TestScore}\)).

Directed Acyclic Graph (Causal DAG) of the Identification Challenge Structural Causal Model

The question mark represents the true causal relationship \(\beta_{\text{causal}}\) we wish to measure. The confounding paths introduce backdoor bias.

Observed Regressor (X)
Observed Outcome (Y)
Unobserved Confounders (Z₁, Z₂)
Backdoor Confounding Paths
Causal Path to Identify (?)
Confounding in the Class Size–Test Score Relationship:

Teacher quality (\(Z_1\)) and parental involvement (\(Z_2\)) simultaneously affect both classroom size and student test scores, making the observed statistical correlation between class size and achievement potentially misleading:

  • The Identification Goal: We seek the true causal effect of class size on test scores (\(\beta_2\)), represented by the central arrow with the question mark (?).
  • The Backdoor Bias: Affluent school districts have higher parental support and attract top-tier educators, which naturally leads both to smaller classrooms and higher exam marks.
  • Omitted Variable Bias: Because \(Z_1\) and \(Z_2\) are unobserved in simple bivariate regression, OLS bundles their positive benefits into the class size estimate, creating severe upward bias (or an overly negative slope estimate).

Simulated District Scatterplot: Observed vs. Hidden Variables

Naive Bivariate Association: The raw scatterplot shows a stark downward slope (\(\hat{\beta}_2 \approx -1.73\)). Policymakers might conclude cutting class sizes drastically raises test scores alone, ignoring that affluent districts simultaneously have smaller classes, master teachers, and higher parental tutoring.

🔎 WHAT TO NOTICE

◆ WHY IT MATTERS

The steep negative slope in the aggregate data is largely an artifact of comparing wealthy districts to under-resourced districts.

It is not the isolated causal effect of class size!

📊 ECONOMETRIC INTERPRETATION

Omitted Variable Bias:

\(\text{Bias} = \beta_{\text{confounder}} \times \frac{\text{Cov}(X, Z)}{\text{Var}(X)}\)

Because both terms are positive, the naive slope is severely contaminated by omitted confounders.

🎮 TRY THIS: Toggle between "Observed Relationship" and "Reveal Hidden Variables" several times. Notice how the dashed gray line misleads you by bridging the gap between two entirely different socio-economic populations.

3. The High-Stakes Policy Dilemma

Suppose a state legislature allocates $200 million for class size reduction. If the true causal slope is \(-0.5\) rather than the naive \(-1.73\):

  • The actual test score improvement will be less than one-third of what was promised.
  • Hiring hundreds of new teachers in a hurry may dilute average teacher quality (\(Z_1\)), potentially reducing student learning overall!

This is why econometrics is critical: rigorous identification separates genuine policy levers from mere statistical illusions.

Confounding Bias Omitted Variable Bias Decomposition
\[ \text{Bias} = \beta_{\text{confounder}} \times \frac{\text{Cov}(X, \text{Confounder})}{\text{Var}(X)} \]
WHERE:
\(\text{Bias}\)Discrepancy between \(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}_2]\) and the true causal parameter
\(\beta_{\text{confounder}}\)Direct impact of omitted factor on standardized test score
\(\text{Cov}(X, Z)\)Covariance between class size and the omitted factor
\(\text{Var}(X)\)Sample variance of class size
⚠️ Common Student Mistake
MISCONCEPTION: "If we collect a massive dataset with 5,000,000 students instead of 500, the law of large numbers will eliminate the confounding bias."
CORRECTION: More data does not fix endogeneity! If your model omits a critical confounding variable, gathering 5 million observations will merely estimate the wrong number with extreme precision. Identification is a theoretical and design requirement, not a sample size problem.
🤖 AI Insight Omitted Variable Intuition

In labor economics, the correlation between owning golf clubs and having higher earnings is strongly positive. Does buying golf clubs increase your salary? No. Golf club ownership proxies for wealth, executive networks, and leisure time. In econometrics, whenever an explanatory variable acts as a proxy for unobserved advantages, naive OLS fails to identify causality.

Quick Knowledge Check

Question: In the regression \(\text{TestScore}_i = \beta_1 + \beta_2 \text{ClassSize}_i + \varepsilon_i\), assume parental tutoring has been omitted. Parental tutoring positively affects test scores, and affluent parents are more likely to place children in districts with smaller class sizes (\(\text{Cov}(\text{ClassSize}, \text{Tutoring}) < 0\)). Will the naive OLS estimate \(\hat{\beta}_2\) overestimate or underestimate the beneficial effect of small class sizes?

Recall the omitted variable bias formula: \(\text{Bias} = \gamma \times \frac{\text{Cov}(X, Z)}{\text{Var}(X)}\). Note that \(\gamma > 0\) (tutoring raises scores) and \(\text{Cov}(X, Z) < 0\) (smaller classes correlate with more tutoring).

Solution:
The omitted variable bias is \(\text{Bias} = (+) \times (-) = \text{negative}\). Because the expected causal effect of class size on test scores is negative (increasing class size reduces scores), a negative bias makes \(\hat{\beta}_2\) more negative than the true effect (e.g. \(-1.73\) instead of \(-0.5\)). Consequently, naive OLS exaggerates (overestimates) the apparent benefit of smaller class sizes by attributing the benefits of parental tutoring directly to class size!

Lesson 1.2 Summary

Correlation between economic variables often reflects shared confounders rather than direct cause and effect. To address this, we must build explicit mathematical models that control for multiple variables simultaneously.
