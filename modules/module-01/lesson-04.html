LESSON 1.4 Estimation & Geometry

OLS, Projection & Residuals

How does Ordinary Least Squares choose the unique line of best fit? Explore algebraic minimization, projection onto \(\text{Col}(X)\), and the crucial boundary between unobservable errors and sample residuals.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Understand the intuitive and mathematical logic of minimizing the Sum of Squared Residuals (SSE).
  • Derive and interpret the closed-form normal equations: \(\hat{\beta} = (X'X)^{-1}X'y\).
  • Visualize OLS geometrically as an orthogonal projection onto the column space of the design matrix.
  • Master the critical conceptual boundary between population errors \(\varepsilon_i\) and sample residuals \(e_i\).

1. The OLS Objective Function

In an empirical sample, observations never lie in a perfect straight line. For any candidate coefficient vector \(b = \begin{bmatrix} b_1 & b_2 \end{bmatrix}'\), the sample residual \(e_i\) is the vertical difference between the observed outcome and the fitted value:

Observed Value \(y_i\) − Fitted Value \(\hat{y}_i = x_i' b\) = Residual \(e_i\)

The principle of Ordinary Least Squares states: choose the coefficient vector \(b\) that minimizes the sum of squared vertical differences between the actual outcomes \(y_i\) and the fitted values \(\hat{y}_i\).

Loss Function Sum of Squared Residuals (SSE)
\[ S(b) = \sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - x_i' b)^2 = (y - Xb)'(y - Xb) \]
WHERE:
\(S(b)\) Sum of squared residuals evaluated at candidate coefficient vector \(b\).
\(e_i\) Sample residual for observation \(i\) under candidate coefficients \(b\).
\(b\) Candidate \(k \times 1\) coefficient vector (distinguished from true \(\beta\) and OLS estimate \(\hat{\beta}\)).
\(\hat{\beta}\) OLS estimate vector \((X'X)^{-1}X'y\) that achieves the global minimum of \(S(b)\).

◆ WHY IT MATTERS

Real economic data never fall perfectly on a single line.

Ordinary Least Squares systematically searches across all possible candidate lines to find the unique pair \((\hat{b}_1, \hat{b}_2)\) that minimizes the sum of squared vertical distances \(\sum e_i^2\).

📊 ECONOMETRIC INTERPRETATION

The OLS estimator chooses parameter values \(\hat{b}_1, \hat{b}_2\) such that:

\(S(b) = \sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2 = \text{minimum}\)

At this optimal point, the residuals sum to zero (\(\sum e_i = 0\)) and are geometrically orthogonal to the regressor (\(\sum e_i x_i = 0\)).

2. The Closed-Form OLS Solution

Formally, the OLS estimator is defined as:

$$\hat{\beta} = \arg\min_b S(b) \quad \text{where} \quad S(b) = (y - Xb)'(y - Xb) = y'y - 2b'X'y + b'X'Xb$$

Taking the matrix derivative with respect to the parameter vector \(b\) and setting it equal to the zero vector:

$$\frac{\partial S(b)}{\partial b} = -2X'y + 2X'Xb = 0 \implies X'X\hat{\beta} = X'y$$

These are called the Normal Equations. Provided the \(k \times k\) matrix \(X'X\) is invertible (Full Rank, which we study in Lesson 1.5), multiplying both sides by \((X'X)^{-1}\) yields the iconic OLS formula:

OLS Estimator The Closed-Form Normal Equation Solution
\[ \hat{\beta} = (X'X)^{-1}X'y \]
WHERE:
\(\hat{\beta}\)Estimated OLS parameter vector (\(k \times 1\))
\(X'X\)Regressor cross-product / covariance matrix (\(k \times k\))
\((X'X)^{-1}\)Matrix inverse (sample variance normalization)
\(X'y\)Regressor-outcome covariance vector (\(k \times 1\))
Component Dimension Role in Estimation
\(X'X\) \(k \times k\) Captures the sample variances and covariances of the regressors.
\((X'X)^{-1}\) \(k \times k\) The matrix inverse (analogue of dividing by \(\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2\) in bivariate regression).
\(X'y\) \(k \times 1\) Captures the sample covariance between each explanatory regressor and the dependent variable \(y\).
\(\hat{\beta}\) \(k \times 1\) Vector of estimated OLS coefficients.

3. The Geometry of OLS: Orthogonal Projection

OLS has a breathtaking geometric interpretation in \(n\)-dimensional Euclidean space \(\mathbb{R}^n\).

The observed outcome vector \(y\) is a point in \(\mathbb{R}^n\). The columns of matrix \(X\) span a \(k\)-dimensional linear subspace, denoted as \(\text{Col}(X)\).

Projection Operator Fitted Values & The Hat Matrix
\[ \hat{y} = X\hat{\beta} = \underbrace{X(X'X)^{-1}X'}_{P_X} y = P_X y \]
WHERE:
\(\hat{y}\)Vector of fitted / predicted values (\(n \times 1\))
\(P_X\)Orthogonal projection matrix (Hat matrix \(H\))
\(M_X\)Annihilator matrix: \(e = (I - P_X)y = M_X y\)

\(P_X\) is the famous Projection Matrix (often called the Hat Matrix, \(H\), because it puts the hat on \(y\)). It projects the vector \(y\) orthogonally onto the subspace spanned by the regressors:

Orthogonal Projection of \(y\) onto Column Space \(\text{Col}(X)\) Euclidean Geometry \(\mathbb{R}^n\)
Figure 1.4: OLS solves for the point in \(\text{Col}(X)\) closest to \(y\). The residual vector \(e\) is perpendicular to every direction in the subspace: \(X'e = 0\).
Fundamental Algebraic Property: Residuals are Orthogonal to Regressors

Because \(\hat{y}\) is an orthogonal projection, the residual vector \(e = y - \hat{y}\) is perpendicular to the entire subspace \(\text{Col}(X)\). In matrix algebra: \[ X'e = 0 \] This guarantees two mathematical truths in sample OLS:
1. The sum of sample residuals is zero whenever an intercept is included: \(\sum e_i = 0\).
2. The sample covariance between the residuals and every included regressor is identically zero: \(\text{Cov}(x_j, e) = 0\).

4. Error Term (\(\varepsilon_i\)) vs. Residual (\(e_i\))

No distinction in econometrics is more vital. Conflating these two concepts causes severe confusion in empirical work:

Population Error: \(\varepsilon_i\)
  • Belongs to: The true unknown data-generating process (\(y_i = x_i'\beta + \varepsilon_i\)).
  • Observability: Unobservable. We never know the true \(\beta\), so we can never observe \(\varepsilon_i\).
  • Geometric meaning: Distance between \(y_i\) and the true population conditional expectation line.
  • Property: May or may not be correlated with \(X\) (if it is, OLS is biased!).
Sample Residual: \(e_i\)
  • Belongs to: The estimated sample regression (\(y_i = x_i'\hat{\beta} + e_i\)).
  • Observability: Observable. Calculated immediately as \(e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i\).
  • Geometric meaning: Distance between \(y_i\) and the sample fitted OLS line.
  • Property: Always mathematically orthogonal to regressors in the sample (\(X'e \equiv 0\)) by construction of OLS!
CARDINAL RULE
\[ \mathbf{\varepsilon_i \neq e_i} \]

The population error \(\varepsilon_i\) is an unobservable conceptual disturbance; the residual \(e_i\) is an observable algebraic byproduct of sample projection.

⚠️ Common Student Mistake
MISCONCEPTION: "I checked my regression output in Stata/Python and the correlation between the regressor \(X\) and the residuals \(e\) is 0.0000. Therefore, the exogeneity assumption holds and my estimates must be causal and unbiased!"
CORRECTION: \(X'e = 0\) is a mathematical identity of OLS, not an empirical discovery! OLS mechanically forces the residuals \(e\) to have zero correlation with \(X\), even when the true error \(\varepsilon\) is wildly endogenous! You can never test exogeneity simply by inspecting the correlation of regressors with residuals.
🤖 AI Insight The Hat Matrix in Computational AI

In modern econometrics and machine learning, the diagonal elements of the projection matrix \(h_{ii} = x_i'(X'X)^{-1}x_i\) are known as leverage scores. They measure how far an observation's features are from the sample mean. If \(h_{ii}\) is large, that single data point exerts disproportionate influence on the orientation of the OLS hyperplane!

Quick Knowledge Check

Question: Suppose you estimate a wage regression: \(\widehat{\ln(\text{Wage})}_i = 1.8 + 0.09 \text{Educ}_i\). For individual #4, \(\text{Educ}_4 = 16\) and actual \(\ln(\text{Wage}_4) = 3.50\). Calculate the predicted value \(\hat{y}_4\) and the residual \(e_4\). Is \(e_4\) equal to the true disturbance \(\varepsilon_4\)?

Plug \(\text{Educ} = 16\) into the sample equation to get \(\hat{y}_4\), then calculate \(e_4 = y_4 - \hat{y}_4\).

Solution:
1. \(\hat{y}_4 = 1.8 + 0.09(16) = 1.8 + 1.44 = 3.24\).
2. Residual \(e_4 = y_4 - \hat{y}_4 = 3.50 - 3.24 = \mathbf{+0.26}\).
3. No, \(e_4 \neq \varepsilon_4\). The residual \(+0.26\) is based on the sample estimates (\(1.8\) and \(0.09\)). The true error \(\varepsilon_4\) depends on the unknown population coefficients \(\beta_1\) and \(\beta_2\), which differ from the sample estimates due to sampling variability.

Lesson 1.4 Summary

OLS chooses coefficients to minimize the sum of squared residuals, producing the closed-form estimator \(\hat{\beta} = (X'X)^{-1}X'y\). Geometrically, this projects \(y\) orthogonally onto \(\text{Col}(X)\), forcing sample residuals to be perpendicular to all regressors (\(X'e = 0\)).
