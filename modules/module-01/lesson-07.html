LESSON 1.7 CLRM Assumptions (Part 3) & Master Summary

Fixed/Random Regressors & Normality

Conclude the Classical Linear Regression Model assumptions: discover why conditioning on \(X\) simplifies derivations, demystify the exact role of normal errors, and study the complete CLRM synthesis table.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Differentiate between fixed regressors in controlled trials vs. random regressors in observational economics.
  • Understand why econometric derivations condition on \(X\): \(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta} \mid X]\).
  • Clarify what Normality of errors \(\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\) is — and is not — required for.
  • Distinguish exact finite-sample inference from large-sample Central Limit Theorem (CLT) asymptotic validity.
  • Synthesize all 6 CLRM assumptions in a master comparison matrix.

1. Assumption A5: Regressor Treatment (Fixed vs. Random)

Econometric theory distinguishes how the regressor matrix \(X\) is treated in probability statements:

Fixed-X View

For theoretical derivations, \(X\) may be treated as given and probability statements are made conditional on the observed regressor matrix. While typical in laboratory experiments, this is a conceptual modeling stance.

Random-X View

In observational settings, \(X\) is itself generated by a stochastic process or joint population distribution \((Y, X)\). When a new sample of households or firms is drawn, their regressors vary randomly as well.

Even when regressors are random, econometric derivations often condition on the realized \(X\). Examples can illustrate experiments vs. observational data, but do not imply an exact equivalence between fixed/random regressors and experimental/observational data.

Why do Econometric Derivations Condition on \(X\)?

When \(X\) is stochastic, the term \((X'X)^{-1}X'\varepsilon\) is a ratio of random variables. To simplify algebraic derivations while maintaining complete generality, econometricians evaluate expectations conditional on the realized matrix \(X\):

Assumption A5 • Conditional Expectation Law of Iterated Expectations (LIE)
$$\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta} \mid X] = \beta + (X'X)^{-1}X'\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid X] = \beta$$ $$\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}] = \mathbb{E}_X\left[\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta} \mid X]\right] = \mathbb{E}_X[\beta] = \mathbf{\beta}$$
WHERE:
\(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta} \mid X]\)Conditional expectation evaluating properties conditional on the realized sample matrix \(X\)
\(\mathbb{E}_X[\cdot]\)Outer expectation taken over the joint distribution of stochastic regressors \(X\)
\(\beta\)True population parameter vector; proves unconditional unbiasedness holds for observational data

Thus, unconditional unbiasedness holds effortlessly, confirming that conditioning on \(X\) is fully compatible with stochastic economic regressors.

2. Assumption A6: Normality of Disturbances

Assumption A6 states that the disturbances, conditional on \(X\), follow a normal distribution:

Assumption A6 • Error Distribution Normality of Disturbances
$$\varepsilon_i \mid X \sim \text{i.i.d. } \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2) \quad \iff \quad \varepsilon \mid X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I_n)$$
WHERE:
\(\varepsilon \mid X\)Vector of disturbance terms conditional on the realized regressor matrix
\(\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I_n)\)Multivariate normal distribution with mean zero and spherical covariance matrix

NORMALITY IS NOT REQUIRED FOR:

  • OLS Unbiasedness: \(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}] = \beta\) holds strictly under exogeneity (\(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid X] = 0\)), regardless of whether disturbances are skewed or heavy-tailed.
  • OLS Consistency: \(\text{plim } \hat{\beta} = \beta\) holds without normality under standard regularity conditions.
  • Gauss–Markov Theorem (BLUE): Under Linearity, Full Rank, Zero Conditional Mean, and Spherical Errors, OLS is the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator without requiring normality.

NORMALITY IS USEFUL FOR:

  • Exact Finite-Sample \(t\)-Inference: Guarantees that standard \(t\)-statistics follow the exact Student's \(t\)-distribution.
  • Exact Finite-Sample \(F\)-Inference: Enables exact \(F\)-tests for multiple linear hypothesis restrictions.
  • Exact Confidence Intervals: Supports finite-sample confidence intervals with exact coverage probabilities under the classical normal model.

Finite-Sample vs. Large-Sample (Asymptotic) Inference

Exact Finite-Sample Inference

With normal disturbances and the required classical assumptions, exact \(t\)- and \(F\)-distribution results are available for any sample size.

Large-Sample / Asymptotic Inference

Without normality, estimators and test statistics often have approximately normal large-sample distributions through Central Limit Theorem arguments under appropriate regularity conditions.

3. Master CLRM Synthesis

Master CLRM Summary: Assumptions, Meanings, and Consequences of Violation

Keep this comprehensive matrix open as your reference throughout empirical coursework:

Assumption Formal Statement Why It Matters If Violated...
A1: Linearity in Parameters \(y = X\beta + \varepsilon\) Ensures linear Normal Equations and closed-form OLS solution. OLS cannot be computed. Must use Non-linear Least Squares (NLS) or transformations.
A2: Full Rank \(\text{rank}(X) = k\) (\(n \ge k\)) Guarantees \((X'X)^{-1}\) exists, uniquely identifying each coefficient. Matrix is singular. Infinite coefficient solutions; OLS cannot isolate individual effects.
A3: Strict Exogeneity \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid X] = 0\) Master key to unbiasedness: \(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}] = \beta\). Endogeneity bias! \(\hat{\beta}\) is systematically wrong; more data does not fix it.
A4: Spherical Errors \(\text{Var}(\varepsilon|X) = \sigma^2 I_n\) Guarantees OLS is BLUE (efficient) and classical standard errors are valid. OLS remains unbiased, but loses efficiency. Standard errors are invalid (requires Robust SEs).
A5: Regressor Treatment \(X\) treated as given conditional Allows straightforward calculation of moments \(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta} \mid X]\). Requires asynchronous limit theorems and instrumental variables if simultaneity exists.
A6: Normal Errors \(\varepsilon \mid X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)\) Guarantees exact finite-sample \(t\) and \(F\) distributions for testing. Finite-sample tests approximate; in large samples, Central Limit Theorem rescues inference.
⚠️ Common Student Mistake
MISCONCEPTION: "My dependent variable \(y\) (e.g. wages) is heavily right-skewed and non-normal. Therefore, linear regression assumptions are violated and I cannot run OLS."
CORRECTION: The CLRM makes NO assumption that the variables \(X\) or \(y\) must follow a normal distribution! The normality assumption applies exclusively to the unobserved disturbance term \(\varepsilon\), and even then, it is only required for exact small-sample tests. In modern empirical datasets (\(n > 500\)), non-normal variables are completely acceptable.
🤖 AI Insight The Asymptotic Revolution in Econometrics

Fifty years ago, econometrics textbooks devoted entire chapters to testing for normal errors because datasets had 25 observations. Today, administrative registries and big datasets contain hundreds of thousands of rows. The Central Limit Theorem does the heavy lifting, shifting the modern econometrician's primary focus almost entirely to Assumption A3 (Exogeneity)!

Quick Knowledge Check

Question: An empirical researcher has a sample of \(n = 10,000\) workers. A Jarque-Bera test rejects normality of the regression residuals at \(p < 0.001\). The research advisor suggests discarding the model, claiming the OLS coefficients are biased and hypothesis tests are invalid. Is the advisor correct?

Review what guarantees unbiasedness (\(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon|X]=0\)) and how sample size \(n=10,000\) affects asymptotic distribution under the Central Limit Theorem.

Solution:
The advisor is incorrect on both counts:
1. Unbiasedness: Unbiasedness depends exclusively on Strict Exogeneity (\(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid X] = 0\)). Normality plays zero role in whether \(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}] = \beta\).
2. Hypothesis Testing: With \(n = 10,000\) observations, the Central Limit Theorem guarantees that \(\hat{\beta}\) converges to a normal distribution asymptotically. Asymptotic \(t\)-tests, \(z\)-tests, and confidence intervals remain completely valid.
