LESSON 1.3 Model Specification & Matrix Algebra

The Classical Linear Regression Model

From simple bivariate lines to high-dimensional matrix equations: understand parameters, disturbance terms, and the algebra of regression.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Formulate the simple bivariate linear model and explain every mathematical symbol.
  • Understand the role of multiple regressors in isolating ceteris paribus effects.
  • Translate scalar regression equations into full matrix notation \(y = X\beta + \varepsilon\).
  • Master matrix dimensions (\(n \times 1\), \(n \times k\), \(k \times 1\)) and understand why the design matrix contains a leading column of 1s.

1. The Simple Bivariate Regression Model

The simplest econometric model describes a relationship between a single dependent variable \(y\) and a single explanatory variable \(x_2\):

Scalar Model Simple Bivariate Linear Specification
\[ y_i = \beta_1 + \beta_2 x_{i2} + \varepsilon_i \quad (i = 1, 2, \dots, n) \]
WHERE:
\(y_i\) Observed outcome for observation \(i\).
\(x_{i2}\) Value of explanatory variable \(x_2\) for observation \(i\).
\(\beta_1\) Intercept. Under the zero-conditional-mean condition, it is the conditional mean of \(y\) when \(x_2 = 0\).
\(\beta_2\) Slope coefficient. In the simple linear conditional-mean model, it gives the change in \(\mathbb{E}[y \mid x_2]\) associated with a one-unit change in \(x_2\).
\(\varepsilon_i\) Unobserved component of \(y_i\) not captured by the included regressors.

For instance, in our class-size application:

$$\text{TestScore}_i = \beta_1 + \beta_2 \text{ClassSize}_i + \varepsilon_i$$

Adjust the intercept, slope, and noise to see how each component changes the relationship between \(X\) and \(Y\).

Population Regression Function \(\mathbb{E}[y \mid x] = \beta_1 + \beta_2 x_2\) Observed Sample \((y_i, x_{2i})\) & Disturbance (\(\varepsilon_i\))

👁 WHAT YOU ARE SEEING

The blue line represents the population regression relationship:

\(\mathbb{E}[y \mid x] = \beta_1 + \beta_2 x_2\)

Each point represents an observed sample value. Its vertical distance from the line reflects the influence of factors not captured by \(X\):

\(\varepsilon_i = y_i - (\beta_1 + \beta_2 x_{i2})\)

🔎 WHAT TO NOTICE

Change \(\beta_1\): The entire line moves vertically up or down without tilting.

Change \(\beta_2\): The slope rotates around the intercept—becoming steeper, flatter, positive, or negative.

Increase Noise (\(\sigma\)): The observations spread farther away from the regression line.

◆ WHY IT MATTERS

Real economic data rarely fall perfectly on a line.

Econometrics tries to recover the underlying systematic relationship even when observations contain substantial randomness, measurement error, and omitted factors.

📊 ECONOMETRIC INTERPRETATION

\(\beta_1\) describes the intercept: The expected baseline outcome when \(x_2 = 0\).

\(\beta_2\) describes the marginal effect: How the conditional mean of \(Y\) changes as \(X\) changes.

The error term (\(\varepsilon_i\)): Captures all omitted influences on \(Y\) not explicitly represented by the regressor.

🎮 TRY THIS: Increase the Noise Level substantially (e.g. to 9.0 or 10.0). Notice that the underlying regression line does not change, but identifying its direction and slope from the observed scattered data becomes much harder. This is why statistical inference and hypothesis testing are essential.

2. Why Multiple Regression? The Power of Ceteris Paribus

As established in Lesson 1.2, bivariate regression is almost always plagued by omitted variable bias. To hold other factors constant, we expand to \(k-1\) explanatory variables:

$$y_i = \beta_1 + \beta_2 x_{i2} + \beta_3 x_{i3} + \dots + \beta_k x_{ik} + \varepsilon_i$$

In our education example:

$$\text{TestScore}_i = \beta_1 + \beta_2 \text{ClassSize}_i + \beta_3 \text{TeacherQuality}_i + \beta_4 \text{AvgIncome}_i + \varepsilon_i$$
💡 Economic Interpretation of \(\beta_2\)

Now, \(\beta_2 = \frac{\partial \text{TestScore}}{\partial \text{ClassSize}}\) represents the marginal effect of class size holding teacher quality and family income fixed (ceteris paribus). By explicitly including confounders in the model, their influence is partialled out from \(\varepsilon_i\)!

3. Matrix Notation: Compact & Universal

Writing out \(n\) separate equations with \(k\) variables becomes unwieldy. In modern econometrics and computational algorithms, the entire sample is expressed in compact matrix notation:

Matrix Specification The Classical Linear Regression Model
\[ y = X\beta + \varepsilon \]
WHERE:
\(y\) \(n \times 1\) vector of observed outcomes.
\(X\) \(n \times k\) design matrix. Each row corresponds to one observation; each column corresponds to one included regressor.
\(\beta\) \(k \times 1\) vector of unknown population coefficients.
\(\varepsilon\) \(n \times 1\) vector of unobserved disturbances.

The first column of \(X\) is typically a column of ones when an intercept is included. Let us write out every component in full matrix form:

$$\begin{bmatrix} y_1 \\ y_2 \\ \vdots \\ y_n \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & x_{12} & x_{13} & \dots & x_{1k} \\ 1 & x_{22} & x_{23} & \dots & x_{2k} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 1 & x_{n2} & x_{n3} & \dots & x_{nk} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \beta_1 \\ \beta_2 \\ \vdots \\ \beta_k \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} \varepsilon_1 \\ \varepsilon_2 \\ \vdots \\ \varepsilon_n \end{bmatrix}$$

Each row represents one observation. Each column of \(X\) represents one regressor. Multiplying \(X\) (\(n \times k\)) by \(\beta\) (\(k \times 1\)) produces an \(n \times 1\) vector, matching the dimensions of \(y\) and \(\varepsilon\).

Matrix Specification

Matrix Dimensions Breakdown

Understanding the shape of each object in \(y = X\beta + \varepsilon\)

Object Dimensions Meaning
\(y\) \(n \times 1\) Column vector of observed outcomes for all \(n\) observations.
\(X\) \(n \times k\) The Design Matrix. Each row represents one observation and each column represents one regressor.
\(\beta\) \(k \times 1\) Column vector of the \(k\) unknown population coefficients to be estimated.
\(\varepsilon\) \(n \times 1\) Column vector of unobserved random disturbances for all \(n\) observations.
📐 Dimension Intuition
\(y: n \times 1\) One observed outcome for each observation.
\(X: n \times k\) \(n\) observations \(\times\) \(k\) regressors (including constant 1s).
\(\beta: k \times 1\) One population coefficient for each column of \(X\).
Dimension Check Matrix Multiplication Conformability
\[ \underbrace{X}_{(n \times k)} \underbrace{\beta}_{(k \times 1)} = \underbrace{X\beta}_{(n \times 1)} \]

Because the inner dimensions match (\(k = k\)), matrix multiplication is valid: \[ (n \times k)(k \times 1) = n \times 1 \] Therefore, the model \(y = X\beta + \varepsilon\) is dimensionally consistent because \(y\), \(X\beta\), and \(\varepsilon\) are all \(n \times 1\) column vectors.

Individual Observation Representation

For an individual observation \(i\), the matrix model reduces cleanly to scalar-product form:

Observation Level Scalar Representation for the \(i\)-th Observation
\[ y_i = x_i' \beta + \varepsilon_i \]

where \(x_i' = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & x_{i2} & \dots & x_{ik} \end{bmatrix}\) is a \(1 \times k\) row vector representing the \(i\)-th row of the design matrix \(X\).

Why does the first column of \(X\) contain only 1s?

Carrying out matrix multiplication of row \(i\) of \(X\) by vector \(\beta\): \[ x_i' \beta = 1 \cdot \beta_1 + x_{i2} \beta_2 + \dots + x_{ik} \beta_k \] The constant column of 1s ensures that \(\beta_1\) is multiplied by 1 for every single observation, creating the intercept term! If we omitted this column of 1s, the regression line would be artificially forced through the origin \((0,0)\).

⚠️ Common Student Mistake
MISCONCEPTION: "In matrix regression, \(X\) has dimension \(n \times (k-1)\) because there are only \(k-1\) economic variables like class size and income."
CORRECTION: In econometrics, \(k\) is defined as the total number of parameters in \(\beta\), including the intercept. Because the constant term requires its own column of 1s, the matrix \(X\) contains \(k\) columns, making its dimension strictly \(n \times k\).
🤖 AI Insight Linear Algebra as Computational Superpower

When modern AI frameworks (like PyTorch or TensorFlow) or econometrics packages (like Stata or statsmodels) solve linear regression with 500,000 observations, they do not iterate over scalar summations. They translate the entire data frame into GPU memory as the tensor \(X\) and perform matrix multiplications. Mastering the matrix formulation connects classical econometrics directly with state-of-the-art computational workflows.

Quick Knowledge Check

Question: An empirical researcher has survey data on \(n = 1,200\) households. She specifies a regression model explaining household savings using income, household size, head of household age, and head of household education. If the model includes an intercept, what are the exact dimensions of \(y\), \(X\), and \(\beta\)?

Count the total number of parameters \(k\): intercept + 4 economic regressors. Remember each row of \(X\) corresponds to one observation, and each column corresponds to one parameter.

Solution:
The model has 4 explanatory variables plus 1 intercept, so \(k = 5\).
- \(y\) has dimensions \(1,200 \times 1\) (a column vector of savings outcomes).
- \(X\) has dimensions \(1,200 \times 5\) (the first column contains 1s; the remaining 4 columns contain data for income, size, age, and education).
- \(\beta\) has dimensions \(5 \times 1\) (the vector \([\beta_1, \beta_2, \beta_3, \beta_4, \beta_5]'\)).

Lesson 1.3 Summary

Matrix notation \(y = X\beta + \varepsilon\) compresses hundreds of equations into one elegant algebraic expression. It makes computing multi-variable ceteris paribus derivatives computationally tractable. In Lesson 1.4, we will use this matrix foundation to derive the legendary Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) formula \(\hat{\beta} = (X'X)^{-1}X'y\) and explore its geometric projection properties.
