LESSON 1.8 Applied Economic Interpretation

Coefficients & Functional Forms

How do you translate estimated numbers into economic meaning? Learn marginal effects, quadratic curves, semi-log growth rates, and log-log constant elasticities.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State the exact economic interpretation of marginal effects in linear models.
  • Compute and visualize dynamic marginal effects and turning points in quadratic models.
  • Master percentage interpretations in semi-log (log-level) regressions.
  • Interpret coefficients as elasticities in log-log models.
  • Log-linearize multiplicative production functions (Cobb-Douglas).

1. The Linear Model Marginal Effect

Consider the multiple regression model:

LINEAR SPECIFICATION Conditional Mean Slope Interpretation
$$\mathbb{E}[y \mid x] = \beta_1 + \beta_2 x \implies \frac{\partial \mathbb{E}[y \mid x]}{\partial x} = \beta_2$$
WHERE
\(\partial \mathbb{E}[y \mid x] / \partial x\) Marginal association: Rate of change in the conditional mean of \(y\) with respect to \(x\)
\(\beta_2\) Slope coefficient: Change in the conditional mean \(\mathbb{E}[y \mid x]\) associated with a one-unit increase in \(x\)
Preferred Econometric Interpretation:

"Holding the other included regressors fixed, \(\beta_2\) is the change in the conditional mean of \(y\) associated with a one-unit increase in \(x\)."

Identification Note: Do not automatically call \(\beta_2\) a causal effect. A causal interpretation requires additional identification assumptions, especially exogeneity.

2. The Quadratic Model: Non-Constant Marginal Effects

In economics, relationships between variables often curve. For instance, in wage equations, early career experience boosts earnings rapidly, but depreciation of skills eventually moderates the marginal gain:

QUADRATIC SPECIFICATION Marginal Association & Turning Point
$$y = \beta_1 + \beta_2 x + \beta_3 x^2 + \varepsilon \implies \frac{\partial \mathbb{E}[y \mid x]}{\partial x} = \beta_2 + 2\beta_3 x$$ $$x^* = -\frac{\beta_2}{2\beta_3} \quad \left(\text{Turning point where } \frac{\partial \mathbb{E}[y \mid x]}{\partial x} = 0\right)$$
WHERE
\(\beta_2 + 2\beta_3 x\) Marginal association: The marginal association varies with \(x\) rather than remaining constant
\(x^*\) Turning point: The value of \(x\) where the slope of the conditional mean crosses zero

Notice that the marginal association varies with \(x\). It depends directly on the baseline level of \(x\) at which the change is evaluated.

Quadratic Curve & Dynamic Marginal Effect Tangent

$$\frac{\partial y}{\partial x} = 6.0 + 2(-0.25)(8.0) = \mathbf{2.00}$$

👁 WHAT YOU ARE SEEING

The blue curve shows the quadratic expectation function:

\(y = 15 + \beta_2 x + \beta_3 x^2\)

The red dot marks the current operating point \(x\), and the dashed red tangent line represents the instantaneous marginal effect \(\partial y / \partial x\).

🔎 WHAT TO NOTICE

Slide \(x\) from 0 to 20: The slope begins steep and positive, flattens to exactly zero at the turning point:

\(x^* = -\frac{\beta_2}{2\beta_3}\)

Beyond this peak, additional units of \(x\) cause \(y\) to decline!

◆ WHY IT MATTERS

In real economic policy, diminishing returns are ubiquitous.

Interventions yield major returns at low baseline levels but taper off or become counterproductive when over-expanded.

📊 ECONOMETRIC INTERPRETATION

When reporting quadratic estimates, you cannot quote a single slope!

You must evaluate the dynamic marginal effect \(\beta_2 + 2\beta_3 x\) at specific benchmark points (e.g. sample mean \(\bar{x}\), 25th, and 75th percentiles).

🎮 TRY THIS: Set \(\beta_2 = 8.0\) and \(\beta_3 = -0.20\). Move the operating point slider to \(x = 20\). Notice the tangent slope is exactly zero! Then slide to \(x = 22\) and watch the marginal return turn negative.

3. Semi-Log Models: Percentage Changes

In many financial and labor settings, wages, prices, and GDP grow exponentially. Taking the natural logarithm of \(y\) yields the Log-Level (Semi-Log) model:

Log-Level Specification • Semi-Elasticity Percentage Change Interpretation
$$\ln(y) = \beta_1 + \beta_2 x + \varepsilon \implies \frac{d\ln(y)}{dx} = \beta_2 \implies \frac{\Delta y}{y} \approx \beta_2 \cdot \Delta x$$
WHERE:
\(\approx 100\beta_2\%\) Approximate percentage change in \(y\) associated with a 1-unit increase in \(x\) (\(\Delta x = 1\)).
Exact Change For the deterministic conditional relationship with \(\Delta x = 1\), exact change is \(100(e^{\beta_2} - 1)\%\).
Log-Level Economic Interpretation:

"A 1-unit increase in \(x\) is associated with approximately a \(\mathbf{100 \cdot \beta_2\%}\) change in \(y\) (with exact multiplicative effect \(100(e^{\beta_2} - 1)\%\))."

Example: In \(\ln(\text{Wage}) = 1.6 + 0.082 \text{Educ} + \varepsilon\), each additional year of education is associated with an estimated \(\approx 8.2\%\) increase in hourly wage (exact: \(100(e^{0.082} - 1) \approx 8.54\%\)).

4. Log-Log Models: Elasticities

When both the dependent and independent variables are logged, the slope coefficient directly represents an economic elasticity:

Log-Log Specification • Constant Elasticity Direct Elasticity Interpretation
$$\ln(y) = \beta_1 + \beta_2 \ln(x) + \varepsilon \implies \frac{d\ln(y)}{d\ln(x)} = \frac{dy / y}{dx / x} = \mathbf{\beta_2}$$
WHERE:
\(\beta_2\) (Elasticity) A 1% increase in \(x\) is associated with approximately a \(\beta_2\%\) change in \(y\) (conditional association unless causal assumptions hold).
Scale Invariance Because both variables are measured in logarithms, the elasticity interpretation does not depend on the units used for \(x\) and \(y\).
Common Applications Demand estimation (price elasticity of demand) and production functions (output elasticities) are common examples.
Log-Log Economic Interpretation:

"\(\beta_2\) is the elasticity of \(y\) with respect to \(x\). A \(\mathbf{1\%}\) increase in \(x\) is associated with approximately a \(\mathbf{\beta_2\%}\) change in \(y\)."

Example: In demand estimation, \(\ln(Q) = 4.2 - 0.75 \ln(P) + \varepsilon\). A 1% increase in price leads to a 0.75% decline in quantity demanded.

5. Log-Linearization of the Cobb-Douglas Model

Consider the celebrated Cobb-Douglas production function with stochastic shock:

Transformation • Cobb-Douglas Function Log-Linear Parameter Transformation
$$Y = A \cdot L^\alpha \cdot K^\beta \cdot e^\varepsilon \iff \ln(Y) = \beta_0 + \alpha \ln(L) + \beta \ln(K) + \varepsilon \quad (\beta_0 = \ln A)$$
WHERE:
\(\alpha, \beta\)Labor and capital output elasticities; \(\alpha + \beta = 1\) represents constant returns to scale
\(\beta_0 = \ln(A)\)Transformed total factor productivity (TFP) intercept parameter
\(\varepsilon\)Additive disturbance term satisfying Assumption A1 (Linearity in parameters)

The model is now strictly linear in parameters! Standard OLS can be run directly, where \(\alpha\) is the labor elasticity of output and \(\beta\) is the capital elasticity of output.

6. Comparative Matrix of Functional Forms

Master Functional Form Comparison Table

Model Specification Formula Marginal Effect \(\partial y / \partial x\) Standard Interpretation
Level-Level \(y = \beta_1 + \beta_2 x\) \(\beta_2\) (Constant) \(\Delta x = 1 \implies \Delta y = \beta_2\) units
Log-Level (Semi-log) \(\ln(y) = \beta_1 + \beta_2 x\) \(\beta_2 \cdot y\) \(\Delta x = 1 \implies \% \Delta y \approx 100 \beta_2\%\)
Level-Log \(y = \beta_1 + \beta_2 \ln(x)\) \(\beta_2 / x\) \(\% \Delta x = 1\% \implies \Delta y \approx \beta_2 / 100\) units
Log-Log (Elasticity) \(\ln(y) = \beta_1 + \beta_2 \ln(x)\) \(\beta_2 (y/x)\) \(\% \Delta x = 1\% \implies \% \Delta y \approx \beta_2\%\)
Quadratic \(y = \beta_1 + \beta_2 x + \beta_3 x^2\) \(\beta_2 + 2\beta_3 x\) Marginal effect changes with \(x\); peak at \(x^* = -\beta_2 / (2\beta_3)\)
⚠️ Common Student Mistake
MISCONCEPTION: "In a log-log model \(\ln(y) = \beta_1 + 0.45 \ln(x)\), a 1-unit increase in \(x\) increases \(y\) by 45%."
CORRECTION: In a log-log model, both variables are measured in percentage changes! The interpretation is: "A 1% increase in \(x\) is associated with a 0.45% increase in \(y\)." Do not mix up unit changes with percentage changes!
🤖 AI Insight The Small \(\beta\) Approximation Warning

In log-level models, saying \(\% \Delta y \approx 100\beta\%\) is an approximation based on the Taylor series \(\ln(1 + g) \approx g\) for small \(g\). If \(\beta = 0.05\), \(100(e^{0.05}-1) = 5.13\%\), so the approximation is outstanding. But if \(\beta = 0.50\), \(100(e^{0.50}-1) = 64.87\%\)! For large coefficients, always use the exact formula.

Quick Knowledge Check

Question: An empirical wage equation is estimated as: $$\widehat{\text{Wage}}_i = 12.0 + 1.6 \text{Exper}_i - 0.02 \text{Exper}_i^2$$ (a) What is the marginal effect of an additional year of experience when \(\text{Exper} = 10\)?
(b) At what experience level does expected wage reach its peak?

Differentiate with respect to \(\text{Exper}\): \(\frac{\partial \text{Wage}}{\partial \text{Exper}} = \beta_2 + 2\beta_3 \text{Exper}\). Set it to 0 to find the peak.

Solution:
(a) The marginal effect is: $$\frac{\partial \text{Wage}}{\partial \text{Exper}} = 1.6 + 2(-0.02)\text{Exper} = 1.6 - 0.04\text{Exper}$$ At \(\text{Exper} = 10\): \(1.6 - 0.04(10) = 1.6 - 0.40 = \mathbf{+1.20}\) (i.e. +$1.20 per hour).
(b) Peak wage occurs where the marginal effect equals zero: $$1.6 - 0.04 \text{Exper}^* = 0 \implies \text{Exper}^* = \frac{1.6}{0.04} = \mathbf{40 \text{ years}}.$$

Lesson 1.8 Summary

Functional form specification dictates economic interpretation. Linear models yield constant marginal effects, quadratics capture turning points, semi-logs express percentage changes, and log-log models estimate constant elasticities.
