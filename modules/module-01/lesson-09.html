🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Derive the mathematical proof of the exact dummy variable percentage effect: \(100(e^{\beta_1} - 1)\%\).
  • Contrast the common approximation \(100\beta_1\%\) with the exact formula across parameter magnitudes.
  • Review the complete econometric inquiry pipeline from research question to empirical conclusion.

  • Complete multi-category practice questions with step-by-step solutions.

    • 1. Dummy Variables with Log Dependent Variables

    Consider a semi-log model where one of the regressors is a binary indicator (dummy variable) \(D \in \{0, 1\}\) (e.g. \(D = 1\) if holding a college degree, \(0\) otherwise):

    $$\ln(y) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 D + \beta_2 x + \varepsilon$$

    Because \(D\) jumps discretely from 0 to 1, we cannot take a calculus derivative \(\partial \ln(y) / \partial D\). Instead, holding \(x\) and \(\varepsilon\) fixed for the algebraic comparison:

    Step-by-Step Algebraic Comparison

    For the baseline group (\(D = 0\)):

    $$\ln(y_0) = \beta_0 + \beta_2 x + \varepsilon \implies y_0 = \exp(\beta_0 + \beta_2 x + \varepsilon)$$

    For the treatment group (\(D = 1\)):

    $$\ln(y_1) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 + \beta_2 x + \varepsilon \implies y_1 = \exp(\beta_0 + \beta_1 + \beta_2 x + \varepsilon)$$

    Taking the ratio of the two outcomes:

    $$\frac{y_1}{y_0} = \frac{\exp(\beta_0 + \beta_1 + \beta_2 x + \varepsilon)}{\exp(\beta_0 + \beta_2 x + \varepsilon)} = e^{\beta_1}$$

    Therefore, the relative change is:

    $$\frac{y_1 - y_0}{y_0} = \frac{y_1}{y_0} - 1 = e^{\beta_1} - 1$$

    Multiplying by 100 yields the exact percentage change:

    SEMI-LOG SPECIFICATION Dummy Variable Percentage Change: Exact vs. Linear Approximation
    $$\text{Exact: } 100(e^{\beta_1} - 1)\% \qquad \text{Approximation: } 100\beta_1\%$$
    WHERE
    \(e^{\beta_1} - 1\) Relative change: Proportional outcome difference \(\frac{y_1 - y_0}{y_0}\) holding \(x\) and \(\varepsilon\) fixed
    \(100(e^{\beta_1} - 1)\%\) Exact percentage change: True percentage effect on \(y\) associated with switching indicator \(D\) from 0 to 1
    \(100\beta_1\%\) Linear approximation: First-order Taylor approximation; accurate when \(|\beta_1|\) is close to zero
    \(\beta_1\) Dummy coefficient: Population parameter multiplying binary regressor \(D \in \{0, 1\}\)

    Approximation Precision & Mathematical Properties

    In empirical studies, researchers often quote the linear shortcut \(100\beta_1\%\). Understanding when and how it diverges from the exact formula is critical:

    • For positive \(\beta_1\): Because \(e^{\beta_1} - 1 > \beta_1\) for all \(\beta_1 > 0\), the linear approximation \(100\beta_1\%\) understates the positive percentage change.
    • For negative \(\beta_1\): Because \(e^{\beta_1} - 1 < \beta_1\) for negative values, the approximation becomes more negative than the exact percentage change in magnitude.
    • Accuracy for small \(|\beta_1|\): The approximation is most accurate when \(|\beta_1|\) is small (e.g. \(|\beta_1| \le 0.05\)). For larger magnitudes, the exponential formula should always be reported.
    💡 Numerical Example (\(\beta_1 = 0.20\))

    Suppose an estimated wage equation yields \(\hat{\beta}_1 = 0.20\) for a college degree dummy:
    • Linear approximation: \(100 \cdot (0.20)\% = \mathbf{20.00\%}\)
    • Exact percentage change: \(100(e^{0.20} - 1)\% \approx 100(1.2214 - 1)\% = \mathbf{22.14\%}\)
    The linear approximation understates the true estimated percentage change by more than 2.1 percentage points.

    Reporting the naive approximation in policy or academic papers for substantial coefficients significantly misstates economic impact.

    ECONOMETRIC INTERPRETATION

    Because \(e^x = 1 + x + x^2/2! + \dots\), the approximation ignores all higher-order terms in the Taylor series expansion.

    2. Module 1 Synthesis: The Econometric Inquiry Pipeline

    You have covered the foundational pillars of linear econometric modeling. Every applied empirical research project follows this systematic roadmap:

    1. ECONOMIC RESEARCH QUESTION
    What is the theoretical hypothesis? Does policy require a causal effect?
    ↓
    2. CAUSATION OR PREDICTION?
    Causal inquiry requires ceteris paribus; prediction seeks optimal forecast \(\hat{y}\).
    ↓
    3. SPECIFY ECONOMETRIC MODEL
    Choose functional form (linear, logs, quadratics) to ensure linearity in parameters.
    ↓
    4. ESTIMATE VIA OLS: \(\mathbf{\hat{\beta} = (X'X)^{-1}X'y}\)
    Minimizes sum of squared residuals; projects \(y\) orthogonally onto \(\text{Col}(X)\).
    ↓
    5. VALIDATE CLRM ASSUMPTIONS
    Full rank (\(n \ge k\)), Exogeneity (\(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon|X]=0\)), Spherical errors (\(\sigma^2 I\)).
    ↓
    6. INTERPRET COEFFICIENTS & DRAW POLICY CONCLUSIONS
    Marginal effects, elasticities, exact dummy impact, and economic significance.

    3. The 12 Fundamental Review Cards

    01 Regression Model
    \(y = X\beta + \varepsilon\)

    Observed outcomes are decomposed into a systematic component \(X\beta\) and an unobserved disturbance \(\varepsilon\).

    02 OLS Estimation
    \(\hat{\beta} = (X'X)^{-1}X'y\)

    When \(X\) has full column rank, OLS chooses coefficients that minimize the sum of squared residuals.

    03 Error vs. Residual
    \(\varepsilon_i \neq e_i\)

    \(\varepsilon_i\) is the unobserved population disturbance. \(e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i\) is the sample residual calculated after estimation.

    04 Linearity in Parameters
    \(y = X\beta + \varepsilon\)

    The regression must be linear in the unknown coefficients, not necessarily in the regressors themselves.

    \(x\), \(x^2\), and \(\ln(x)\) may all appear in a model that is still linear in parameters.
    05 Full Rank
    \(\operatorname{rank}(X) = k\)

    No regressor column is an exact linear combination of the others.

    Consequence: \(X'X\) is invertible.
    06 Zero Conditional Mean / Exogeneity
    \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid X] = 0\)

    Once \(X\) is conditioned on, the disturbance contains no systematic mean component related to the regressors.

    Key condition supporting OLS unbiasedness in the classical model.
    07 Homoskedasticity
    \(\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_i \mid X) = \sigma^2\)

    Every observation has the same conditional disturbance variance.

    If violated: OLS can remain unbiased under exogeneity, but conventional variance formulas are not valid.
    08 No Autocorrelation
    \(\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon_i, \varepsilon_j \mid X) = 0, \; i \neq j\)

    Conditional on \(X\), disturbances from different observations are uncorrelated.

    If violated: Conventional standard errors and classical efficiency results are affected.
    09 Normality
    \(\varepsilon \mid X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)\)

    Normality is used for exact finite-sample distributional results.

    Not required for OLS unbiasedness or consistency.
    10 Linear Marginal Association
    \(\partial \mathbb{E}[y \mid x]/\partial x = \beta\)

    In a linear conditional-mean model, \(\beta\) is the change in expected \(y\) associated with a one-unit change in \(x\).

    Causal only with appropriate identification assumptions.
    11 Log-Log Elasticity
    \(\ln(y) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \ln(x) + \varepsilon\)

    \(\beta_1\) is an elasticity: a 1% change in \(x\) is associated with approximately a \(\beta_1\%\) change in \(y\).

    12 Dummy Variable in a Log Model
    \(\text{Exact: } 100(e^{\beta_1} - 1)\% \quad \text{Approx: } 100\beta_1\%\)

    Use the exponential transformation for the exact percentage difference associated with switching the dummy from 0 to 1.

    4. Comprehensive Practice Exam

    Test your mastery across conceptual foundations, interpretations, assumption diagnostics, and calculations:

    CONCEPTUAL Question 1: Why does an observed negative correlation between class sizes and student test scores across public school districts not automatically prove that shrinking class sizes will raise exam performance?

    Think about omitted confounding variables that correlate with both district wealth and student educational resources.

    Solution:
    Observational correlation does not imply causation because of omitted confounding variables. Wealthier school districts can afford smaller class sizes, but they also attract more experienced teachers, provide state-of-the-art facilities, and have parents with higher educational attainment who invest in private tutoring. If these confounding factors are omitted, OLS attributes their positive impact on test scores to class size, biasing the slope estimate (violating Strict Exogeneity \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid X] = 0\)).

    INTERPRETATION Question 2: An analyst runs the multiple regression: $$\widehat{\text{TestScore}} = 740 - 2.5 \cdot \text{ClassSize} + 1.8 \cdot \text{TeacherExp} + 0.4 \cdot \text{Income}$$ Provide the precise economic interpretation of the slope coefficient \(-2.5\).

    Always explicitly state the ceteris paribus condition (what is being held constant).

    Solution:
    "Holding teacher experience and district average income constant (ceteris paribus), an increase of one student per classroom is associated with an expected 2.5-point decrease in average district test scores."

    ASSUMPTION DIAGNOSTIC Question 3: In a firm-level dataset, a student regresses profit on: Total Revenue (\(x_2\)), Total Costs (\(x_3\)), and Operating Margin (\(x_4 = x_2 - x_3\)). Which CLRM assumption is violated, and what will happen when the student attempts to run OLS?

    Examine whether \(x_4\) is an exact linear combination of \(x_2\) and \(x_3\).

    Solution:
    - Assumption A2 (Full Column Rank) is violated completely.
    - Because \(x_4 = x_2 - x_3\) holds identically for every firm, column 4 is an exact linear combination of columns 2 and 3 (perfect multicollinearity).
    - The matrix \(X'X\) is singular (\(\text{det}(X'X) = 0\)), its inverse \((X'X)^{-1}\) does not exist, and statistical software will report an error or drop one of the three variables.

    FUNCTIONAL FORM Question 4: An econometrician estimates: $$\widehat{\ln(\text{Wage})} = 2.1 + 0.08 \cdot \text{Education} + 0.04 \cdot \text{Experience}$$ Approximately how much does an additional year of education change wage? If an employee receives 3 more years of education, what is the approximate percentage increase?

    Use the semi-log rule: \(\% \Delta y \approx 100 \cdot \beta \cdot \Delta x\).

    Solution:
    - For a 1-year increase in education: \(\% \Delta \text{Wage} \approx 100 \cdot (0.08) = \mathbf{\approx 8\%}\).
    - For 3 additional years of education: \(\% \Delta \text{Wage} \approx 100 \cdot (0.08 \times 3) = \mathbf{\approx 24\%}\).

    DUMMY VARIABLE Question 5: In the wage model \(\ln(\text{Wage}) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{Union} + \beta_2 \text{Exper} + \varepsilon\), the estimated union coefficient is \(\hat{\beta}_1 = 0.15\).
    (a) Calculate the naive approximate percentage effect.
    (b) Calculate the exact percentage effect using \(100(e^{\beta_1} - 1)\%\).
    (c) What is the discrepancy?

    Use \(e^{0.15} \approx 1.16183\).

    Solution:
    (a) Naive approximation: \(100 \cdot 0.15 = \mathbf{15.00\%}\).
    (b) Exact effect: \(100 \cdot (e^{0.15} - 1) = 100 \cdot (1.161834 - 1) = \mathbf{16.18\%}\).
    (c) Discrepancy: \(16.18\% - 15.00\% = \mathbf{1.18 \text{ percentage points}}\). The naive calculation underestimates the union premium by more than 1.1 percentage points.
