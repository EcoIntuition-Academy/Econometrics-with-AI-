LESSON 6.2 Precision & Variance

Omitted Variables, Variance & Standard Errors

Lesson 6.1 examined bias: "Is \(\hat{\beta}_1\) centered on \(\beta_1\)?" Now we examine precision: "How variable is \(\hat{\beta}_1\) across samples?" Discover how omitting or including regressors affects sampling uncertainty, explore the competing forces of residual variance versus multicollinearity, and examine the canonical standard-error summary table from page 5 of the source.

LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • Strictly distinguish between estimator bias (centering) and standard error (precision).
  • Inspect the partitioned regression matrix variance formulas for omitting versus including \(X_2\).
  • Identify the two competing forces that affect standard errors when adding regressors: residual variance reduction vs. collinearity penalty.
  • Master the 4-case system classifying relevance (\(\beta_2 = 0\) vs. \(\beta_2 \neq 0\)) and correlation (\(X_1'X_2 = 0\) vs. \(X_1'X_2 \neq 0\)).
  • Recognize that Case 2 (\(X_1'X_2 \neq 0, \beta_2 = 0\)) is the single scenario where omitting a variable unambiguously improves precision.

1. The Fundamental Distinction: Bias vs. Precision

In econometric specification, students frequently confuse an estimator’s expected location with its spread. A model can have small standard errors but be wildly biased, or it can be perfectly unbiased but suffer from large sampling uncertainty.

BIAS (Location / Centering)

Defined as \(\operatorname{Bias}(\hat{\beta}_1) = \mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}_1] - \beta_1\). It measures whether the estimator is centered on the true population parameter across hypothetical repeated samples. Unbiasedness guarantees that our procedure does not systematically overshoot or undershoot truth.

PRECISION (Spread / Standard Error)

Measured by the sampling variance \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\beta}_1)\) and its square root, the Standard Error (\(\operatorname{SE}\)). It dictates how tightly estimates cluster around their mean. High precision means little noise between individual realized samples.

2. Source Variance Expressions: Omitting vs. Including Regressors

From pages 3–4 of the primary source, consider how the conditional variance of the estimated coefficient on \(X_1\) differs between the restricted model (omitting \(X_2\)) and the unrestricted model (including \(X_2\)).

Theoretical Variance When Omitting X₂ (Restricted)
\[\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\beta}_1^{\text{omit}} \mid X_1) = \sigma_{\text{omit}}^2 (X_1'X_1)^{-1}\]
Where \(\sigma_{\text{omit}}^2 = \operatorname{Var}(\omega_i)\) is the variance of the composite error term that absorbs \(X_2\beta_2\).
Theoretical Variance When Including X₂ (Unrestricted Partitioned Regression)
\[\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\beta}_1^{\text{incl}} \mid X_1, X_2) = \sigma_{\text{incl}}^2 \Big[ X_1' \big(I - X_2(X_2'X_2)^{-1}X_2'\big) X_1 \Big]^{-1} = \sigma_{\text{incl}}^2 (X_1' M_2 X_1)^{-1}\]
Where \(M_2 = I - X_2(X_2'X_2)^{-1}X_2'\) is the orthogonal projection matrix that partials out \(X_2\) from \(X_1\).

By matrix algebra, the matrix \((X_1' M_2 X_1)^{-1} - (X_1'X_1)^{-1}\) is positive semi-definite! This means that partialing out \(X_2\) removes independent variation in \(X_1\), which mechanically increases the matrix factor. However, this is only half the story.

The Two Competing Forces Operating on Standard Errors

FORCE 1 (Good)
Residual Variance Reduction (\(\sigma^2 \downarrow\)): If \(X_2\) explains variation in \(y\) (\(\beta_2 \neq 0\)), adding it to the regression removes that variation from the residual. A lower residual variance tends to decrease standard errors.
FORCE 2 (Bad)
Collinearity Penalty (\((X_1'M_2 X_1)^{-1} \uparrow\)): If \(X_2\) is correlated with \(X_1\) (\(X_1'X_2 \neq 0\)), adding it means \(X_1\) and \(X_2\) compete to explain the same variance. Less unique variation remains in \(X_1\), which tends to inflate standard errors.

5. Common Methodological Pitfall

METHODOLOGICAL CAUTION Common Mistake: Choosing Models Solely by Standard Errors

"When I dropped variable \(X_2\), the standard error on my main treatment variable dropped by 30%. Therefore, omitting \(X_2\) improved my specification."

Correction: A lower standard error does NOT indicate an unbiased or valid specification! If \(X_2\) is relevant and correlated with treatment, dropping it will create severe omitted variable bias. A precisely estimated lie is far worse than an honest estimate with wider confidence intervals.

Unbiasedness and causal identification must always take precedence over superficial precision gains.

QUICK CHECK When Does Omitting a Regressor Unambiguously Improve Precision?

Under which specific combination of correlation and relevance does omitting \(X_2\) definitely help precision without introducing omitted variable bias into \(\hat{\beta}_1\)?

Show Answer & Explanation
ANSWER: Case 2: When \(X_2\) is CORRELATED with \(X_1\) (\(X_1'X_2 \neq 0\)) and IRRELEVANT to the outcome (\(\beta_2 = 0\)).

WHY?
  1. Because \(\beta_2 = 0\), omitting \(X_2\) causes zero OVB: \(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}_1 \mid X_1, X_2] = \beta_1 + (X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'X_2 (0) = \beta_1\).
  2. Because \(\beta_2 = 0\), including \(X_2\) would not reduce the residual variance \(\sigma^2\).
  3. However, because \(X_1'X_2 \neq 0\), including \(X_2\) forces \(X_1\) to share variation with \(X_2\), inflating \((X_1' M_2 X_1)^{-1}\). Omitting \(X_2\) eliminates this multicollinearity, shrinking \(\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\beta}_1)\)!
TAKEAWAYS

What You Should Now Understand

  • Bias refers to the expected centering of an estimator; standard error refers to its sampling variability.
  • Including regressors triggers two competing forces: it can reduce residual variance \(\sigma^2\) (shrinking SE) or induce multicollinearity (inflating SE).
  • In the 4-case system, omitting an irrelevant correlated regressor (Case 2) is the only scenario where dropping a variable unambiguously improves precision.
  • Omitting a relevant uncorrelated regressor (Case 3) remains unbiased but inflates standard errors because residual variance \(\sigma^2\) is unnecessarily large.
  • Never drop a relevant regressor simply to lower standard errors: a small standard error around a biased estimate invalidates causal inference.
