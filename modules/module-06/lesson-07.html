LESSON 6.7 Triple Differences

Difference-in-Difference-in-Differences (DDD)

What happens when standard Difference-in-Differences fails because a state-level economic shock coincides with policy enactment? Discover Difference-in-Difference-in-Differences (DDD), explore the primary source's Medicaid expansion intuition, and see how a third dimension of within-state comparison purges confounding through the triple interaction.

LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • Identify why concurrent region-specific shocks invalidate conventional 2×2 Difference-in-Differences.
  • Define the DDD estimator as the difference between two separate DD contrasts: \(\text{DDD} = \text{DD}_1 - \text{DD}_2\).
  • Apply the source's Medicaid expansion example comparing eligible poor children to ineligible non-poor children.
  • Map the three dimensions of variation: Location/Policy (\(S\)) \(\times\) Demographic Group (\(G\)) \(\times\) Time (\(T\)).
  • Understand how a saturated regression with a triple interaction isolates the causal DDD parameter.

1. Motivation: When Standard DD Fails

In a standard 2×2 Difference-in-Differences design, we compare changes over time in a treated state against changes in an untreated state.

The Scenario: Suppose that at the exact same time the treated state passes its law, its local economy experiences a separate state-specific macroeconomic shock (for example, a regional energy boom or localized technology expansion).

CONFOUNDING PROBLEM Standard DD Mixes Two Effects
\[\Delta_{\text{Treated State}} - \Delta_{\text{Control State}} = \text{Policy Effect} + \text{State-Specific Macro Shock}\]

Because both the policy and the regional economic shock occur simultaneously in the treated jurisdiction, standard DD cannot disentangle the policy impact from the macro shock.

KEY IDEA

To eliminate the state-specific shock, we need another comparison group exposed to the same local economic environment but not to the policy itself. This naturally motivates Difference-in-Difference-in-Differences (DDD).

2. The Primary Source Medicaid Example (Page 15)

Page 15 of the primary source introduces a classic textbook example: evaluating the impact of state-level Medicaid eligibility expansion on child health outcomes.

Some states expanded Medicaid while others did not. However, expansion states may have differed in their broader health care infrastructure or local economic growth. To isolate the true policy impact, DDD compares eligible poor children to ineligible non-poor children within the same states:

DIFFERENCE-IN-DIFFERENCES #1 (ELIGIBLE GROUP)
\(\text{DD}_{\text{poor}} = \Delta_{\text{poor, expansion}} - \Delta_{\text{poor, non-expansion}}\)
Captures: True Medicaid Effect + State Economic Shock + Demographic Trend.
DIFFERENCE-IN-DIFFERENCES #2 (INELIGIBLE COMPARISON GROUP)
\(\text{DD}_{\text{rich}} = \Delta_{\text{rich, expansion}} - \Delta_{\text{rich, non-expansion}}\)
Captures: State Economic Shock + Demographic Trend (Zero Medicaid Effect, since non-poor children are ineligible).
THE TRIPLE DIFFERENCE (DDD)
\(\text{DDD} = \text{DD}_{\text{poor}} - \text{DD}_{\text{rich}} = \text{True Causal Policy Effect}\)
Subtracting \(\text{DD}_{\text{rich}}\) completely purges the state economic shock!

3. The Three Dimensions of Variation

A DDD design simultaneously exploits three distinct binary dimensions of variation to eliminate confounding:

DIMENSION 1: GEOGRAPHY / POLICY
Expansion State (\(S = 1\)) vs Non-Expansion (\(S = 0\))

Controls for national macroeconomic trends common to all states.

DIMENSION 2: ELIGIBILITY / TARGET
Poor Group (\(G = 1\)) vs Rich Group (\(G = 0\))

Controls for state-level macroeconomic shocks common to all residents.

DIMENSION 3: TIME HORIZON
Post-Reform (\(T = 1\)) vs Pre-Reform (\(T = 0\))

Controls for permanent baseline disparities across demographic groups.

4. The Triple Interaction Regression Framework

In practice, empirical researchers estimate DDD in a single saturated OLS regression. For binary dimensions \(S_i \in \{0, 1\}\), \(G_i \in \{0, 1\}\), and \(T_t \in \{0, 1\}\):

SATURATED REGRESSION SPECIFICATION Triple Interaction OLS Model
\[\begin{aligned} y_{it} = \beta_0 &+ \beta_1 S_i + \beta_2 G_i + \beta_3 T_t \\ &+ \beta_4 (S_i \cdot G_i) + \beta_5 (S_i \cdot T_t) + \beta_6 (G_i \cdot T_t) \\ &+ \mathbf{\beta_7 (S_i \cdot G_i \cdot T_t)} + \varepsilon_{it} \end{aligned}\]

The Triple Interaction Coefficient (\(\beta_7\)): Captures the unique causal effect on the target group in the treated state during the post-period, after controlling for all main effects and all two-way interaction confounders.

WHAT YOU ARE SEEING

The decomposition of the DDD estimator into the target Difference-in-Differences (\(\text{DD}_1\)), the comparison Difference-in-Differences (\(\text{DD}_2\)), and the net DDD estimate.

WHAT TO NOTICE

Even if the state economy booms and inflates \(\text{DD}_1\) all the way to \(+12.0\), \(\text{DD}_2\) captures that exact same \(+6.0\) state-and-demographic shock. Subtracting them yields the true policy effect of \(+6.0\).

WHY IT MATTERS

Standard DD would falsely report a policy impact of \(+12.0\). DDD protects the econometrician from taking credit for broader regional trends that affected all residents equally.

ECONOMETRIC INTERPRETATION

The identifying assumption of DDD is weaker than DD: it requires that the gap between poor and rich children would have followed parallel trends across states in the absence of treatment.

6. Common Methodological Pitfall

METHODOLOGICAL CAUTION Common Mistake: Choosing an Affected Comparison Group

"To evaluate Medicaid expansion for low-income children, we used low-income adults in the same state as our third difference comparison group."

Correction: If the comparison group is also partially affected by the policy (or if Medicaid expansion crowded out private clinics used by low-income adults), the comparison group is contaminated!

For DDD to be valid, the comparison group must experience the local macroeconomic shock while being strictly unexposed to the policy itself. Ineligible high-income children are an ideal comparison because their Medicaid eligibility probability is essentially zero.

QUICK CHECK Why Not Always Use DDD?

If DDD controls for more confounding shocks than standard DD, why don't empirical economists use DDD for every single evaluation?

Show Answer & Explanation
ANSWER: DDD requires finding a credible third comparison group and typically suffers from substantially larger standard errors.

WHY?
  1. Finding a group that shares the exact same regional economic environment but is completely immune to the policy (and free of spillover effects) is very difficult.
  2. Statistically, subtracting two estimated differences adds their sampling variances: \(\operatorname{Var}(\text{DDD}) = \operatorname{Var}(\text{DD}_1) + \operatorname{Var}(\text{DD}_2)\). Standard errors in DDD are substantially larger, demanding much larger sample sizes to achieve statistical significance!
TAKEAWAYS

What You Should Now Understand

  • Standard Difference-in-Differences fails when region-specific economic shocks coincide with policy enactment.
  • DDD compares two Difference-in-Differences estimates: \(\text{DDD} = \text{DD}_{\text{Target}} - \text{DD}_{\text{Comparison}}\).
  • In the Medicaid example, comparing poor children across states (\(\text{DD}_1\)) is purged of state economic trends by subtracting the comparison across rich children (\(\text{DD}_2\)).
  • In a saturated regression, the triple interaction coefficient \(\beta_7\) on \(S_i \cdot G_i \cdot T_t\) directly captures the DDD treatment effect.
  • The third group must share the same macroeconomic environment while remaining strictly unexposed to the policy.
