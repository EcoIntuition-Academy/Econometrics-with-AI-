LESSON 6.3 Experiments & Controls

Controls & OVB in Randomized Experiments

Why are randomized controlled trials (RCTs) considered the gold standard for causal inference? Discover how random assignment breaks the correlation condition for Omitted Variable Bias, understand why pre-treatment controls are not needed for unbias, and explore why econometricians still include them to boost statistical precision.

LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • Formulate the regression model for a randomized experiment with treatment indicator \(D_i\) and baseline controls \(X_i\).
  • Explain why randomized assignment ensures that \(\operatorname{Cov}(D_i, X_i) = 0\) in expectation, eliminating systematic OVB.
  • Distinguish between the sample orthogonality shorthand \(D'X \approx 0\) and strict independence in expectation.
  • Demonstrate how predictive controls reduce the residual variance \(\hat{\sigma}^2\) without incurring a multicollinearity penalty.
  • Analyze the source's FAFSA text-reminder numerical illustration showing point estimates and shrinking standard errors.

1. The Randomized Experiment Setup

Consider an experiment where individuals are randomly assigned to either a treatment group (\(D_i = 1\)) or a control group (\(D_i = 0\)). We measure an outcome \(y_i\) and have access to pre-treatment characteristics \(X_i\) (such as baseline test scores, parental income, or age).

Experimental Regression with Baseline Controls
\[y_i = \alpha + \beta D_i + \gamma X_i + \varepsilon_i\]
Where \(D_i \in \{0, 1\}\) is randomly assigned, \(\beta\) is the average treatment effect, \(X_i\) represents pre-treatment controls, and \(\varepsilon_i\) is an idiosyncratic error term.

Suppose a researcher runs the simple regression omitting all baseline controls \(X_i\):

Simple Experimental Regression (Omitting Controls)
\[y_i = \alpha + \beta D_i + \omega_i \quad \text{where} \quad \omega_i = \gamma X_i + \varepsilon_i\]
The composite error term \(\omega_i\) absorbs the influence of the omitted baseline characteristics \(\gamma X_i\).

2. Randomization and Covariate Orthogonality

From Lesson 6.1, we know that omitting \(X_i\) causes bias in the treatment effect estimator \(\hat{\beta}\) only if:

\(\operatorname{Bias}(\hat{\beta}) = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(D_i, X_i)}{\operatorname{Var}(D_i)} \times \gamma\)

Under randomized assignment, the coin flip or lottery that assigns \(D_i\) is completely independent of the subject’s pre-treatment characteristics by design!

THE RANDOMIZATION MECHANISM

1. Independence by Design: Because assignment to treatment \(D\) was generated by a physical randomization device (e.g. computer RNG), \(D_i \perp X_i\). In expectation:
\(\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Cov}(D_i, X_i)] = 0\)
2. No Systematic OVB: Because the correlation condition fails in expectation, omitting baseline controls \(X_i\) does NOT create systematic omitted variable bias in \(\hat{\beta}\).
3. Academic Caution on Notation: The primary source sometimes uses the idealized shorthand \(D'X = 0\) to communicate this orthogonality. In finite realized samples, \(D'X\) is rarely exactly zero due to minor random sampling fluctuations, but it is zero in expectation under the randomization design.

3. If Treatment is Randomized, Why Include Controls?

If omitting \(X_i\) produces an unbiased estimate of the treatment effect, why would an econometrician bother collecting and adjusting for baseline controls?

The answer lies in statistical precision:

1. Shrinking Residual Variance (\(\hat{\sigma}^2 \downarrow\))

Pre-treatment controls explain part of the natural variation in \(y_i\). When included, they pull that variation out of the residual term, driving down \(\hat{\sigma}^2\).

2. No Collinearity Penalty!

Because \(D_i \perp X_i\), adding \(X_i\) does NOT induce multicollinearity with \(D_i\). We get the full benefit of variance reduction without the variance inflation penalty!

3. Tighter Confidence Intervals

Since \(\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\beta}) \propto \frac{\hat{\sigma}}{\sqrt{n}}\), reducing \(\hat{\sigma}\) directly narrows standard errors, lowers \(p\)-values, and increases statistical power.

4. Numerical Source Example: FAFSA Reminder Study

Pages 8–9 of the primary source present a real-world example from an experimental evaluation of text-message reminders designed to encourage college financial aid (FAFSA) renewal:

FAFSA Text-Reminder Experiment Results

Source Illustration

\(\hat{\beta} = +0.030\)
\(\operatorname{SE} = 0.017\)  |  \(p \approx 0.078\)

\(\hat{\beta} = +0.027\)

Point estimate barely moves; SE shrinks by ~12%!
What Changed?
  • The Point Estimate: Moved trivially from \(0.030\) to \(0.027\). Because treatment was randomized, controls were not needed to remove confounding. The slight shift is pure finite-sample noise.
  • The Standard Error: Dropped from \(0.017\) to \(0.015\). Baseline demographic and academic covariates soaked up outcome variation, yielding a sharper, more precise estimate!

WHY IT MATTERS

ECONOMETRIC INTERPRETATION

Because \(D_i \perp X_i\) by design, \(\hat{\beta}_D\) does not suffer from OVB. Including \(X_i\) does not change the expectation of \(\hat{\beta}_D\); it purely improves sample precision by reducing \(\hat{\sigma}^2\).

6. Common Methodological Pitfall

METHODOLOGICAL CAUTION Common Mistake: "Controls Are Useless in an RCT"

"Since our experiment was perfectly randomized, there is no confounding, so controlling for baseline covariates is a waste of time."

Correction: While randomization prevents bias, it does nothing to eliminate outcome variation caused by pre-existing differences between individuals.

If high school GPA strongly predicts college completion, controlling for baseline GPA in a mentoring RCT will explain a substantial portion of the outcome variance. This drives down residual error variance \(\sigma^2\) and produces smaller standard errors, making the experiment significantly more powerful at zero additional cost.

QUICK CHECK What Happens When You Add Non-Predictive Controls in an RCT?

In a randomized trial, what happens to \(\hat{\beta}_D\) and its standard error if you add a baseline control \(X\) that has zero predictive power for \(y\) (\(\gamma = 0\))?

Show Answer & Explanation
ANSWER: \(\hat{\beta}_D\) remains unbiased, but its estimated standard error will increase very slightly (or stay virtually unchanged).

WHY?
  1. Because \(\gamma = 0\), adding \(X\) does not reduce the residual sum of squares.
  2. Because \(X \perp D\), adding \(X\) does not induce collinearity with treatment.
  3. However, adding an extra parameter costs 1 degree of freedom (\(n - k\) in the denominator of \(s^2\)). In small samples, this degree-of-freedom loss slightly inflates estimated standard errors without any explanatory payoff. This is why researchers only pre-specify predictive baseline controls!
TAKEAWAYS

What You Should Now Understand

  • Randomized assignment ensures that treatment \(D_i\) is statistically independent of baseline covariates \(X_i\) (\(\operatorname{Cov}(D_i, X_i) = 0\) in expectation).
  • Because the correlation condition fails, omitting baseline controls in an RCT does not cause systematic omitted variable bias.
  • Econometricians include predictive baseline controls in RCTs to absorb residual outcome variance, lowering \(\hat{\sigma}^2\) and shrinking standard errors.
  • Because \(D_i \perp X_i\), adding baseline controls in an RCT does not cause a collinearity penalty.
  • In the FAFSA reminder experiment, adding controls moved the point estimate only slightly (from 0.030 to 0.027) while cutting the standard error from 0.017 to 0.015.
