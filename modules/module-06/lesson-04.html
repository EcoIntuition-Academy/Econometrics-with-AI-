LESSON 6.4 Quasi-Experiments

Difference-in-Differences Fundamentals

Randomized experiments create clean treatment variation by design. But how can economists evaluate real-world policy reforms when randomization is impossible? Discover the Difference-in-Differences (DD) design, understand how the 2×2 group-by-time regression isolates treatment impacts, and derive why the interaction coefficient \(\beta_4\) recovers the causal effect.

LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • Formulate the canonical 2×2 Difference-in-Differences regression specification from page 10 of the source.
  • Interpret each of the four parameters (\(\beta_1, \beta_2, \beta_3, \beta_4\)) in terms of group baselines and time trends.
  • Construct the 2×2 group-time table and algebraically derive \(\beta_4\) as the difference between changes.
  • Explain how double differencing removes both permanent group unobserved heterogeneity and common macroeconomic shocks.
  • Use the interactive DD calculator to observe how changes in baseline gaps and secular trends alter empirical estimates.

1. Motivation & The Canonical DD Regression

In observational policy analysis, researchers frequently observe two groups:

  • A Treated Group (\(D_i = 1\)) that is exposed to a policy reform (such as a state minimum wage increase).
  • A Control Group (\(D_i = 0\)) that remains unexposed.

Both groups are observed across two time horizons: a Pre-period (\(\text{Time}_t = 0\)) and a Post-period (\(\text{Time}_t = 1\)).

The Canonical 2×2 Difference-in-Differences Regression
\[y_{it} = \beta_1 + \beta_2 \text{Time}_t + \beta_3 D_i + \beta_4 (\text{Time}_t \cdot D_i) + \varepsilon_{it}\]
Where \(y_{it}\) is the outcome for unit \(i\) at time \(t\), \(\text{Time}_t \in \{0, 1\}\) indicates the post-reform period, \(D_i \in \{0, 1\}\) indicates membership in the treated group, and \(\text{Time}_t \cdot D_i\) is the interaction term.

2. What Each Coefficient Means

Every parameter in the 2×2 DD regression has a distinct economic interpretation:

INTERCEPT
\(\beta_1\)

Control Group Pre Mean: The baseline average outcome of the untreated comparison group before the policy takes effect (\(\text{Time}=0, D=0\)).

TIME TREND
\(\beta_2\)

Common Time Change: The change in outcome experienced over time by the control group. It captures shared macroeconomic trends or secular drift.

BASELINE GAP
\(\beta_3\)

Initial Group Difference: The pre-existing baseline disparity between the treated group and the control group prior to treatment (\(D=1\) vs \(D=0\)).

DD COEFFICIENT
\(\beta_4\)

Treatment Effect: The differential change in outcome over time for the treated group relative to the control group.

3. The 2×2 Group-Time Table & Algebraic Derivation

Evaluating the regression equation at each combination of \(D_i \in \{0, 1\}\) and \(\text{Time}_t \in \{0, 1\}\) produces the canonical 2×2 expectation matrix:

Group Pre-Period (\(\text{Time} = 0\)) Post-Period (\(\text{Time} = 1\)) Time Difference (\(\Delta\))
Control (\(D = 0\)) \(\beta_1\) \(\beta_1 + \beta_2\) \(\beta_2\)
Treated (\(D = 1\)) \(\beta_1 + \beta_3\) \(\beta_1 + \beta_2 + \beta_3 + \beta_4\) \(\beta_2 + \beta_4\)
Group Difference \(\beta_3\) \(\beta_3 + \beta_4\) \(\text{DD} = \mathbf{\beta_4}\)
Derivation Goal

Prove that \(\beta_4\) Equals the Difference of Differences

Demonstrate how taking the difference of changes between the treated and control groups isolates \(\beta_4\).

1

Compute Treated Group Change Over Time

\(\Delta_{\text{Treated}} = \beta_2 + \beta_4\)

Subtract the treated group's pre-period mean from its post-period mean:

\[\Delta_{\text{Treated}} = (\beta_1 + \beta_2 + \beta_3 + \beta_4) - (\beta_1 + \beta_3) = \beta_2 + \beta_4\]
Why this step works

This difference removes \(\beta_1\) (baseline) and \(\beta_3\) (permanent treated-group difference), leaving only the time trend plus the policy impact.

2

Compute Control Group Change Over Time

\(\Delta_{\text{Control}} = \beta_2\)

Subtract the control group's pre-period mean from its post-period mean:

\[\Delta_{\text{Control}} = (\beta_1 + \beta_2) - \beta_1 = \beta_2\]
Why this step works

Because the control group never received treatment, its change over time reflects only the common secular time shock \(\beta_2\).

3

Take the Difference of the Two Differences

\(\text{DD} = \beta_4\)

Subtract \(\Delta_{\text{Control}}\) from \(\Delta_{\text{Treated}}\):

\[\text{DD} = \Delta_{\text{Treated}} - \Delta_{\text{Control}} = (\beta_2 + \beta_4) - \beta_2 = \mathbf{\beta_4}\]
Why this step works

Subtracting \(\beta_2\) removes the common time trend, leaving purely the differential causal treatment effect \(\beta_4\).

THE INTUITION OF DOUBLE DIFFERENCING

A simple post-treatment cross-section comparison (\(D=1\) vs \(D=0\)) fails because groups may have had pre-existing baseline differences (\(\beta_3\)). A simple before-and-after comparison on the treated group fails because macroeconomic forces may have changed over time (\(\beta_2\)). Difference-in-Differences subtracts away both biases simultaneously!

The trajectories of both groups from the pre-treatment period (\(t=0\)) to the post-treatment period (\(t=1\)). The dashed blue line depicts the counterfactual trajectory: where the treated group would have landed without the policy.

WHAT TO NOTICE

Notice that the counterfactual dashed line is exactly parallel to the control group line (both have slope \(\beta_2\)). The vertical gap between the observed treated post point and this counterfactual dashed point is precisely the treatment effect \(\beta_4\).

WHY IT MATTERS

By comparing the treated change (\(\Delta = +14.0\)) against the control change (\(\Delta = +6.0\)), DD subtracts out the common time trend, isolating the true reform effect (\(\text{DD} = 14.0 - 6.0 = 8.0\)).

ECONOMETRIC INTERPRETATION

A naive comparison of post-treatment levels would suggest an effect of \(44 - 26 = +18.0\), which erroneously credits the baseline advantage (\(\beta_3 = 10\)) to the policy. DD purges this initial gap!

5. Common Methodological Pitfall

METHODOLOGICAL CAUTION Common Mistake: Demanding Equal Baseline Levels

"Difference-in-Differences cannot be applied here because the treated state had significantly higher initial wages than the control state."

Correction: Having different initial levels is NOT a violation of Difference-in-Differences! The parameter \(\beta_3\) in the regression specifically measures and controls for baseline level disparities.

What DD requires is that the two groups would have experienced the same change over time in the absence of treatment (the Parallel Trends assumption). Two states can have vastly different baseline wage levels and still serve as valid counterfactuals if their underlying growth trends are parallel.

QUICK CHECK Reading a DD Regression Table

An empirical paper reports: \(y_{it} = 15.0 + 3.0 \text{Time}_t + 4.0 D_i + 5.0 (\text{Time}_t \cdot D_i)\). What was the average outcome of the treated group in the post-treatment period, and what was the estimated treatment effect?

Show Answer & Explanation
ANSWER: Treated post mean = \(27.0\); Estimated treatment effect = \(+5.0\).

WHY?
  1. For the treated group in the post period, both \(\text{Time}_t = 1\) and \(D_i = 1\).
  2. Plugging these in: \(y = \beta_1 + \beta_2(1) + \beta_3(1) + \beta_4(1 \cdot 1) = 15.0 + 3.0 + 4.0 + 5.0 = 27.0\).
  3. The treatment effect is the interaction coefficient \(\beta_4 = +5.0\).
  4. Check via changes: Treated change = \(27.0 - (15.0 + 4.0) = 8.0\). Control change = \((15.0 + 3.0) - 15.0 = 3.0\). \(\text{DD} = 8.0 - 3.0 = 5.0\).
TAKEAWAYS

What You Should Now Understand

  • The canonical DD regression is \(y_{it} = \beta_1 + \beta_2 \text{Time}_t + \beta_3 D_i + \beta_4 (\text{Time}_t \cdot D_i) + \varepsilon_{it}\).
  • \(\beta_1\) is the control pre mean; \(\beta_2\) is the control time trend; \(\beta_3\) is the baseline group gap; \(\beta_4\) is the causal treatment effect.
  • Double differencing eliminates time-invariant group differences (\(\beta_3\)) and common secular shocks (\(\beta_2\)).
  • DD does NOT require groups to have identical initial levels; it allows different baseline values as long as their counterfactual changes are comparable.
  • The validity of DD depends fundamentally on whether the control group’s trajectory represents what would have happened to the treated group without the policy.
