LESSON 6.1 OVB Foundations

Omitted Variable Bias — Formal Derivation & Intuition

What happens when an econometrician omits an important variable from a regression model? Discover how included regressors accidentally absorb the influence of missing covariates, prove the canonical matrix bias formula, and explore the two essential conditions required for bias to exist.

LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • State the two necessary and sufficient conditions for Omitted Variable Bias (Correlation and Relevance).
  • Distinguish between the true data-generating process and the restricted estimated OLS model.
  • Follow the 6-step algebraic derivation of the conditional expectation \(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}_1 \mid X_1, X_2]\).
  • Decompose the bias term into the linear association \((X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'X_2\) multiplied by the omitted effect \(\beta_2\).
  • Apply the Sanaz and Mike cookie jar story to build physical intuition for the direction and magnitude of OLS bias.

1. Intuition First: Why Regressors Absorb Omitted Variables

In multiple regression, the Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) estimator attributes changes in the dependent variable \(y\) to the variables present in the model. If a relevant factor is left out of the regression, OLS does not simply ignore it—instead, any included regressor that is correlated with the omitted variable will accidentally absorb its influence.

For this bias to contaminate our estimates, two conditions must hold simultaneously:

OVB REQUIRES BOTH CONDITIONS TO HOLD

CONDITION 1: CORRELATION
\(X_1'X_2 \neq 0\)

The omitted regressor(s) \(X_2\) must be linearly correlated with the included regressor(s) \(X_1\). If they are completely uncorrelated, \(X_1\) cannot absorb \(X_2\)’s movements.

CONDITION 2: RELEVANCE
\(\beta_2 \neq 0\)

The omitted variable must actually matter for the outcome \(y\). If \(\beta_2 = 0\), the omitted variable has no population effect to pass onto \(X_1\).

CORRELATION (\(X_1'X_2 \neq 0\))  &  RELEVANCE (\(\beta_2 \neq 0\))  \(\implies\)  OMITTED VARIABLE BIAS

2. Formal Model Setup & Notation

Following the primary source setup, let the true population relationship between outcome \(y\) and explanatory variables be partitioned into included regressors \(X_1\) and omitted regressors \(X_2\):

True Data-Generating Process
\[y = X_1\beta_1 + X_2\beta_2 + \varepsilon\]
Where \(X_1\) is the \(n \times k_1\) matrix of included regressors (including the constant), \(X_2\) is the \(n \times k_2\) matrix of omitted regressors, \(\beta_1\) and \(\beta_2\) are true population parameter vectors, and \(\varepsilon\) satisfies \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid X_1, X_2] = 0\).

The econometrician does not observe or mistakenly excludes \(X_2\), running OLS solely on \(X_1\). This yields the restricted estimator:

Restricted OLS Estimator
\[\hat{\beta}_1 = (X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'y\]
Notation Note: The primary source writes this restricted OLS estimator as \(b_1\). This course website uses \(\hat{\beta}_1\) for consistency with previous modules.

3. Connected Derivation Journey: Finding \(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}_1 \mid X_1, X_2]\)

To discover whether \(\hat{\beta}_1\) is unbiased, we compute its mathematical expectation conditional on both \(X_1\) and \(X_2\).

Derivation Goal

Evaluate the Conditional Expectation of the Restricted Estimator

Find \(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}_1 \mid X_1, X_2]\) when the true data-generating process contains \(X_2\), and isolate the exact mathematical bias term.

1

Start with the Restricted OLS Formula

\(\hat{\beta}_1 = (X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'y\)

Write down the formula computed by the regression software that omits \(X_2\):

\[\hat{\beta}_1 = (X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'y\]
Why this step works

Because the econometrician omitted \(X_2\), the estimator is computed purely using the normal equations for \(X_1\).

2

Substitute the True Model for y

Insert DGP

Replace \(y\) with its true population expression \(y = X_1\beta_1 + X_2\beta_2 + \varepsilon\):

\[\hat{\beta}_1 = (X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'\Big(X_1\beta_1 + X_2\beta_2 + \varepsilon\Big)\]
Why this step works

This allows us to link the sample estimator directly to the true underlying parameters \(\beta_1\) and \(\beta_2\).

3

Distribute \((X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'\) Across Terms

Three Additive Terms

Expand the matrix multiplication across all three components:

\[\hat{\beta}_1 = (X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'X_1\beta_1 + (X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'X_2\beta_2 + (X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'\varepsilon\]
Why this step works

Matrix multiplication is distributive over matrix addition: \(A(B + C + D) = AB + AC + AD\).

4

Simplify the First Term via the Identity Matrix

\((X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'X_1 = I\)

Notice that the matrix inverse multiplies the original gram matrix:

\[(X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'X_1 = I \implies I\beta_1 = \beta_1\]
Why this step works

Any non-singular square matrix multiplied by its inverse yields the identity matrix \(I\).

5

Take the Conditional Expectation

\(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid X_1, X_2] = 0\)

Take expectation conditional on all regressors \(X_1\) and \(X_2\):

\[\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}_1 \mid X_1, X_2] = \beta_1 + (X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'X_2\beta_2 + (X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid X_1, X_2]}_{= 0}\]
Why this step works

Under the standard strict exogeneity assumption of the true DGP, the expected error is zero given the regressors.

6

Final Result: The Canonical OVB Expression

Bias Isolated

The conditional expectation of \(\hat{\beta}_1\) equals the true parameter plus the bias term:

\[\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}_1 \mid X_1, X_2] = \beta_1 + \underbrace{(X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'X_2 \beta_2}_{\text{Omitted Variable Bias}}\]
Why this step works

Whenever \((X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'X_2 \beta_2 \neq 0\), the estimator is systematically biased away from \(\beta_1\).

THE BIG PICTURE DECOMPOSITION

Notice what the term \((X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'X_2\) actually represents: it is the OLS coefficient matrix from regressing the omitted regressors \(X_2\) on the included regressors \(X_1\)! Hence, the bias loaded onto \(\hat{\beta}_1\) equals:

Bias = [Relationship between Included and Omitted] \(\times\) [True Effect of Omitted on Outcome]

4. The Cookie Story: Sanaz and Mike

To build intuition, page 2 of the primary source introduces the vivid classroom example of Sanaz and Mike raiding a shared jar of 100 cookies.

PRIMARY SOURCE CASE

The Cookie Jar Data-Generating Process

There are 100 cookies in the kitchen jar. Two roommates visit the jar:

  • Sanaz: takes exactly 2 cookies on each trip (\(\beta_1 = -2\)).
  • Mike: takes exactly 5 cookies on each trip (\(\beta_2 = -5\)).
True Jar Model
\[\text{Cookies Remaining} = 100 - 2 \cdot \text{SanazTrips} - 5 \cdot \text{MikeTrips} + \varepsilon\]

Now suppose the econometrics professor places a camera that only tracks Sanaz, leaving Mike unobserved. The estimated regression is:
\(\text{Cookies Remaining} = \alpha + \beta_1 \cdot \text{SanazTrips} + \omega\), where \(\omega = -5 \cdot \text{MikeTrips} + \varepsilon\).

WHAT TO NOTICE

When Sanaz and Mike are Always Together, every time Sanaz takes 2 cookies, Mike also takes 5. The regression attributes all 7 missing cookies to Sanaz, causing \(\hat{\beta}_1\) to plunge to \(-7.0\)!

WHY IT MATTERS

OLS cannot determine who ate the cookies. It only minimizes squared residuals based on the data it has. An omitted variable's effect is seamlessly transferred to any regressor that tags along with it.

ECONOMETRIC INTERPRETATION

The mathematical bias \((X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'X_2 \beta_2\) translates here to: \([+1.0 \text{ Mike trip per Sanaz trip}] \times [-5 \text{ cookies per Mike trip}] = -5 \text{ cookies of bias}\). Adding this to \(-2\) yields \(-7\).

5. Common Methodological Pitfall

METHODOLOGICAL CAUTION Common Mistake: "Every Omitted Variable Causes Bias"

"Because our regression omits hundreds of real-world variables, our OLS estimates are automatically biased."

Correction: In real life, econometrics models ALWAYS leave out countless variables. However, an omitted variable causes bias ONLY IF it is both relevant (\(\beta_2 \neq 0\)) AND correlated with an included variable (\(X_1'X_2 \neq 0\)).

If an omitted variable is completely independent of \(X_1\), it merely becomes part of the error term \(\varepsilon\). It inflates the error variance \(\sigma^2\) (affecting standard errors), but it induces ZERO BIAS into \(\hat{\beta}_1\)!

QUICK CHECK What Determines the Direction of OVB?

In a wage equation, suppose we estimate \(\ln(\text{wage}) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{educ} + \varepsilon\), omitting individual ability. If ability positively impacts wages (\(\beta_2 > 0\)) and is positively correlated with education (\(\operatorname{Cov}(\text{educ}, \text{ability}) > 0\)), what is the sign of the OVB, and will \(\hat{\beta}_1\) overestimate or underestimate the true return to schooling?

Show Answer & Explanation
ANSWER: The bias is POSITIVE, causing \(\hat{\beta}_1\) to OVERESTIMATE the true causal effect of education.

WHY?
  1. The formula for omitted variable bias is: \(\text{Bias} = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(\text{educ}, \text{ability})}{\operatorname{Var}(\text{educ})} \times \beta_{\text{ability}}\).
  2. Since \(\operatorname{Cov}(\text{educ}, \text{ability}) > 0\) and \(\beta_{\text{ability}} > 0\), the product is: \((+) \times (+) = (+)\).
  3. Therefore, \(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}_1] = \beta_1 + \text{Positive Bias} > \beta_1\). Education appears more lucrative than it actually is because it receives credit for the omitted native ability of the student!
TAKEAWAYS

What You Should Now Understand

  • OVB occurs when an included regressor absorbs the causal effect of an omitted variable with which it is correlated.
  • Two conditions are strictly necessary for bias: Correlation between included and omitted (\(X_1'X_2 \neq 0\)) AND Relevance of the omitted in the true DGP (\(\beta_2 \neq 0\)).
  • The expectation of the restricted estimator is \(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}_1 \mid X_1, X_2] = \beta_1 + (X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'X_2 \beta_2\).
  • The term \((X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'X_2\) is the regression of omitted regressors on included regressors.
  • If an omitted variable is uncorrelated with included regressors, OLS remains completely unbiased.
