LESSON 18.1 Discrete Choice Foundations

Unordered Choice & Multinomial Logit Foundations

Formulate discrete choice across multiple unordered alternatives from random utility theory, explore the role of Gumbel disturbances, derive closed-form choice probabilities, and understand why location normalization (β₀ = 0) is mathematically essential.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish unordered discrete choice from binary response and ordered selection models.
  • Specify the Random Utility Model \(U_{ij} = \mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}_j + \varepsilon_{ij}\) using observed individual characteristics and alternative-specific parameters.
  • Characterize the i.i.d. Gumbel (Type I Extreme Value) error assumption and how it yields logistic differences.
  • Derive the closed-form Multinomial Logit (MNL) probabilities and explain why setting \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_0 = \mathbf{0}\) is mathematically required for parameter identification.

1. The Unordered Choice Problem

In Module 16, we analyzed binary choice settings where an agent decides between two outcomes: \(y_i \in \{0, 1\}\) (e.g., labor force participation, loan approval, or home ownership). In empirical economics, however, agents frequently face choices among three or more mutually exclusive, unordered alternatives.

The canonical application introduced in the lecture source is occupational choice: an individual chooses one occupation from \(J + 1\) available alternatives (e.g., waiter, teacher, engineer, doctor, artist). Other classic settings include travel mode choice (walk, bus, train, car) and voting behavior among multiple political candidates.

DECISION ARCHITECTURE · RANDOM UTILITY IN UNORDERED CHOICE

Every decision maker observes their personal characteristics \(\mathbf{x}_i\) and selects the single option that yields the maximal total utility:

Individual \(i\) with Characteristics \(\mathbf{x}_i\) (Age, Gender, Education)
↓
Option 0 (Base) \(U_{i0} = \mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}_0 + \varepsilon_{i0}\)
Option 1 (e.g. Teacher) \(U_{i1} = \mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}_1 + \varepsilon_{i1}\)
Option 2 (e.g. Engineer) \(U_{i2} = \mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}_2 + \varepsilon_{i2}\)
↓
Selected Choice \(y_i = j \iff U_{ij} > U_{ik}\) for all \(k \ne j\)

2. The Random Utility Framework

The econometrician does not observe utility directly; utility is a latent construct. We observe the individual's socio-demographic characteristics \(\mathbf{x}_i\) and their realized categorical choice \(y_i \in \{0, 1, \dots, J\}\).

The latent utility that individual \(i\) derives from alternative \(j\) decomposes into a deterministic systematic component and an unobserved stochastic shock:

SPECIFICATION Random Utility Model (RUM)
\[ U_{ij} = \mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}_j + \varepsilon_{ij} \]
WHERE:
\(U_{ij}\) Latent total utility derived by individual \(i\) from choosing alternative \(j\).
\(\mathbf{x}_i\) Vector of observed individual characteristics (e.g., age, gender, education).
\(\boldsymbol{\beta}_j\) Alternative-specific parameter vector reflecting how covariates affect utility from option \(j\).
\(\varepsilon_{ij}\) Unobserved random utility disturbance idiosyncratic to person \(i\) and option \(j\).

Under utility maximization, individual \(i\) chooses alternative \(j\) if and only if alternative \(j\) delivers higher utility than every other available option \(k \ne j\). Therefore, the probability of selecting option \(j\) is:

PROBABILITY Utility Difference Choice Condition
\[ P(y_i = j \mid \mathbf{x}_i) = \Pr\left( \mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}_j + \varepsilon_{ij} > \mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}_k + \varepsilon_{ik}, \; \forall k \ne j \right) = \Pr\left( \varepsilon_{ij} - \varepsilon_{ik} > \mathbf{x}_i'(\boldsymbol{\beta}_k - \boldsymbol{\beta}_j), \; \forall k \ne j \right) \]

PEDAGOGICAL INTUITION · OFFSETTING SYSTEMATIC DEFICITS

As emphasized in the lecture notes: if an individual's characteristics predict that option \(j\) has very low systematic utility relative to option \(k\) (\(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}_j \ll \mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}_k\)), but the individual nonetheless chooses option \(j\), then their idiosyncratic random shock \(\varepsilon_{ij}\) must be exceptionally large relative to \(\varepsilon_{ik}\). The random shock must completely offset the systematic utility deficit. In a well-specified model, such extreme shocks are statistically rare, producing a small predicted choice probability.

3. Distributional Assumptions: Gumbel Errors

To transform the joint probability of utility differences into a computable choice probability, we must specify a joint distribution for the unobserved disturbance vector \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_i = (\varepsilon_{i0}, \varepsilon_{i1}, \dots, \varepsilon_{iJ})'\).

The Multinomial Logit (MNL) model assumes that the disturbances \(\varepsilon_{ij}\) are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) following the Gumbel distribution (Type I Extreme Value), with cumulative distribution function:

\(F(\varepsilon) = \exp\left(-\exp(-\varepsilon)\right)\)

The decisive mathematical convenience of the Gumbel distribution is that the difference between two independent Gumbel random variables follows a logistic distribution. This property yields an elegant closed-form analytical expression for the choice probabilities, completely bypassing the multidimensional numerical integration required when disturbances are assumed normal (as in the Multinomial Probit model).

4. Choice Probabilities & Base-Category Normalization

Under i.i.d. Gumbel errors, integrating over the disturbances produces the general Multinomial Logit choice probability:

PROBABILITY General Multinomial Logit Choice Probability
\[ P(y_i = j \mid \mathbf{x}_i) = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}_j)}{\sum_{k=0}^J \exp(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}_k)} \]

Why Is Location Normalization Essential?

Examine the general probability expression above. What happens if we add any arbitrary vector \(\boldsymbol{\gamma}\) to every single parameter vector \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_j\)?

Let \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_j^* = \boldsymbol{\beta}_j + \boldsymbol{\gamma}\) for all \(j = 0, \dots, J\). The resulting choice probability is:

\[ P^*(y_i = j \mid \mathbf{x}_i) = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{x}_i'(\boldsymbol{\beta}_j + \boldsymbol{\gamma}))}{\sum_{k=0}^J \exp(\mathbf{x}_i'(\boldsymbol{\beta}_k + \boldsymbol{\gamma}))} = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}_j)\cdot\exp(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\gamma})}{\exp(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\gamma})\sum_{k=0}^J \exp(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}_k)} = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}_j)}{\sum_{k=0}^J \exp(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}_k)} = P(y_i = j \mid \mathbf{x}_i) \]

The common factor \(\exp(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\gamma})\) factors out of both numerator and denominator and cancels completely. This proves that the raw parameter vectors \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_0, \boldsymbol{\beta}_1, \dots, \boldsymbol{\beta}_J\) are not uniquely identified; infinitely many parameter sets produce identical choice probabilities!

To achieve identification, we must impose a location normalization. Without loss of generality, we designate alternative \(0\) as the base category (reference alternative) and normalize its coefficient vector to zero:

NORMALIZATION Normalized Multinomial Logit Probabilities
\[ \boldsymbol{\beta}_0 = \mathbf{0} \] \[ P(y_i = j \mid \mathbf{x}_i) = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}_j)}{1 + \sum_{k=1}^J \exp(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}_k)}, \quad \text{for } j = 1, \dots, J \] \[ P(y_i = 0 \mid \mathbf{x}_i) = \frac{1}{1 + \sum_{k=1}^J \exp(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}_k)}, \quad \text{for Base Category } j = 0 \]
PROPERTIES:
Bounded Support Since \(\exp(\cdot) > 0\), every choice probability strictly satisfies \(0 < P(y_i = j \mid \mathbf{x}_i) < 1\).
Unit Probability Sum The probabilities across all \(J + 1\) alternatives automatically sum to exactly \(1\): \(\sum_{j=0}^J P(y_i = j \mid \mathbf{x}_i) = 1\).

Probabilities are interdependent: changing an attribute that raises the attractiveness of one career necessarily depresses the choice probabilities of all other careers, preserving the unit sum \(\sum_j P_j = 1\).

📚 ECONOMETRIC INTERPRETATION

Because the choice probabilities reflect a competitive zero-sum allocation across options, an option's probability depends on its systematic utility relative to all alternatives in the choice set.

6. Quick Knowledge Check

Question: Why Can't We Estimate \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_0\) Freely?

A student attempts to estimate an unconstrained Multinomial Logit model with \(3\) alternatives (options \(0, 1, 2\)) by estimating three separate intercept parameters \(\beta_{00}, \beta_{10}, \beta_{20}\). The numerical optimization routine crashes with a singular Hessian matrix. Explain why this happens.

Hint: What happens to the choice probabilities if you add a constant \(c\) to all three intercepts? Can the data distinguish between \((0, 1, 2)\) and \((10, 11, 12)\)?

Solution:
The multinomial logit probability depends strictly on differences between systematic utilities: \(U_{ij} - U_{ik} = \mathbf{x}_i'(\boldsymbol{\beta}_j - \boldsymbol{\beta}_k) + (\varepsilon_{ij} - \varepsilon_{ik})\). Adding an arbitrary constant \(c\) to all intercepts shifts all utilities equally, leaving all utility differences and choice probabilities unchanged. The parameter space has a direction of infinite flatness (perfect collinearity in utility levels), creating a singular information matrix. Setting \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_0 = \mathbf{0}\) pins down the baseline location and restores full identification.

Lesson 18.1 Summary

In this lesson, we established the random-utility foundations for multiple unordered alternatives:

  • Unordered Choice: Models choices over \(J + 1\) discrete options based on observed individual characteristics \(\mathbf{x}_i\).
  • Random Utility: Latent utility \(U_{ij} = \mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}_j + \varepsilon_{ij}\) leads to choice when \(U_{ij} > U_{ik}\) for all \(k \ne j\).
  • Gumbel Disturbances: i.i.d. extreme value errors ensure that utility differences follow a logistic distribution, yielding closed-form choice probabilities.
  • Identification & Normalization: Setting \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_0 = \mathbf{0}\) resolves location indeterminacy, ensuring all probabilities strictly lie in \((0, 1)\) and sum to \(1\).
