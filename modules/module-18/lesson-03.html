LESSON 18.3 Partial Effects & Discrete Changes

Marginal Effects & Predicted Choice Probabilities

Derive continuous marginal effects as own-alternative utility changes minus probability-weighted portfolio averages, calculate discrete dummy variable switches as finite differences, and verify that probability adjustments sum to zero.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Derive and interpret the continuous marginal effect formula \(\frac{\partial P_{ij}}{\partial x_{ir}} = P_{ij} \left[ \beta_{jr} - \sum_{k=0}^J P_{ik}\beta_{kr} \right]\).
  • Understand why an option's probability can decline even when its own coefficient \(\beta_{jr} > 0\) (if its coefficient falls short of the weighted portfolio average).
  • Reproduce the exact lecture numerical calculation: \(\beta_1 = 1.2, \beta_2 = 0.6, P_0 = 0.2, P_1 = 0.5, P_2 = 0.3 \implies \frac{\partial P_1}{\partial x} = +0.21\).
  • Compute discrete dummy variable marginal effects as finite differences \(\Delta P_{ij} = P(y_i=j \mid x=1) - P(y_i=j \mid x=0)\) and verify the zero-sum adding-up condition.

1. Continuous Marginal Effect Formula

Because multinomial logit choice probabilities are non-linear functions of all parameter vectors, the raw coefficient \(\beta_{jr}\) is not the derivative of \(P_{ij}\) with respect to \(x_{ir}\). To compute the actual partial effect of a continuous regressor \(x_r\) on the choice probability of category \(j\), we differentiate \(P(y_i = j \mid \mathbf{x}_i)\) directly:

DERIVATIVE Continuous Marginal Effect Formula
\[ \frac{\partial P(y_i = j \mid \mathbf{x}_i)}{\partial x_{ir}} = P_{ij} \left[ \beta_{jr} - \sum_{k=0}^J P_{ik} \beta_{kr} \right] \]
WHERE:
\(P_{ij}\) Predicted choice probability for alternative \(j\): \(P(y_i = j \mid \mathbf{x}_i)\).
\(\beta_{jr}\) Alternative-\(j\) coefficient on regressor \(r\) (with \(\beta_{0r} = 0\) for the base category).
\(\sum_{k=0}^J P_{ik} \beta_{kr}\) Probability-weighted average effect of covariate \(r\) across all \(J + 1\) available alternatives.

PEDAGOGICAL INTUITION · OWN EFFECT MINUS THE PORTFOLIO BENCHMARK

As formulated in the lecture source: the marginal effect is the difference between how \(x_r\) affects option \(j\)'s utility and how it affects the average utility across all choices. An increase in \(x_r\) increases \(P_{ij}\) if and only if option \(j\)'s coefficient exceeds the market average: \[ \frac{\partial P_{ij}}{\partial x_{ir}} > 0 \iff \beta_{jr} > \sum_{k=0}^J P_{ik}\beta_{kr} \] Consequently, an option with a strictly positive coefficient (\(\beta_{jr} > 0\)) can still experience a negative marginal probability effect if other options gain utility at an even faster rate!

The Adding-Up Property

Because the probabilities must sum to 1 at every covariate value (\(\sum_{j=0}^J P_{ij} = 1\)), the sum of marginal effects across all \(J + 1\) alternatives must identically equal zero:

\[ \sum_{j=0}^J \frac{\partial P_{ij}}{\partial x_{ir}} = \sum_{j=0}^J P_{ij} \beta_{jr} - \left(\sum_{j=0}^J P_{ij}\right) \left(\sum_{k=0}^J P_{ik}\beta_{kr}\right) = \sum_{j=0}^J P_{ij} \beta_{jr} - \sum_{k=0}^J P_{ik}\beta_{kr} = 0 \]

Any probability gained by one alternative is necessarily subtracted from the remaining alternatives.

2. Lecture Numerical Example: Continuous Marginal Effect

To make this intuition completely concrete, we reproduce the exact numerical worked example from Page 4 of the lecture notes:

AUTHORITATIVE SOURCE EXAMPLE · THREE ALTERNATIVES (J = 2)

Consider a model with 3 choices: Option 0 (base), Option 1, and Option 2. Suppose the estimated coefficients and predicted choice probabilities for an individual are:

\(\beta_0 = 0\) (Base)  |  \(P_{i0} = 0.20\)
\(\beta_1 = 1.20\)  |  \(P_{i1} = 0.50\)
\(\beta_2 = 0.60\)  |  \(P_{i2} = 0.30\)
Step 1: Compute the probability-weighted average coefficient
\(\sum_{k=0}^2 P_{ik}\beta_k = (0.20 \cdot 0) + (0.50 \cdot 1.20) + (0.30 \cdot 0.60) = 0 + 0.60 + 0.18 = 0.78\)
Step 2: Evaluate the marginal effect for Option 1
\(\frac{\partial P_{i1}}{\partial x_i} = 0.50 \cdot [1.20 - 0.78] = 0.50 \cdot 0.42 = +0.21\)

Interpretation: A 1-unit increase in \(x_i\) increases the predicted probability of choosing Option 1 by 0.21, or 21 percentage points, at this covariate profile.

COMPLETE THE SYSTEM · WHAT HAPPENS TO OPTIONS 2 AND 0?

• Option 2: \(\frac{\partial P_{i2}}{\partial x_i} = 0.30 \cdot [0.60 - 0.78] = 0.30 \cdot (-0.18) = \mathbf{-0.054}\) (-5.4 percentage points). Notice that Option 2's probability drops even though \(\beta_2 = +0.60 > 0\)!
• Option 0 (Base): \(\frac{\partial P_{i0}}{\partial x_i} = 0.20 \cdot [0 - 0.78] = \mathbf{-0.156}\) (-15.6 percentage points).
• Sum Verification: \(+0.210 - 0.054 - 0.156 = \mathbf{0.000}\). The system balances exactly.

3. Discrete Marginal Effects of Dummy Variables

When a covariate is a binary indicator \(x_{ir} \in \{0, 1\}\) (such as a gender or treatment dummy), differential calculus does not apply because the variable cannot change by an infinitesimal amount. Its marginal effect is evaluated as a finite discrete difference:

FINITE DIFFERENCE Dummy Variable Marginal Effect
\[ \Delta P_{ij} = P(y_i = j \mid x_{ir} = 1, \mathbf{x}_{i,-r}) - P(y_i = j \mid x_{ir} = 0, \mathbf{x}_{i,-r}) \]

Lecture Travel Mode Choice Example (Pages 5–6)

Consider a travel mode model with three alternatives:

  • \(j = 0\): Walk (base category)
  • \(j = 1\): Bus
  • \(j = 2\): Car

Let the covariate of interest be the binary indicator \(x_{i1} = \text{Female}\). The model yields the following predicted choice probabilities:

Alternative Female (\(x = 1\)) Male (\(x = 0\)) Discrete Change \(\Delta P_j\)
Option 1: Bus 0.45 0.30 +0.15 (+15 points)
Option 2: Car 0.40 0.50 -0.10 (-10 points)
Option 0: Walk (Implied) \(1 - 0.45 - 0.40 = \mathbf{0.15}\) \(1 - 0.30 - 0.50 = \mathbf{0.20}\) \(0.15 - 0.20 = \mathbf{-0.05}\) (-5 points)
SOURCE READING NOTE: On Page 5, the lecture states \(\Delta P_{i0} = 1 - P_{i1} - P_{i2} = 1 - 0.45 - 0.40 = 0.15\) (which is the female walking level \(P_{i0}(1)\)). On Page 6, the summary text states that female "raises the likelihood of walking by 5 points". Calculating the complete probability vectors shows that male walking is \(1 - 0.30 - 0.50 = 0.20\), so the actual change is \(0.15 - 0.20 = -0.05\). For all three changes to sum to zero, the base category change must balance the remaining options: \((+0.15) + (-0.10) + \Delta P_0 = 0 \implies \Delta P_0 = -0.05\). Always verify that discrete changes across all mutually exclusive categories sum exactly to zero.

⚠ WHAT TO NOTICE

Even when both \(\beta_1 > 0\) and \(\beta_2 > 0\), Option 2's marginal effect can be negative! It is not enough for an alternative to have a positive coefficient; its coefficient must exceed the weighted portfolio average.

⚖ WHY IT MATTERS

In policy evaluation, looking only at the sign of \(\hat{\beta}_j\) will lead to false conclusions about whether a policy intervention increases or decreases participation in a program.

Marginal effects vary across individuals because they depend on the predicted probabilities \(P_{ik}\), which are evaluated at specific covariate values \(\mathbf{x}_i\).

5. Quick Knowledge Check

Question: Sign of Marginal Effect vs. Coefficient

Under what mathematical condition will a continuous covariate \(x_r\) have a negative marginal effect on the choice probability of alternative \(j\), even if its estimated coefficient \(\hat{\beta}_{jr}\) is strictly positive (\(\hat{\beta}_{jr} > 0\))?

Hint: Look at the bracketed term in \(\frac{\partial P_{ij}}{\partial x_{ir}} = P_{ij} \left[ \beta_{jr} - \sum_k P_{ik}\beta_{kr} \right]\). When is that bracket negative?

Solution:
Since \(P_{ij} > 0\), the marginal effect \(\frac{\partial P_{ij}}{\partial x_{ir}}\) is negative if and only if: \[ \beta_{jr} < \sum_{k=0}^J P_{ik}\beta_{kr} \] That is, whenever option \(j\)'s coefficient is smaller than the probability-weighted average effect of covariate \(r\) across all alternatives. Even though \(x_r\) increases the utility of option \(j\), it increases the utility of other competing alternatives by even more, pulling probability mass away from option \(j\).

Lesson 18.3 Summary

In this lesson, we established the calculus and arithmetic of partial probability effects:

  • Continuous Marginal Effect: Given by \(\frac{\partial P_{ij}}{\partial x_{ir}} = P_{ij} \left[ \beta_{jr} - \sum_k P_{ik}\beta_{kr} \right]\).
  • Weighted Benchmark: An option's choice probability increases only if its coefficient exceeds the probability-weighted average coefficient across all alternatives.
  • Dummy Variables: Must be evaluated as discrete finite differences \(\Delta P_{ij} = P(x=1) - P(x=0)\).
  • Adding-Up Condition: Both continuous marginal effects and discrete dummy changes sum to zero across all alternatives: \(\sum_j \Delta P_j = 0\).
