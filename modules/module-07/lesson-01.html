LESSON 7.1 Motivation & Placebos

Why Difference-in-Difference-in-Differences?

As established in Module 6, standard Difference-in-Differences compares the over-time change for a treated group against that of a control group. But what happens when another policy or shock hits the treatment environment at the exact same time?

LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • Understand why standard \(2 \times 2\) DD fails when concurrent local shocks contaminate the treatment environment.
  • Deconstruct the classic Medicaid expansion case study with simultaneous physical education requirements.
  • Master the three-layer comparison structure: State \(\times\) Time \(\times\) Subgroup.
  • Define Difference-in-Difference-in-Differences (DDD) conceptually as the difference between two DD estimates.
  • Recognize the vital empirical requirement and limitation of finding a substantively defensible placebo group.

1. Why Standard DD May Still Be Contaminated

Recall from Module 6 that Difference-in-Differences (DD) isolates a causal effect by netting out time-invariant group differences and common macroeconomic time trends:

STANDARD 2 × 2 DD ESTIMATOR Standard Two-Group, Two-Period Difference-in-Differences
\[\begin{aligned} \widehat{\delta}_{\text{DD}} &= \left(\bar{Y}_{\text{Treated, Post}} - \bar{Y}_{\text{Treated, Pre}}\right) - \left(\bar{Y}_{\text{Control, Post}} - \bar{Y}_{\text{Control, Pre}}\right) \\[6pt] &= \Delta \bar{Y}_{\text{Treated}} - \Delta \bar{Y}_{\text{Control}} \end{aligned}\]

Under the parallel trends assumption, the change in the control group serves as the counterfactual trajectory for the treated group absent the policy. However, this identification strategy breaks down if the treated jurisdiction experiences a concurrent local shock or another policy reform at the same time as the treatment of interest.

WARNING The Fundamental Threat: Simultaneous Local Policies

If any other event occurs in the treated environment during the post-treatment period that also influences the outcome, the standard DD estimator lumps that concurrent shock together with the true treatment effect. The counterfactual is no longer clean.

2. Motivating Case Study: Medicaid Expansion

SCENARIO

Evaluating Child Health Improvements

Suppose an economist wishes to evaluate the causal impact of a state-level Medicaid expansion on child health outcomes. Because Medicaid eligibility is means-tested, the policy primarily expands health insurance coverage for poor children.

  • Treatment of Interest: State Medicaid expansion policy.
  • Target Subgroup: Poor children eligible for the expanded coverage.
  • Treated State: State expanding Medicaid.
  • Control State: Neighboring state with no Medicaid expansion.
THE PROBLEM

A Simultaneous Environmental Reform

Suppose that concurrently with the Medicaid expansion, the treated state legislature also enacts mandatory school physical-education (PE) requirements to promote child wellness.

Consider what happens if we estimate a standard Difference-in-Differences comparing poor children in the expansion state against poor children in the control state:

OBSERVED POOR-CHILDREN DD Medicaid Expansion State · Poor Children
\[\begin{aligned} \text{DD}_{\text{poor}} &= \Delta \bar{Y}_{\text{Expansion, Poor}} - \Delta \bar{Y}_{\text{Control, Poor}} \\[6pt] &= \text{True Medicaid Effect} + \text{State PE Requirement Shock} \end{aligned}\]
WHAT DOES THIS ESTIMATE CONTAIN?
Target Policy of Interest:
True Medicaid Treatment Effect
+
Confounding Simultaneous Policy:
State-Specific PE Requirement Shock

Because the PE mandate also improves child health and occurred in the same state at the same time, \(\text{DD}_{\text{poor}}\) cannot disentangle whether health improved because of doctor visits funded by Medicaid or because children exercised more in gym class.

THE KEY IDEA

Introducing a Placebo Subgroup

To solve this identification challenge, notice that within the expansion state, rich children attend the same public schools and are equally exposed to the new physical-education mandate. However, rich children were already privately insured or ineligible for Medicaid, so they receive zero health benefits from the Medicaid expansion itself.

Rich children constitute an ideal placebo subgroup:

PLACEBO RICH-CHILDREN DD Medicaid Expansion State · Rich Children (Ineligible)
\[\begin{aligned} \text{DD}_{\text{rich}} &= \Delta \bar{Y}_{\text{Expansion, Rich}} - \Delta \bar{Y}_{\text{Control, Rich}} \\[6pt] &= 0 + \text{State PE Requirement Shock} \end{aligned}\]
WHAT DOES THIS ESTIMATE CONTAIN?
Medicaid Gain for Rich Children:
\(0\) (Ineligible for subsidized coverage)
+
Common State Shock:
State PE Requirement Shock (Equal Exposure)

By taking the difference between these two Difference-in-Differences estimates, the common state-level PE shock differences out completely:

CLEAN TRIPLE DIFFERENCE (DDD) Subgroup Differencing Cancels Common State Shock
\[\begin{aligned} \text{DDD} &= \text{DD}_{\text{poor}} - \text{DD}_{\text{rich}} \\[6pt] &= \Big(\text{Medicaid} + \text{PE Shock}\Big) - \Big(0 + \text{PE Shock}\Big) \\[6pt] &= \mathbf{\text{True Medicaid Treatment Effect}} \end{aligned}\]

KEY INTUITION

A Difference-in-Difference-in-Differences estimate is simply a difference between two Difference-in-Differences estimates: one computed on the targeted subgroup, and the other computed on an unaffected placebo subgroup.

4. Why DDD Helps and Where It Falls Short

Consider an environmental change such as state-mandated physical-education classes that affects both rich and poor children in the treatment state: the second DD subtraction removes that common state-specific change.

By benchmarking the treated subgroup against a group in the same geographical and political environment that is ineligible or unaffected by the policy, we gain a defense against arbitrary local confounders that violate standard parallel trends.

LIMITATION Finding Defensible Placebo Groups

It is not always possible to find a suitable placebo group. To serve as a valid third difference, the placebo subgroup must satisfy two demanding criteria:

  • Equal Exposure to Confounders: It must be genuinely exposed to the same environmental shocks, local policies, and macroeconomic forces as the treated subgroup.
  • Zero Treatment Contamination: It must strictly experience zero direct or indirect spillover effects from the treatment under study.

DDD does not automatically solve all identification problems. A credible third comparison dimension must exist and be substantively defensible within the economic context.

QUICK CHECK Why does DDD require a third comparison dimension?

Suppose you estimate the effect of a city-level tax incentive on small manufacturing businesses using a standard DD against a neighboring city. When would this standard DD fail, and why would examining large retail corporations in both cities rescue your identification?

Show Answer & Explanation
ANSWER: The standard DD fails if the treated city simultaneously experienced an infrastructure upgrade or local boom that boosted manufacturing independent of the tax incentive.

WHY?
  1. Examining large retail businesses (which do not qualify for the manufacturing tax break but operate in the same municipal economy) provides a second DD that measures and nets out the city-wide economic shock, leaving only the tax incentive effect (\(\text{DDD} = \text{DD}_{\text{manufacturers}} - \text{DD}_{\text{retail}}\)).
  2. Subtracting two differences isolates the specific treatment parameter while controlling for concurrent geographic confounders.
TAKEAWAYS

What You Should Now Understand

  • Standard \(2 \times 2\) DD can be contaminated by a simultaneous shock affecting the treated environment.
  • A placebo subgroup provides another comparison exposed to the same broader environment but not the treatment of interest.
  • DDD subtracts one DD estimate from another: \(\text{DDD} = \text{DD}_{\text{Target}} - \text{DD}_{\text{Placebo}}\).
  • The third comparison dimension helps remove common shocks affecting both targeted and placebo subgroups.
  • Identification still requires the third comparison group to be credible and free from policy spillovers.
