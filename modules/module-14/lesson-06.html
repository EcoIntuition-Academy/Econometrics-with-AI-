LESSON 14.6 Experimental IV

2SLS in Field Experiments, LATE vs ATE & Exclusion Restrictions

Connect randomized encouragement designs to Two-Stage Least Squares, contrast complier-specific local effects with full population averages, and critically scrutinize the exclusion restriction in practical field applications.

LEARNING OBJECTIVES
  • Formulate Two-Stage Least Squares (2SLS) using randomized assignment as an instrument for treatment receipt.
  • Apply proper IV standard error calculations and avoid manual second-stage OLS inference pitfalls.
  • Contrast the Local Average Treatment Effect (LATE) with the Average Treatment Effect (ATE) under effect heterogeneity.
  • Evaluate potential violations of the Exclusion Restriction using field trial design cautions and measurement diagnostics.

1. Two-Stage Least Squares with Randomized Assignment

When randomized assignment \(Z_i\) changes actual treatment receipt \(T_i\) imperfectly, ordinary regression of outcome \(Y_i\) on \(T_i\) suffers from self-selection bias. Because \(Z_i\) is truly randomized, it satisfies instrument exogeneity, allowing us to estimate the causal impact of \(T_i\) via Two-Stage Least Squares (2SLS).

EXPERIMENTAL 2SLS SPECIFICATION Two-Stage Model
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{First Stage:} \quad & T_i = \pi_0 + \pi_1 Z_i + \pi_2 x_i + \nu_i \\[0.5rem] \text{Second Stage:} \quad & Y_i = \alpha + \beta \hat{T}_i + \varepsilon_i \end{aligned} \]

In the first stage, treatment receipt \(T_i\) is predicted using randomized assignment \(Z_i\) and baseline controls \(x_i\). The fitted values \(\hat{T}_i\) purge endogenous selection from treatment participation. In the second stage, outcome \(Y_i\) is regressed on \(\hat{T}_i\), identifying the causal treatment effect \(\beta\).

INFERENCE RULE · DO NOT RUN MANUAL SECOND-STAGE OLS

Never manually estimate \(\hat{T}_i\) and then run standard OLS on \(Y_i = \alpha + \beta \hat{T}_i + \varepsilon_i\). Standard OLS computes residuals as \(Y_i - \hat{\alpha} - \hat{\beta} \hat{T}_i\), underestimating the true error variance and generating invalid standard errors and p-values. True 2SLS software computes residuals using actual treatment: \(Y_i - \hat{\alpha} - \hat{\beta} T_i\).

2. Local Average Treatment Effect (LATE) vs. Average Treatment Effect (ATE)

A common misconception is that 2SLS estimates the impact of treatment for everyone in the experimental population. When treatment impacts vary across individuals (heterogeneous treatment effects), IV identifies a local effect rather than a population average.

POPULATION AVERAGE
Average Treatment Effect (ATE)

The expected causal effect if every single individual in the target population were assigned and received treatment:

\(\text{ATE} = E[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)]\)
COMPLIER-SPECIFIC
Local Average Treatment Effect (LATE)

The average causal treatment effect strictly for Compliers whose participation is shifted by the offer \(Z\):

\(\text{LATE} = E[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0) \mid \text{Complier}]\)
PRACTICAL INSIGHT · COMPLIANCE & ETHICS

If compliance were 100%, assignment and receipt would coincide (\(Z_i = T_i\)), and LATE would equal ATE. However, in economic policy and health interventions, forcing compliance is frequently infeasible or ethically impermissible. Imperfect compliance is an inherent feature of human decision-making, which makes instrumental variables essential for policy evaluation.

3. The Exclusion Restriction in Field Experiments

Randomization guarantees that assignment \(Z_i\) is unconfounded, but it does not automatically guarantee the exclusion restriction. The exclusion restriction requires that the offer \(Z_i\) impacts outcome \(Y_i\) solely through treatment receipt \(T_i\).

Field Trial Example: Financial Incentive for Vaccination

Consider a trial where \(Z\) is a cash incentive offer, \(T\) is vaccination receipt, and \(Y\) is health:

Instrument \(Z\): Cash voucher offered to get vaccinated.
Treatment \(T\): Actual clinical receipt of the vaccine.
Outcome \(Y\): Infection rate / health metrics.
Potential Exclusion Failure: If the cash incentive alters perceptions of vaccine safety ("why else would they pay me?"), or if the cash alters nutrition or hygiene directly, then \(Z\) affects \(Y\) outside of vaccination \(T\), biasing 2SLS.
VALID IDENTIFICATION PATH
\(Z \longrightarrow T \longrightarrow Y\)

Random offer operates entirely through shifting treatment take-up.

INVALID DIRECT CHANNEL
\(Z \xrightarrow{\quad \text{Direct} \quad} Y\)

Offer alters beliefs, stigma, or resources, violating exclusion.

6. Quick Check

QUICK CHECK

Under what condition does the 2SLS / Wald estimator identify the Average Treatment Effect (ATE) rather than just the Local Average Treatment Effect (LATE)?

HINT: Consider whether treatment effects differ across compliance types, or what happens when compliance is 100%.

ANSWER: Homogeneity of treatment effects or full compliance.

WHY? 2SLS identifies ATE if either: (1) treatment effects are identical across compliers, always-takers, and never-takers (homogeneous treatment effects), or (2) there is 100% compliance so that all individuals are compliers and \(Z_i = T_i\) for the entire sample.

TAKEAWAYS

What You Should Now Understand

  • Randomized assignment \(Z_i\) serves as an instrumental variable for endogenous treatment receipt \(T_i\) via Two-Stage Least Squares.
  • Inference must use proper 2SLS standard errors rather than manual second-stage OLS regression on \(\hat{T}_i\).
  • Under heterogeneous treatment effects, 2SLS estimates LATE (effect for compliers) rather than the population ATE.
  • The exclusion restriction requires that the randomized offer \(Z_i\) affects outcome \(Y_i\) solely through treatment receipt \(T_i\).
  • Field trial design practices—such as minimizing noncompliance and surveying perceptions—protect and probe exclusion validity.
