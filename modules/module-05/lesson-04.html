LESSON 5.4 Joint Hypothesis Testing

Wald & F Tests for Joint Restrictions

The vector \(R\hat{\beta} - q\) measures how far the estimated coefficients are from satisfying the restrictions. The Wald and F statistics determine whether that discrepancy is large relative to its sampling uncertainty.

LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • Derive the covariance matrix of \(R\hat{\beta} - q\).
  • Explain why the Wald statistic scales restriction discrepancies by their covariance.
  • Connect the Wald statistic to the F statistic for \(J\) restrictions.
  • Interpret the restricted-versus-unrestricted \(R^2\) formulation.

1. Intuition First: Distance Scaled by Uncertainty

In sample estimation, the raw discrepancy vector: \[ d = R\hat{\beta} - q \] measures how far our empirical estimates deviate from the restriction \(H_0: R\beta = q\).

However, raw discrepancy alone is meaningless without knowing how precisely the linear combination is estimated:

  • A discrepancy of \(0.5\) is enormous if the standard error of estimation is \(0.05\) (10 standard deviations away!).
  • That same discrepancy of \(0.5\) is negligible if the standard error is \(5.0\) (just 0.1 standard deviations away!).

Therefore, in econometrics:

STATISTICAL DISTANCE = \(\frac{\text{EMPIRICAL DISCREPANCY}}{\text{SAMPLING UNCERTAINTY}}\)

When testing \(J > 1\) restrictions simultaneously, sampling uncertainty is captured by a \(J \times J\) covariance matrix.

2. Sampling Covariance of the Restriction Discrepancy

Under the null hypothesis \(H_0: R\beta = q\), the expected value of the sample discrepancy vector is zero: \[ \mathbb{E}[R\hat{\beta} - q \mid X] = R\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta} \mid X] - q = R\beta - q = \mathbf{0} \] To measure sampling uncertainty around zero, we derive the variance-covariance matrix of \(R\hat{\beta} - q\). Because \(q\) is a non-stochastic constant vector, \(\operatorname{Var}(R\hat{\beta} - q \mid X) = \operatorname{Var}(R\hat{\beta} \mid X)\).

DERIVATION RESULT Covariance Matrix of Linear Restrictions
\[ \operatorname{Var}(R\hat{\beta} - q \mid X) = R \operatorname{Var}(\hat{\beta} \mid X) R' = \sigma^2 R(X'X)^{-1} R' \]
WHERE:
\(R\) \(J \times K\) restriction matrix of rank \(J \le K\)
\(\sigma^2\) Population disturbance variance under spherical errors
\((X'X)^{-1}\) \(K \times K\) inverse data matrix from OLS estimation
\(V_d = \sigma^2 R(X'X)^{-1} R'\) \(J \times J\) symmetric, positive-definite covariance matrix of the restrictions

3. The Wald Statistic: Quadratic Form Standardization

For a single restriction (\(J = 1\)), we divide by the standard error and square: \(z^2 = \left(\frac{R\hat{\beta} - q}{\operatorname{SE}}\right)^2\). For \(J > 1\) simultaneous restrictions, vector division is impossible. Instead, we use the matrix inverse to form a quadratic form:

CONCEPTUAL STEPS From Discrepancy to Test Statistic
Step 1 (Discrepancy Vector): \(d = R\hat{\beta} - q \quad (J \times 1)\)
Step 2 (Sampling Covariance): \(V_d = \sigma^2 R(X'X)^{-1} R' \quad (J \times J)\)
Step 3 (Scale by Inverse Covariance): \(W = d' V_d^{-1} d\)
TEST STATISTIC The Wald Statistic
\[ W = (R\hat{\beta} - q)' \left[ \sigma^2 R(X'X)^{-1} R' \right]^{-1} (R\hat{\beta} - q) \]
UNDER \(H_0\):
\(W \sim \chi^2_J\) Follows a Chi-Square distribution with \(J\) degrees of freedom
\(J\) Number of restrictions being tested (equals rows of \(R\))

The quadratic form weights departures by precision: deviations along dimensions with large estimation variance are discounted, while deviations along precisely estimated dimensions are penalized heavily.

4. The Feasible Finite-Sample \(F\)-Statistic

In practice, the true population error variance \(\sigma^2\) is unknown. We replace it with the unbiased residual variance estimator: \[ s^2 = \frac{e'e}{n - k} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n \hat{\varepsilon}_i^2}{n - k} \] where \(n\) is the sample size and \(k\) is the number of estimated parameters. Dividing the estimated Wald quadratic form by the number of restrictions \(J\) yields the classical \(F\)-statistic:

EXACT TEST STATISTIC Finite-Sample \(F\)-Statistic
\[ F = \frac{W(s^2)}{J} = \frac{1}{J} (R\hat{\beta} - q)' \left[ s^2 R(X'X)^{-1} R' \right]^{-1} (R\hat{\beta} - q) \]
DISTRIBUTION:
\(F \sim F(J, n - k)\) Exact \(F\)-distribution under normality of disturbances and true \(H_0\)
\(J\) Numerator degrees of freedom (number of restrictions)
\(n - k\) Denominator degrees of freedom (residual degrees of freedom)
Large-Sample Asymptotics

Validity Without Normality

Even if disturbances are not normally distributed, under standard regularity conditions the quantity \(J \cdot F \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2_J\) as \(n \to \infty\). Thus, \(F\)-tests remain asymptotically valid in large empirical samples.

5. The \(R^2\) Loss-of-Fit Formulation

A deeply intuitive way to understand the \(F\)-test is by comparing how well two competing regressions fit the same data:

UNRESTRICTED MODEL: Explanatory fit = \(R^2_U\)
↓   impose \(H_0\) (\(J\) restrictions)
RESTRICTED MODEL: Explanatory fit = \(R^2_R\)
↓   loss of fit
FIT PENALTY: \(\Delta R^2 = R^2_U - R^2_R \ge 0\)
INTUITIVE FORMULA Goodness-of-Fit Representation of \(F\)
\[ F = \frac{(R^2_U - R^2_R) / J}{(1 - R^2_U) / (n - k)} \]
INTERPRETATION:
\(\frac{R^2_U - R^2_R}{J}\) Loss in explanatory power per restriction imposed
\(\frac{1 - R^2_U}{n - k}\) Unexplained variance per residual degree of freedom in the unrestricted model

The shaded red gap \(\Delta R^2 = R^2_U - R^2_R\) represents the penalty in fit caused by forcing the coefficients to satisfy the restrictions under \(H_0\).

WHY IT MATTERS

If the restrictions are true in the population, imposing them should barely reduce \(R^2\). If the restrictions are false, imposing them substantially damages explanatory power.

ECONOMETRIC INTERPRETATION

When the loss in fit per restriction is large compared to the baseline unexplained variance \(\frac{1 - R^2_U}{n - k}\), the \(F\)-statistic exceeds critical value 3.09, rejecting \(H_0\).

QUICK CHECK Joint F-Test Calculation

An econometrician estimates an unrestricted model with \(n = 105\) and \(k = 5\) parameters, obtaining \(R^2_U = 0.60\). She tests the joint hypothesis that three slope coefficients are zero (\(J = 3\)). The restricted model yields \(R^2_R = 0.54\). What is the value of the \(F\)-statistic?

Show Answer & Explanation
ANSWER: \(F = 5.00\). Reject \(H_0\) at the 5% significance level.

CALCULATION:
  1. Numerator loss of fit per restriction: \(\frac{R^2_U - R^2_R}{J} = \frac{0.60 - 0.54}{3} = \frac{0.06}{3} = 0.02\).
  2. Denominator residual variance per df: \(\frac{1 - R^2_U}{n - k} = \frac{1 - 0.60}{105 - 5} = \frac{0.40}{100} = 0.004\).
  3. The ratio is: \(F = \frac{0.02}{0.004} = 5.00\).
  4. Since \(5.00 > F_{0.05}(3, 100) \approx 2.70\), we reject the joint null hypothesis \(H_0\) at the 5% level.
TAKEAWAYS

What You Should Now Understand

  • The restriction discrepancy \(R\hat{\beta} - q\) must be scaled by its covariance matrix \(\operatorname{Var}(R\hat{\beta} - q \mid X) = \sigma^2 R(X'X)^{-1} R'\).
  • The Wald statistic \(W = (R\hat{\beta} - q)' \left[\sigma^2 R(X'X)^{-1} R'\right]^{-1} (R\hat{\beta} - q)\) follows a \(\chi^2_J\) distribution under \(H_0\).
  • When \(\sigma^2\) is replaced by sample residual variance \(s^2\), dividing by \(J\) yields the exact finite-sample \(F(J, n - k)\) statistic.
  • The \(R^2\) formula \(F = \frac{(R^2_U - R^2_R)/J}{(1 - R^2_U)/(n - k)}\) provides an intuitive test of whether imposing restrictions causes an intolerable loss of fit.
