LESSON 12.5 Attenuation & IV

Attenuation Bias & IV as a Measurement-Error Solution

When an explanatory regressor is measured with classical error, the estimated OLS slope is systematically compressed toward zero. Instrumental variables can isolate uncontaminated variation to restore consistency.

🎯

Learning Objectives

  • Derive the asymptotic limit of the OLS estimator under classical measurement error in \(x\).
  • Define the attenuation factor \(\lambda \in (0, 1)\) as the signal-to-total-variance ratio.
  • Interpret the directional meaning of attenuation bias toward zero for positive and negative slopes.
  • Analyze the benchmark numerical illustration (\(\beta = 2, \lambda = 0.7 \implies \mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}}] = 1.4\)).
  • Derive the IV estimator and evaluate the identification conditions under which IV restores consistency.
Section 1

1. Derivation of Attenuation Bias

ALGEBRAIC DERIVATION

Measurement Error in the Independent Variable

GOAL: Show why measurement error in \(x\) shrinks the OLS slope toward zero.
1

Express OLS Estimator via Sample Moments

In the simple bivariate regression of \(y\) on observed \(x\), the OLS slope estimator satisfies:

\[\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} = \frac{\text{Cov}(x, y)}{\text{Var}(x)}\]
2

Substitute True Model into Covariance

Substitute the structural model \(y = \alpha + x^*\beta + \varepsilon\) and observed regressor \(x = x^* + u\):

\[\text{Cov}(x, y) = \text{Cov}(x^* + u, \alpha + x^*\beta + \varepsilon) = \beta \text{Var}(x^*) + \text{Cov}(x^*, \varepsilon) + \text{Cov}(u, x^*)\beta + \text{Cov}(u, \varepsilon)\]

Under the classical assumptions (\(\text{Cov}(u, x^*) = 0\), \(\text{Cov}(u, \varepsilon) = 0\), and \(\text{Cov}(x^*, \varepsilon) = 0\)), the numerator simplifies to:

\[\text{Cov}(x, y) = \beta \text{Var}(x^*)\]
3

Compute Denominator Total Variance

The observed regressor variance combines true signal variance with measurement noise:

\[\text{Var}(x) = \text{Var}(x^* + u) = \text{Var}(x^*) + \text{Var}(u) + 2\underbrace{\text{Cov}(x^*, u)}_{= 0} = \text{Var}(x^*) + \text{Var}(u)\]
4

Combine into the Attenuated Slope

Dividing numerator by denominator establishes the asymptotic expectation:

\[\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} \xrightarrow{p} \beta \cdot \left[ \frac{\text{Var}(x^*)}{\text{Var}(x^*) + \text{Var}(u)} \right]\]
5

Define the Attenuation Factor \(\lambda\)

Define \(\lambda\) as the ratio of signal variance to total variance:

\[\lambda \equiv \frac{\text{Var}(x^*)}{\text{Var}(x^*) + \text{Var}(u)} \implies \mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}}] = \lambda \beta\]
KEY RESULT
Because \(\text{Var}(u) > 0\), the attenuation factor is strictly bounded between zero and one: \(0 < \lambda < 1\). The OLS slope is systematically attenuated toward zero.
BIG PICTURE Denominator Noise Dilution

OLS projects \(y\) onto the total variance \(\text{Var}(x^*) + \text{Var}(u)\). Because the added noise in the denominator contributes zero covariance to the numerator, the observed relationship is diluted toward zero.

Section 2

2. Directional Meaning & Worked Illustration

POSITIVE SLOPE (\(\beta > 0\))

Compressed Downward

When \(\beta > 0\), \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}}\) remains positive but smaller than \(\beta\): \(0 < \lambda\beta < \beta\).

NEGATIVE SLOPE (\(\beta < 0\))

Compressed Upward

When \(\beta < 0\), \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}}\) remains negative but closer to zero: \(\beta < \lambda\beta < 0\) (less negative).

WORKED NUMERICAL EXAMPLE

Benchmark Illustration: Attenuation Factor \(\lambda = 0.7\)

Consider a maintained relationship with true structural coefficient \(\beta = 2\):

\[\beta = 2, \quad \lambda = 0.70 \implies \mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}}] = 0.70 \times 2 = 1.4\]

Even though the true underlying coefficient is \(2.0\), measurement noise accounting for \(30\%\) of total regressor variance causes OLS to report an expected slope of only \(1.4\)—an underestimation of \(30\%\).

Section 4

4. Instrumental Variables as a Measurement-Error Solution

IV DERIVATION

IV Consistency Under Classical Measurement Error

GOAL: Show how an instrumental variable isolates clean signal to restore consistency under the maintained assumptions.
1

Instrument Identification Conditions

An instrumental variable \(z\) requires three conditions under measurement error:

\[\text{Cov}(z, x^*) \neq 0, \quad \text{Cov}(z, u) = 0, \quad \text{Cov}(z, \varepsilon) = 0\]

The instrument must be correlated with true \(x^*\), but orthogonal to both the measurement error \(u\) and structural disturbance \(\varepsilon\).

2

Express the IV Estimator

In the bivariate setup, the IV estimator is given by the ratio of sample covariances with \(z\):

\[\hat{\beta}_{\text{IV}} = \frac{\text{Cov}(z, y)}{\text{Cov}(z, x)}\]
3

Substitute Structural Equations

Substitute \(y = \alpha + x^*\beta + \varepsilon\) and \(x = x^* + u\):

\[\hat{\beta}_{\text{IV}} = \frac{\text{Cov}(z, \alpha + x^*\beta + \varepsilon)}{\text{Cov}(z, x^* + u)} = \frac{\beta \text{Cov}(z, x^*) + \text{Cov}(z, \varepsilon)}{\text{Cov}(z, x^*) + \text{Cov}(z, u)}\]
4

Apply the Orthogonality Restrictions

Because \(\text{Cov}(z, \varepsilon) = 0\) and \(\text{Cov}(z, u) = 0\), the noise components vanish:

\[\hat{\beta}_{\text{IV}} \xrightarrow{p} \frac{\beta \text{Cov}(z, x^*) + 0}{\text{Cov}(z, x^*) + 0} = \beta\]
KEY RESULT
Under the maintained conditions, the IV estimator is consistent: \(\text{plim } \hat{\beta}_{\text{IV}} = \beta\).
CAUTION: DO NOT OVERSTATE IV
A valid and relevant IV can restore consistency under the maintained measurement-error assumptions. However, finding an instrument that is correlated with \(x^*\) while completely independent of both \(u\) and \(\varepsilon\) is an empirical challenge.
Section 5

5. Classical Endogeneity vs. Measurement Error

OMITTED VARIABLES / SIMULTANEITY

Structural Endogeneity

  • Observation: \(x = x^*\) (variable is measured accurately).
  • Source of Failure: Correlation between regressor and disturbance: \(\text{Cov}(x^*, \varepsilon) \neq 0\).
  • Bias Direction: Can be positive or negative depending on the sign of omitted covariance.
  • Role of IV: Isolates exogenous variation in \(x\) that is orthogonal to structural shock \(\varepsilon\).
NOISY REGRESSOR CONTAMINATION

Measurement-Error Endogeneity

  • Observation: \(x = x^* + u\) (variable is observed with noise).
  • Source of Failure: Shared noise component: \(\text{Cov}(x, \varepsilon - \beta u) = -\beta\sigma_u^2 \neq 0\).
  • Bias Direction: Always toward zero (attenuation bias).
  • Role of IV: Bypasses the measurement noise \(u\) using an instrument orthogonal to \(u\) and \(\varepsilon\).

Quick Check 12.5

QUICK CHECK

Why must an instrument \(z\) be uncorrelated with the measurement error \(u\) (\(\text{Cov}(z, u) = 0\)) to solve the measurement-error problem?

HINT: Consider what happens in the denominator \(\text{Cov}(z, x^* + u)\) if the instrument shares the same noise as the regressor.

ANSWER: Otherwise, the instrument would inherit the measurement contamination.

WHY? If \(\text{Cov}(z, u) \neq 0\), then in the IV denominator \(\text{Cov}(z, x) = \text{Cov}(z, x^*) + \text{Cov}(z, u)\), the noise term does not cancel out. The instrument would capture reporting noise rather than isolating clean variation in \(x^*\), leaving the IV estimator inconsistent.

TAKEAWAYS

What You Should Now Understand

  • Attenuation Derivation: Classical measurement error in \(x\) causes OLS to converge to \(\lambda \beta\), where \(\lambda = \text{Var}(x^*)/[\text{Var}(x^*) + \text{Var}(u)]\).
  • Bounded Factor: Because \(0 < \lambda < 1\), the OLS estimate is always attenuated toward zero—positive coefficients become smaller, and negative coefficients become less negative.
  • Benchmark Example: When \(\beta = 2\) and \(\lambda = 0.7\), OLS yields \(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}}] = 1.4\), attenuating the true slope by \(30\%\).
  • Instrument Conditions: A valid IV for measurement error requires \(\text{Cov}(z, x^*) \neq 0\), \(\text{Cov}(z, u) = 0\), and \(\text{Cov}(z, \varepsilon) = 0\).
  • Consistency Restoration: A valid and relevant IV can restore consistency under the maintained measurement-error assumptions by filtering out measurement noise.
