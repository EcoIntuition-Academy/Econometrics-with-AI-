Seeing OLS as “closest possible shadow”

Imagine each observation as one axis in a big room: if there are $n$observations, the room has $n$dimensions. The outcome vector $y$is then a point in this room: one coordinate per observation. The regressors in $X$define a lower-dimensional flat surface (a subspace) inside this large room, spanned by the columns of $X$.

What OLS does is: it finds the point on this flat surface that lies as close as possible to the point $y$. In other words, you shine a light orthogonally down onto the subspace spanned by $X$and look at where the “shadow” of $y$lands. That shadow is the fitted vector $\hat{y}=X\hat{β}$. The residual vector $\hat{e}=y-\hat{y}$is the difference between $y$and its shadow; it points straight out of the surface, at a right angle.

This geometric picture already encodes the key idea: OLS is an orthogonal projection of $y$onto the column space of $X$. Once that is clear, all the matrix algebra is just a way of writing this down symbolically.

Deriving the normal equations in slow motion

Start from the classical linear regression model in matrix form:

$y=Xβ+ε,$

where $y$is an $n×1$vector of outcomes, $X$is an $n×k$matrix of regressors, $β$is a $k×1$vector of unknown coefficients, and $ε$is an $n×1$vector of errors.

The OLS idea is simple in words: choose $\hat{β}$so that the sum of squared residuals, the total squared distance between what is observed and what is predicted, is as small as possible. In symbols, the residual for a candidate $b$is

$e(b)=y-Xb,$

and the objective function is

$S(b)=e(b)^{'}e(b)=(y-Xb)^{'}(y-Xb).$

To see what this looks like algebraically, carefully expand:

$S(b)=y^{'}y-b^{'}X^{'}y-y^{'}Xb+b^{'}X^{'}Xb.$

The two middle terms are actually the same scalar, just written in different ways. Using $(B^{'}A^{'}$and $a^{'}b=b^{'}a$for scalars, we can rewrite:

$b^{'}X^{'}y=(Xb)^{'}y=y^{'}Xb.$

So those two middle terms combine into a single term with coefficient $-2$:

$S(b)=y^{'}y-2b^{'}X^{'}y+b^{'}X^{'}Xb.$

Now treat $S(b)$as a function of the vector $b$. Taking the derivative with respect to $b$gives

$\frac{∂S(b)}{∂b}=-2X^{'}y+2X^{'}Xb.$

Setting this gradient equal to zero (the first-order condition for a minimum) yields

$-2X^{'}y+2X^{'}X\hat{β}=0⇒X^{'}X\hat{β}=X^{'}y.$

These are the normal equations. They express the idea that, at the optimum, shifting $b$a tiny bit in any direction does not reduce the sum of squared residuals.

Assuming $X$has full column rank, the matrix $X^{'}X$is invertible. Then there is a unique solution:

$\hat{β}=(X^{'}X)^{-1}X^{'}y.$

This is the familiar closed-form expression for OLS: it takes the data summarized in $X^{'}X$and $X^{'}y$and turns them into coefficient estimates.

To be sure this indeed corresponds to a minimum, inspect the Hessian:

$\frac{∂^{2}S(b)}{∂b∂b^{'}}=2X^{'}X.$

Under the full-rank condition and $n>k$, the matrix $X^{'}X$is positive definite, so the Hessian is positive definite and the critical point is a global minimum.

Projection and residual-maker matrices as “operators”

The geometry suggests two very special linear operators: one that keeps only the part of a vector that lives in the space spanned by $X$, and another that keeps only the part that is orthogonal to that space. These are the projection matrix and the residual-maker matrix.

The projection matrix onto the column space of $X$is

$P=X(X^{'}X)^{-1}X^{'}.$

For any vector $y$, the vector $Py$lies in the column space of $X$. If you apply $P$to $y$in the regression context, you get

$\hat{y}=Py=X(X^{'}X)^{-1}X^{'}y=X\hat{β},$

the fitted values from the regression.

The residual-maker matrix is defined as

$M=I-P.$

It does the opposite job: it strips away any component that lies in the span of $X$and leaves only what is orthogonal to that span. Applying $M$to $y$yields

$\hat{e}=My=y-\hat{y}.$

So, in a perfectly compact form, the regression decomposition is

$y=Py_{⏟}_{\hat{y}}+My_{⏟}_{\hat{e}}.$

One elegant property here is that $P$and $M$are idempotent: $P^{2}=P$, $M^{2}=M$. Projecting twice does nothing new. Also, they are symmetric: $P^{'}=P$, $M^{'}=M$. These facts make them genuine orthogonal projection operators in the Euclidean geometry of $R^{n}$.

What the residual conditions really say

The normal equations $X^{'}(X\hat{β}-y)=0$can be rewritten as

$X^{'}\hat{e}=0.$

This is a very compact way of saying: the residual vector $\hat{e}$is orthogonal to each column of $X$. Geometrically, this is exactly the property that characterizes orthogonal projection: the error between the original vector and its projection is perpendicular to the subspace.

Sum of residuals as a special case

Suppose the first column of $X$is a vector of ones $i$. Then the corresponding condition is

$i^{'}\hat{e}=0.$

What does this mean? It says

$\sum_{i=1}^{n} \hat{e}_{i}=0.$

So if the regression includes a constant term, the residuals necessarily add up to zero. The residuals may swing positive for some observations and negative for others, but on average they cancel exactly.

This can be visualized as follows: if you placed each residual as a vertical segment above or below the fitted line in a standard scatter plot, the total signed “area” above the line equals the total signed “area” below the line when a constant is present.

Passing through the means

Another famous property is that the regression hyperplane passes through the point of sample means. Start from

$\sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_{i}-x_{i}^{'}\hat{β})=0,$

which follows from $\sum_{i}^{} \hat{e}_{i}=0$. Rearrange:

$\sum_{i=1}^{n} y_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{i}^{'}\hat{β}.$

Divide both sides by $n$:

$yˉ=xˉ^{'}\hat{β}.$

So if you take the average of each regressor (forming $xˉ$) and plug that into the regression equation, the predicted value equals $yˉ$. In a simple regression, this means the fitted line crosses the point $\left(yˉ\right)$.

Mean of fitted equals mean of actual

The basic regression equation is

$y=X\hat{β}+\hat{e}.$

Take the average of each component:

$yˉ=X\hat{β}‾+eˉ.$

Knowing that $eˉ=0$, this simplifies to

$yˉ=\hat{y}‾.$

So the average predicted value equals the average actual value. This is another reflection of the same balancing property of residuals when a constant is included.

Frisch–Waugh–Lovell (FWL): “remove the old story, study what’s left”

Suppose the regressors are split into two groups:

$X=[X_{1}  X_{2}],$

and the model is

$y=X_{1}β_{1}+X_{2}β_{2}+ε.$

The FWL theorem gives a beautiful way to understand what the coefficients on $X_{2}$are really doing. Conceptually, there are two stories about $y$: a story told by $X_{1}$and a story told by $X_{2}$. The theorem says: if you want to learn the pure contribution of $X_{2}$, you should first remove the part of the story already explained by $X_{1}$, from both $y$and $X_{2}$, and then see how much of what remains in $y$can be explained by what remains in $X_{2}$.

Concretely, define the residual-maker with respect to $X_{1}$as

$M_{1}=I-X_{1}(X_{1}^{'}X_{1})^{-1}X_{1}^{'}.$

If you apply this to $y$, you get

$y^{*}=M_{1}y,$

the component of $y$orthogonal to $X_{1}$. If you apply it to $X_{2}$, you get

$X_{2}^{*}=M_{1}X_{2},$

the component of $X_{2}$that is orthogonal to $X_{1}$.

The theorem states that the coefficient vector $\hat{β}_{2}$from the full regression of $y$on $\left(X_{2}\right)$is exactly the same as the coefficient vector obtained by regressing $y^{*}$on $X_{2}^{*}$:

$\hat{β}_{2}=(X_{2}^{*^{'}}X_{2}^{*})^{-1}X_{2}^{*^{'}}y^{*}.$

This is a deep geometric statement: the effect of $X_{2}$is identified by looking at the angles and lengths inside the part of space that is orthogonal to $X_{1}$.

This also matches the economics intuition: the coefficient on a variable like “treatment” or “income” is the relationship between what cannot be explained by other controls in that variable and what cannot be explained by those same controls in the outcome.

Demeaning and the role of the constant term

Now specialize $X_{1}$to be just a constant term. Let

$X=[i  X_{2}],$

where $i$is the $n×1$vector of ones, and $X_{2}$contains the remaining regressors. The residual-maker for $i$is

$M_{0}=I-i(i^{'}i)^{-1}i^{'}.$

Notice that $i^{'}i=n$, and for any variable $z$,

$M_{0}z=z-\frac{1}{n}ii^{'}z.$

But $ii^{'}z$is a vector whose entries are all equal to $\sum_{i}^{} z_{i}$, so $\frac{1}{n}ii^{'}z$is a vector whose entries are all equal to $zˉ$. Thus,

$M_{0}z=z-zˉ,$

which means $M_{0}$simply subtracts the sample mean from each coordinate.

Apply this to $X_{2}$and $y$:

$X_{2}^{*}=M_{0}X_{2}=X_{2}-Xˉ_{2},y^{*}=M_{0}y=y-yˉ.$

The FWL formula says the coefficients on $X_{2}$in a regression with a constant are

$\hat{β}_{2}=(X_{2}^{*^{'}}X_{2}^{*})^{-1}X_{2}^{*^{'}}y^{*}.$

So, rather than first including a constant and then doing OLS, one can equivalently subtract the means from $y$and all the non-constant regressors, drop the constant, and run OLS on these mean-adjusted variables. The same slopes appear. The constant itself is then just $yˉ-Xˉ_{2}^{'}\hat{β}_{2}$.

This leads to the familiar formula for the slope in simple regression. With regressor vector $x$and outcome $y$, define

$x^{*}=x-xˉ,y^{*}=y-yˉ.$

Then the OLS slope is

$\hat{β}=\frac{\sum_{i}^{} (x_{i}-xˉ)(y_{i}-yˉ)}{\sum_{i}^{} (x_{i}-xˉ)^{2}}=\frac{sample covariance[x,y]}{sample variance[x]}.$

This expresses the slope as “how much $x$and $y$move together” divided by “how much $x$moves on its own.”

In multiple regression, the same spirit holds but with an extra layer: the coefficient on $x_{k}$is the covariance between the part of $x_{k}$remaining after regressing it on other regressors and the part of $y$remaining after regressing it on those regressors, divided by the variance of that residual part of $x_{k}$.

Orthogonal regressors and the power of randomization

Consider again a partition $X=[X_{1}  X_{2}]$. Suppose, in addition, that the two sets of regressors are orthogonal:

$X_{1}^{'}X_{2}=0.$

This is a very strong condition: it means the column space of $X_{1}$and the column space of $X_{2}$are perpendicular in the $n$-dimensional space.

Look at the expression

$X_{2}^{'}M_{1}=X_{2}^{'}(I-X_{1}(X_{1}^{'}X_{1})^{-1}X_{1}^{'}).$

If $X_{1}^{'}X_{2}=0$, then the second term vanishes:

$X_{2}^{'}X_{1}(X_{1}^{'}X_{1})^{-1}X_{1}^{'}=0,$

and therefore

$X_{2}^{'}M_{1}=X_{2}^{'}.$

Plug this back into the FWL expression for $\hat{β}_{2}$:

$\hat{β}_{2}=(X_{2}^{'}M_{1}X_{2})^{-1}X_{2}^{'}M_{1}y=(X_{2}^{'}X_{2})^{-1}X_{2}^{'}y.$

In words: if $X_{1}$and $X_{2}$are orthogonal, then the coefficient on $X_{2}$in the full regression of $y$on both $X_{1}$and $X_{2}$is exactly the same as the coefficient you would get from regressing $y$on $X_{2}$alone.

This is one of the most important intuitions behind randomized experiments. If $X_{2}$is a treatment indicator, and it is randomly assigned, then (in large samples) it is approximately orthogonal to any vector of pre-treatment covariates collected in $X_{1}$. That means one can estimate the treatment effect simply by regressing the outcome on the treatment indicator; including or excluding the additional controls does not change the expected value of the treatment coefficient, only its precision.
